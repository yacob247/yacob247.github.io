Volume Two · c. 1100–700 BC

The Dark Ages & the Age of Colonization

The Return of Literacy · Homer · Hesiod · The Rise of the Polis · Greek Colonization

Chronology note. The "Dark Ages" (c. 1100–800 BC) are so called from the poverty of the archaeological record and the loss of writing, not because we know nothing: archaeology, linguistics and the epic tradition together give a real, if shadowy, picture.

1. The End of the Palaces and the "Collapse"

The centuries after the destruction of the Mycenaean palaces (c. 1200–1100 BC) are traditionally called the Greek Dark Ages. The great fortified citadels of Mycenae, Pylos, Tiryns and Thebes were sacked and abandoned; the palace-based economy with its scribes, workshops and redistributive stores vanished; and, crucially, writing disappeared from Greece. With the administrative need for tablets gone, Linear B was simply no longer used, and for roughly four centuries Greek society was illiterate — a fact that explains why so much of Bronze Age record is lost but also why memory was kept alive by oral poetry (World History Encyclopedia, "Mycenaean Civilization," worldhistory.org/Mycenaean_Civilization/).

Population declined sharply. The large settlements shrank into small villages; houses became meaner; imported luxury goods and metals became scarce. Trade with the eastern Mediterranean, so vital in the Bronze Age, contracted dramatically, though it never entirely ceased — there is evidence of continuing, if reduced, contact between the Aegean and Cyprus, the Levant and even the Near East. The Greeks of this era lived by farming and herding, in small communities organised around the household and clan. There were no kings in the Bronze Age sense; leadership was exercised by local chieftains and the heads of powerful families (the basileis) within a society that the Homeric epics, written later, still vividly describe (World History Encyclopedia, "Greek Dark Age," worldhistory.org/Greek_Dark_Age/).

2. The "Dorian Invasion" — Myth or Migration?

The Greeks themselves explained the upheaval with a legend: the Return of the Heraclids — the descendants of Heracles, the Dorians, who "returned" to the Peloponnese generations after Heracles' death and seized Argos, Sparta and Messenia. In this account the Dorians, who spoke a distinct dialect, drove out or subjugated the earlier (Ionian and Achaean) populations. This tradition of a "Dorian invasion" was long taken literally, and indeed the dialect map of classical Greece — with a Dorian band across the Peloponnese and the southern Aegean, flanked by Ionians in Attica and the central Aegean — matches such a migration. Modern scholars, however, treat the story with caution: population movement was probably gradual and complex rather than a single invasion, and some of it may predate the collapse (World History Encyclopedia, "Dorian Invasion," worldhistory.org/Dorian_Invasion/).

Whatever its reality, the legend mattered enormously: it underwrote the ethnic identities and land claims of the classical Greek states, and it shaped the special character of Sparta, whose citizens believed themselves the purest of the Dorians.

3. Rebuilding — Population Growth and the New Order

From about 900 BC onwards, Greece began to recover. The archaeological record shows increasing population, the revival of trade, the reappearance of metal-working, and the growth of sanctuaries such as those at Olympia and Delphi, which attracted worshippers from across the Greek world and became meeting-places that helped knit the scattered communities into a single cultural whole. The Geometric style of pottery — named for its painted compass-drawn circles, meanders and abstract motifs — flourished in Athens from c. 900 BC and is sometimes so elaborate that scenes of funerals, battles and ships appear amid the decoration, giving us some of our earliest visual evidence for Greek life after the collapse (World History Encyclopedia, "Geometric Art," worldhistory.org/Geometric_Art/).

It is in this period that the fundamental unit of Greek civilisation — the polis (city-state) — began to take shape. The polis was far more than a city: it was a self-governing community of citizens, defined by shared religious cults, laws and institutions, centred on an acropolis (high town), an agora (marketplace and public space), and the surrounding countryside (chora). Over the next centuries hundreds of such poleis would emerge, each fiercely independent — the political form that would make Greek history so creative and so turbulent (World History Encyclopedia, "Polis," worldhistory.org/Polis/).

4. The Return of Literacy — The Phoenician Alphabet (c. 800 BC)

One of the most consequential moments in western history came in the early eighth century BC: the Greeks adopted the Phoenician alphabet. The Phoenicians of the Levant had developed a consonantal script, and the Greeks, in close trading contact with them through settlements such as Al-Mina in Syria and through traders in the Aegean, took its letters and — crucially — added vowels, creating the first true alphabet in which every sound of the language could be written. The direction of writing and letter forms would develop over time into the classical Greek alphabet, and from it would eventually descend the Latin alphabet of Rome and the modern western world (World History Encyclopedia, "Alphabet," worldhistory.org/alphabet/).

The consequences were immense. Once writing returned, so did recorded memory, law, literature and thought. The earliest surviving Greek alphabetic inscriptions date to around 770–720 BC. And it is no coincidence that this new literacy coincided with the composition — in writing — of the two greatest epics of antiquity.

5. Homer — The Iliad and the Odyssey

Around the late eighth century BC, the oral bard Homer composed, or fixed in written form, the Iliad and the Odyssey, the two foundational epics of Greek — and western — literature. The Iliad tells of the "wrath of Achilles" in the last year of the Trojan War; the Odyssey recounts the ten-year wanderings of Odysseus home from Troy. Long presented as the work of a single blind poet, the epics are now understood to stand at the end of a long oral tradition, shaped from generations of song; the "Homeric Question" of their authorship and unity has occupied scholars since antiquity (World History Encyclopedia, "Homer," worldhistory.org/homer/).

Whatever their composition, the epics became the Greeks' bible — the shared encyclopaedia of gods, heroes, ethics and history that educated every Greek from childhood. To the poets and thinkers of later ages, Homer was "the educator of Greece." His influence on language, values and self-image is incalculable: Greek religion, art, drama and even politics were all shaped by his account of how gods and heroes ought to behave. Alongside Homer stood Hesiod, whose Theogony (the genealogy of the gods) and Works and Days (a farmer's almanac and moral essay) give us the earliest systematic Greek theology and a realistic picture of ordinary life (World History Encyclopedia, "Hesiod," worldhistory.org/hesiod/).

6. The Age of Colonization (c. 750–550 BC)

With population rising and land in short supply at home, the Greeks poured out across the Mediterranean in one of history's great colonial movements. Between roughly 750 and 550 BC, Greek cities founded hundreds of colonies (apoikiai) — independent new poleis planted on foreign shores. The driving causes were land hunger, trade and political strife; colonies were organised expeditions led by an oikistes (founder) who, after consultation with the Delphic oracle, led settlers to a chosen site. Though linked by kinship, religion and commerce with their mother cities, colonies were politically independent (World History Encyclopedia, "Greek Colonization," worldhistory.org/Greek_Colonization/).

The result was a Greek presence strung around the Mediterranean like "frogs around a pond" (as Plato put it): in Italy and Sicily (“Magna Graecia” — Sybaris, Croton, Tarentum, Syracuse, Rhegium, Cumae, Naples/Ncapoli, Locri); on the coasts of the Black Sea (Byzantium, later Constantinople, and Sinope, Olbia); in North Africa (Cyrene in Libya); in southern France (Massalia, modern Marseille); in Spain (Emporion); and across the Aegean and Anatolian coast (“Ionia” — Miletus, Ephesus, Smyrna). These colonies, and especially the wealth of Ionia and Magna Graecia, spread Greek culture, trade and thought to the whole Mediterranean basin and made the Greeks a genuinely Mediterranean people (World History Encyclopedia, "Greek Colonization," worldhistory.org/Greek_Colonization/).

7. Panhellenic Sanctuaries & the Olympic Games (776 BC)

Amid the fragmentation of poleis and colonies, the Greeks remained united by shared sanctuaries, festivals and gods — the "Panhellenic" (all-Greek) institutions that transcended the individual city. Chief among them were the sanctuary of Apollo at Delphi, home of the famous oracle whose prophecies guided founders, kings and cities; the sanctuary of Zeus at Olympia; the isthmian games at Corinth; the Nemean games; and the Pythian games at Delphi. Contests of athletics, music and poetry drew competitors from every corner of the Greek world.

Above all stood the Olympic Games, traditionally founded in 776 BC in honour of Zeus at Olympia. Held every four years (the Olympiad), the games began with the single foot-race and grew to include boxing, wrestling, the pentathlon (discus, javelin, long jump, stadion race and wrestling), chariot-racing and more. They were so important that the Greeks used the four-year Olympiad as a system of dating. An Olympic truce suspended wars to let athletes travel in safety. Victory brought enormous honour — the winner received an olive wreath but, more importantly, undying glory for his city (World History Encyclopedia, "Olympic Games," worldhistory.org/Olympic_Games/).

These festivals, together with the shared language, the epics of Homer, and the common gods, gave the Greeks a powerful sense of common identity — expressed in the famous distinction between Greeks and "barbarians" (those who did not speak Greek) — even though politically they remained divided into fiercely independent city-states. It was a unity in culture, not in politics, that would prove both their genius and their fatal weakness.

7b. Funeral Customs, the Geometric Revival and Everyday Life

The Dark Ages are so named partly from the poverty of the material record, yet even here we can glimpse real people. The society of the eighth century BC emerges most vividly from its burial practices. In Athens, the great cemetery of the Kerameikos yields vases — above all the tall, monumental Geometric amphorae and kraters — that served as grave markers and vessels, often decorated with scenes of the prothesis (the laying out of the dead) and the ekphora (the funeral procession), with rows of mourners rending their hair in grief. The wealth of some Dark Age graves — such as the famous Athenian grave of 10th-century Athens (the "rich Athenian lady" burial) and the Lefkandi Toumba in Euboea, where around 1000 BC a great building ("the hero’s house") was raised over a royal burial with a woman and a sacrificed horse — shows that even in the supposed darkness there remained powerful chiefs and rich individuals. Everyday life was largely agrarian: the people grew barley and wheat, olives and vines, and raised sheep, goats, cattle and pigs; they wove cloth, made pottery, worked bronze and (increasingly) iron — the very name of the succeeding era reflects the adoption of iron for tools and weapons. The household (oikos) was the basic unit, under a male head, and society was organised in clans (gene) and tribes (phylai). Villages clustered resources and, over time, the synoecism — the drawing together of villages into a single city — produced the polis. The epic world of Homer, with its palaces, feasts, honours and heroes, reflects — though it does not simply photograph — the chieftain society of the Dark and early Archaic ages (World History Encyclopedia, "Greek Dark Age," worldhistory.org/Greek_Dark_Age/).

7c. The Emergence of the Mythological System

It was in the Dark Ages and early Archaic period, as the oral tradition crystallised, that the elaborate system of Greek mythology took the form we know. Homer’s epics were its great storehouse, but even more systematic was the Theogony of Hesiod (fl. c. 700 BC), which set out in ordered genealogy the whole divine family: from Chaos (Void) came Gaia (Earth) and Eros; from Gaia and Ouranos (Sky) the Titans, including Cronus; Cronus’ son Zeus overthrew his father in the Titanomachy (the war of the Titans) and established the rule of the Olympian gods on Mount Olympus. Hesiod’s Works and Days gave the equally influential myth of the Five Ages of Man — Gold, Silver, Bronze, the Age of Heroes, and the present Iron Age — a pessimistic account of decline that coloured all later Greek (and Roman) reflection on history. The myths explained the origins of the gods, of sacrifice, of kingship, of the seasons, and of Greek identity itself, and they were passed down through a professional class of bards (aoidoi) who recited the epics at festivals and courts long before they were written down. These stories — of Zeus’s many loves, of the labours of Heracles, the wanderings of Odysseus, the expeditions of the Argonauts and the heroes before Troy — formed the common inheritance, the shared religion and the artistic vocabulary of the entire Greek world and, through Rome, of Western civilisation (World History Encyclopedia, "Hesiod," worldhistory.org/hesiod/).
