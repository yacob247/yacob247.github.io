Volume One · c. 3200–1100 BC

The Bronze Age of Greece

Cyclades · Minoan Crete · Thera · Mycenae · Linear B · The Trojan War · The Bronze Age Collapse

Chronology note. All dates in this volume are approximate. The absolute chronology of the Aegean Bronze Age, especially before c. 1600 BC, is debated; here the traditional, Evans–derived dating is used. The World History Encyclopedia presents the same broad scheme but stresses that "the exact dates of many Bronze Age events remain uncertain."

1. The Land Before the Greeks

The story of Greek civilisation begins long before any people called themselves "Greeks." The Aegean Sea was a highway of islands and culture from the earliest times. In the third millennium BC, the islands of the Cyclades — Delos, Naxos, Paros, Melos, Thera (Santorini) and others — produced a distinctive culture remembered today chiefly for its strikingly modern-looking marble figurines: folded-arm female idols and seated male musicians with lyres. These Cycladic figures, made between roughly 3200 and 2000 BC, are among the most recognisable objects of world art and were found in graves, where they presumably accompanied the dead into the afterlife (World History Encyclopedia, "Cycladic Civilization," worldhistory.org/Cycladic_Civilization/).

The Cycladic islanders were skilled seafarers, traders in obsidian (a volcanic glass prized for blades and brought from Melos), marble, and metals. They built small, tightly-knit coastal settlements and engaged in long-distance exchange that connected them with the coasts of Anatolia, the Levant and Egypt. Their culture, however, would be overshadowed and ultimately absorbed by rising powers on the island of Crete.

2. Minoan Crete — The First Palace Civilisation

The first great Greek-adjacent civilisation arose not on the Greek mainland but on the large island of Crete. Around 2000 BC, imposing palace complexes were built at Knossos, Phaistos, Mallia and Zakros. Because the surviving mythic memory of a King Minos of Crete — owner of the Labyrinth and master of the Minotaur — seemed to match these ruins, the British archaeologist Sir Arthur Evans, who excavated Knossos from 1900, named this civilisation the Minoan. Evans' reconstruction, with its vividly restored frescoes, remains contested, but his label has stuck (World History Encyclopedia, "Minoan Civilization," worldhistory.org/Minoan_Civilization/).

Minoan palaces were not simply royal residences; they were combined religious, administrative, ceremonial and economic centres. Around vast central courts stood storage magazines filled with large ceramic pithoi (storage jars) for olive oil, wine and grain; workshops for craftsmen; and rooms whose walls were covered with brilliant frescoes of dolphins, saffron-gathering maidens, sea life, processions and — most famously — bull-leaping, where young acrobats vaulted over a charging bull's horns. The Minoans had running water and advanced drainage systems; at Knossos, terracotta pipes and even a primitive flushing toilet have been found, all of it a startlingly early example of sophisticated engineering (World History Encyclopedia, "Knossos," worldhistory.org/Knossos/).

The Minoans were a maritime trading people. Their fleets exported pottery, textiles, olive oil and wine across the eastern Mediterranean, reaching Egypt, Cyprus, the Levant and the Aegean islands, and importing copper, tin, gold, ivory and exotic materials. They wrote two scripts in succession: the hieroglyphic script and then Linear A, a still-undeciphered syllabic script used for administrative records (principally, it seems, for lists of commodities) on clay tablets. Because Linear A has not been read, the Minoan language remains unknown, and much of Minoan religion — dominated by goddess and nature worship, double axes (labrys), and sacred horns of consecration — remains a matter of inference (World History Encyclopedia, "Linear A Script," worldhistory.org/Linear_A_Script/).

3. The Eruption of Thera (c. 1600 BC)

One of the most dramatic events in the ancient world, the eruption of the volcanic island of Thera (modern Santorini), devastated the Aegean in the middle of the second millennium BC. The eruption was colossal — of a magnitude comparable to the 1815 Tambora eruption — and buried the prosperous Minoan town of Akrotiri under many metres of volcanic ash and pumice, preserving its multi-storey houses, wall paintings and equipment in a "Pompeii of the Aegean." Tsunamis generated by the eruption are thought to have struck the north coast of Crete, damaging Minoan ports and settlements (World History Encyclopedia, "Thera," worldhistory.org/therasimge/).

The dating of the eruption is hotly debated: palaeo-environmental evidence (ice cores and tree rings) suggests the mid-17th century BC (c. 1628–1600 BC), while the archaeological pottery sequence has traditionally pointed to c. 1500 BC. Whatever the precise date, the eruption coincided with the disruption, though not the immediate end, of the Minoan palaces, and Crete subsequently came increasingly under the influence — and eventually the control — of the Greek-speaking mainland.

4. The Mycenaeans — The First Greeks

On the Greek mainland, meanwhile, a distinct people had risen to power. These were the Mycenaeans, named after their greatest citadel, Mycenae in the Argolid. They were Greek-speaking — the earliest attested Greeks — whose name is preserved in the epic memory of Homer, who called the fighters before Troy "Achaeans" and "Danaans." Between about 1600 and 1100 BC they dominated the mainland from fortified citadels at Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos, Thebes and Athens, and extended their reach across the Aegean to Crete, the Cyclades and the Anatolian coast (World History Encyclopedia, "Mycenaean Civilization," worldhistory.org/Mycenaean_Civilization/).

Their wealth and warrior ethos are written in their tombs. The shaft graves of Mycenae (the two burial circles known as Grave Circles A and B), excavated from 1876 by Heinrich Schliemann, yielded astonishing treasures: golden death-masks, most famously the so-called "Mask of Agamemnon" (actually earlier than the legendary king's era), bronze swords and daggers inlaid with scenes of lion-hunting, gold cups, diadems and jewellery. Schliemann, who believed he had found the very world of Homer, telegraphed that he had "gazed upon the face of Agamemnon." The tholos tombs (great beehive-shaped stone tombs) of the later Mycenaean kings, such as the "Treasury of Atreus" at Mycenae, show engineering of remarkable sophistication (World History Encyclopedia, "Mycenae," worldhistory.org/mycenae/).

5. Linear B — The Earliest Greek Writing

Mycenaean administration is documented by the Linear B script, inscribed on clay tablets that survive chiefly because fires hardened them. In 1952 Michael Ventris, together with John Chadwick, demonstrated that Linear B was an early form of Greek — a breakthrough that pushed the attested history of the Greek language back by some seven centuries. The tablets from Pylos, Knossos and elsewhere are essentially bureaucratic inventories: lists of people, animals, agricultural produce, bronze, chariots and religious offerings, and the records of the wanax (king), the lawagetas (war-leader) and various officials (World History Encyclopedia, "Linear B Script," worldhistory.org/Linear_B_Script/).

These dry accounts give us a vivid picture of a palace economy: a complex redistributive system in which the palaces collected, stored and re-issued goods, and supported workshops of smiths, weavers and perfumers. The tablets also record the names of gods later known to the classical Greeks — Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Dionysos, Artemis, Athena — showing religious continuity between the Bronze Age and the classical era.

6. The Trojan War (c. 1250–1180 BC)

The greatest single narrative of the Bronze Age is the Trojan War, the ten-year Greek siege of the city of Troy (Ilios) on the north-west coast of Anatolia, preserved for us by Homer's Iliad and Odyssey and by a vast surrounding body of epic. According to legend, the war was fought because the Trojan prince Paris abducted Helen, wife of the Spartan king Menelaus; his brother Agamemnon, king of Mycenae, then led a great Greek expedition against Troy. The roster of the besiegers reads like a gallery of the Greek heroic age: Achilles, Ajax, Diomedes, Nestor, Odysseus; on the Trojan side, Hector, Priam and Aeneas. The city was finally taken by the famous stratagem of the Wooden Horse (World History Encyclopedia, "Trojan War," worldhistory.org/Trojan_War/).

Whether the war happened as Homer told it is unknowable, but archaeology shows that a great fortified city at the site of Hisarlik (Troy) was destroyed by fire around 1250–1180 BC — a level (Troy VIIa) that many scholars associate with the story that the Greeks themselves believed. Whatever the historical kernel, the Trojan War became the founding myth of Greek identity, the common inheritance recited as the touchstone of Greek valour for a thousand years.

7. The Bronze Age Collapse (c. 1200–1100 BC)

At the end of the thirteenth and in the twelfth century BC, the entire eastern Mediterranean suffered a catastrophic series of destructions. The great Mycenaean palaces — Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos, Thebes — were burned and abandoned; the Hittite empire of Anatolia collapsed; the kingdom of Ugarit in Syria was destroyed; Egypt was attacked by the mysterious "Sea Peoples" and barely survived. The causes are debated — a concatenation of invasion, internal revolt, climate change, drought, famine and the disruption of trade — but the result was definitive: the palace-centred world of the Bronze Age came to an end (World History Encyclopedia, "Sea Peoples," worldhistory.org/Sea_Peoples/).

In Greece, the collapse brought the age of palaces to a close. Writing (Linear B) disappeared; the great citadels were destroyed or shrank into villages; long-distance trade collapsed; and population declined sharply. Greece entered the period that later generations would call the "Dark Ages." Yet the Greeks never forgot their heroic past — the songs of the Trojan heroes, rehearsed by bards, would carry the memory of the Bronze Age through the centuries of darkness and eruption into the brilliant world of the classical polis.

8. Who Were the "Heroes"?

The Bronze Age was, to the classical Greeks, the age of heroes — the semi-divine generation who lived between the gods and ordinary men. The great families of later Greek cities claimed descent from these heroes and their gods. The Heraclids traced themselves to Heracles; Spartan kings claimed descent from Heracles through his great-great-grandson; Athenian aristocrats traced lineage to Theseus, the legendary king who slew the Minotaur in the Labyrinth of Knossos. Through these genealogies the Bronze Age was not merely ancient history seen from a distance — it was living memory, the source of identity, land-rights and pride. The epics of Homer, whatever their historical basis, therefore preserved for all subsequent Greek civilisation its own distant and heroic origin (World History Encyclopedia, "Homer," worldhistory.org/homer/).

8b. The Mycenaean Palaces — Economy, Religion and Administration

The Mycenaean palace states reveal themselves most intimately through the Linear B tablets recovered principally from Pylos, Knossos, Thebes and Mycenae. They show a highly organised, centralised society. At its head stood the wanax (king), who exercised religious and secular authority, ruling alongside the lawagetas ("leader of the people" or war-leader), a group of nobles or hequetai ("companions/followers"), local officials, craftsmen and a large body of dependent workers. The tablets record an intricate redistributive economy: the palaces collected grain, olives, figs, wine, livestock, bronze and textiles from their territories, stored them in vast quantities (the Pylos and Knossos storerooms), and re-issued them as rations to palace dependents — including entire workforces of smiths, weavers, fullers and perfumers, and the rowers and soldiers of the king. Land tenure was carefully recorded, distinguishing royal, temple and private holdings. The religion of the tablets is rich and strikingly familiar: offerings are recorded to Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Hermes, Ares, Dionysus, Artemis, Athena, and a goddess named "Potnia" ("Mistress"), and there are already the characteristic features of Greek cult such as processions and sacred vessels. The invention or perfection of the chariot and of bronze panoply, the building of the great Cyclopean walls (the massive stone fortifications such as those of Tiryns and Mycenae, which the later Greeks believed had been built by the one-eyed giants the Cyclopes) and the richly furnished tholos tombs all belong to this palace world. When this world collapsed around 1200 BC, the tablets ceased — the bureaucratic writing died with the palaces — but the religion and the memory of the kings lived on in epic and cult (World History Encyclopedia, "Linear B Script," worldhistory.org/Linear_B_Script/).

8c. The Archaeology of the Greek Bronze Age — The Great Excavators

Our knowledge of the Greek Bronze Age is the achievement of a remarkable series of archaeologists. Heinrich Schliemann (1822–1890), a self-made German businessman obsessed with Homer, excavated Troy at Hisarlik from 1871 and then Mycenae from 1876, where he dug the shaft graves and, claiming to have found the Masque of Agamemnon, famously telegraphed the king of Greece that he had "gazed upon the face of Agamemnon." His methods were crude by modern standards and his interpretations often wrong, but he put the Bronze Age on the map and inspired all that followed. Arthur Evans (1851–1941), keeper of the Ashmolean Museum, excavated Knossos from 1900 and gave the name "Minoan" to its civilisation; his reconstruction of the palace (with its now-iconic painted columns and frescoes) remains both beloved and controversial among scholars. Carl Blegen (1887–1971) excavated the Mycenaean palace of Pylos (the "Palace of Nestor") from 1939, recovering the great archive of Linear B tablets, and in 1952 Michael Ventris, a young British architect, with the philologist John Chadwick, deciphered Linear B as an early form of Greek — one of the great intellectual breakthroughs of the twentieth century. The site of Akrotiri on Santorini, preserved under volcanic ash like a Bronze Age Pompeii and excavated from 1967 under Spyridon Marinatos, has revealed the everyday world of the Minoanised Aegean. Together these excavations and decipherments built, layer by layer, the now-familiar picture of the first great European civilisations (World History Encyclopedia, "Mycenae," worldhistory.org/mycenae/).

8d. The Chronology of the Greek Bronze Age

Because the Bronze Age has no written dates, scholars organise it into archaeological periods named from pottery styles and sites. In the Aegean the standard scheme, derived from Evans’ work at Knossos, divides each region into Early, Middle and Late phases on a relative scale (e.g., "Late Helladic IIIB" for mainland Greece, c. 1300–1200 BC, the period of the palace of Pylos and the Trojan war tradition). The traditional absolute dates are anchored by synchronisms with the historically dated civilisations of Egypt and the Near East — Egyptian scarabs, Mycenaean pottery found in dated Levantine contexts, and the famous list of Aegean places in Egyptian texts — and by scientific methods such as tree-ring and radiocarbon dating. These have produced the broad framework used in this volume: Minoan palaces from c. 2000 BC; their height c. 1750–1450 BC; the eruption of Thera placed by science in the seventeenth century BC (by Egyptological and pottery cross-dating possibly c. 1500 BC, a famous controversy); the Mycenaean palaces c. 1400–1200 BC; and their destruction c. 1200–1100 BC. The disputes — above all the date of the Thera eruption — remain the subject of lively scholarly debate, since they affect whether Thera caused the end of the Old Palaces and exactly how to correlate Aegean and Near Eastern history. What is not in doubt is the sequence of rise and fall that shaped the world into which Homer’s heroes stepped (World History Encyclopedia, "Greece," worldhistory.org/greece/).

8e. Trade, Ships and the Mediterranean World

The Bronze Age Aegean was no isolated backwater; it was knitted by seaborne trade into the wider Mediterranean. The fleets of the Minoans and then the Mycenaeans carried goods across the sea: Aegean pottery, olive oil, wine, textiles and bronze objects travelled to Egypt, the Levant, Cyprus and beyond; and in return came the precious and essential materials Greece lacked — copper (from Cyprus, whose very name means "copper," and from elsewhere) and tin (needed with copper to make bronze, imported from distant sources), gold (from Egypt and the Near East), ivory, semiprecious stones and exotic perfumes. The famous Uluburun shipwreck (off the coast of Turkey, wrecked c. 1300 BC), with its cargo of copper and tin ingots, ivory, glass, ostrich-egg shells, spices, gold, and items from Greece, Egypt, the Levant, Cyprus and even the Baltic amber, is a spectacular witness to this international trade. Long ships, painted in the frescoes and seals, carried traders, warriors and raiders; and with goods travelled ideas, religious symbols and artistic styles — the "Orientalising" motifs of the Aegean, the exchange of fashions in pottery and jewellery, and eventually the alphabet itself. In this interconnected world the Aegean thrived until the great collapse of c. 1200 BC, when the disruption of these trade routes — a factor in the "Sea Peoples" crisis — helped bring the bronze palaces down (World History Encyclopedia, "Uluburun Shipwreck," worldhistory.org/Uluburun_Shipwreck/).
