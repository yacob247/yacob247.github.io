The Aztec (Mexica) Civilization

Last of the great Mesoamerican civilizations, the Aztecs built their island capital Tenochtitlan in the middle of a lake, forged a mighty empire from the Triple Alliance, and ruled millions until the coming of the Spanish conquistadors in 1519. I. Overview

The Aztec Empire (c. 1345–1521) was, at its greatest extent, the dominant power of Mesoamerica, covering most of northern Mexico — an area of some 135,000 square kilometres. Its capital, Tenochtitlan, stood on an island in Lake Texcoco and was among the largest cities of the world in the early sixteenth century, with perhaps 200,000–300,000 inhabitants (World History Encyclopedia, "Aztec Civilization").

The Aztec civilization is the best-documented of the Mesoamerican cultures, because the sources include native codices, archaeology, and the long, detailed accounts of the Spanish conquerors and clergy. Spanish sources are biased, but combined with archaeology they yield a rich picture of Aztec institutions, religion, daily life and warfare (WHE).

The name "Aztec" comes from Aztlán ("Land of the White Herons"), the mythical homeland of the Mexica, who along with other Nahuatl-speaking peoples formed the group we now call the Aztecs (WHE).

II. Geography & Historical Context

The Valley of Mexico is a highland basin (c. 2,240 metres above sea level) that was originally a great lake system. Here numerous city-states, or altépetl, competed for farmland, water and tribute. The valley is surrounded by volcanic mountains that provided obsidian — a precious resource for tools and weapons — and lay at the crossroads of Mesoamerican trade routes.

Around 1100, the valley's city-states began competing fiercely for resources. By c. 1400 several small empires had formed, the dominant ones being Texcoco (capital of the Acolhua) and Azcapotzalco (capital of the Tepanec). These two collided in 1428 in the Tepanec War (WHE).

Following Azcapotzalco's defeat by an alliance of Texcoco, Tenochtitlan and Tlacopan, the Triple Alliance was formed, and an aggressive campaign of territorial expansion began, with the spoils — usually tributes — shared between the three cities. Over time, Tenochtitlan came to dominate the alliance, and its ruler became the supreme emperor, the Huey Tlatoani (WHE).

III. Origins & the Founding of Tenochtitlan

The Mexica's founding story is both myth and history. According to tradition, the people migrated from the mythical land of plenty, Aztlán, in the far northwest. Their patron god Huitzilopochtli led them, and told them to build their city where they saw an eagle perched on a cactus devouring a snake (WHE).

That vision was found on a small island in Lake Texcoco, and there the Mexica founded Tenochtitlan in c. 1325. The god also gave them their name, the Mexica. The image of the eagle on the cactus remains the central emblem of the Mexican flag today.

At first, the Mexica were a marginal, tribute-paying people, but they were skilled warriors and shrewd diplomats. By allying with the right city-states at the right time, they rose rapidly from lake-dwellers to imperial masters.

IV. Political History & the Rulers of Tenochtitlan

The Aztec tlatoque (rulers) of Tenochtitlan formed a dynasty from the late fourteenth century to the Spanish conquest. As the city grew, each ruler increased its power. The great expansion began with Itzcoatl (1427–1440), who defeated Azcapotzalco and forged the Triple Alliance.

V. Society & Daily Life

Aztec society was rigidly stratified, with clear classes: at the top the local rulers (teteuhctin), then nobles (pipiltin), commoners (macehualtin), serfs (mayeque), and slaves (tlacohtin). There was some movement between classes, especially in the lower orders (WHE).

Daily life centred on the household, the extended family, and the calpulli — a ward or clan that owned land and ran schools, temples and the local market. Children were educated at home until the age of ten or so, then sent either to the telpochcalli (school for commoners) or the calmecac (school for nobles).

Food was based on maize, beans, squash, chilli, amaranth, tomatoes and avocado. Nixtamalization — soaking maize in lime — made it nutritious and produced the tortilla, the everyday staple. Cacao was fermented into a bitter, spiced drink and its beans were used as currency.

Tenochtitlan was not only the political and religious capital but a great trading centre. Goods flowed in: gold, greenstone, turquoise, cotton, cacao, tobacco, pottery, tools, weapons, foodstuffs and slaves (WHE).

VI. Religion & Mythology

Religion and life were inseparable for the Aztecs. Their pantheon mixed ancient Mesoamerican gods with specifically Mexica deities. The two principal gods were Huitzilopochtli (war/sun) and Tlaloc (rain), each with its own temple on the twin summit of the Templo Mayor (WHE).

Other major gods included Quetzalcoatl (the feathered serpent), Tezcatlipoca (smoking mirror), Xipe Totec (spring), Xiuhtecuhtli (fire), Xochipilli (flowers and dance), Ometeotl (the creator), Mictlantecuhtli (the dead) and Coatlicue (the earth-mother).

Ceremonies were governed by complex calendars. The 260-day ritual calendar (tonalpohualli) and the 365-day solar year interlocked to form a 52-year cycle at the end of which the New Fire ceremony was held. The 584-day cycle of Venus and the movements of the stars were also carefully observed (WHE).

The Aztecs believed the world passed through a series of cosmic ages, each with its own sun, and that the present fifth age would end unless the gods were nourished. The gods were honoured with festivals, music, dance, incense, blood-letting and animal sacrifice. Human sacrifice was a central rite, performed to "feed" the gods and keep the sun moving. Victims were usually prisoners of war, and the heart was removed — or, less commonly, the victim was decapitated or forced to fight a hopeless contest (WHE).

VII. Architecture, Art & Engineering

Tenochtitlan was a wonder. The city was built on an island with great canals criss-crossing it, surrounded by chinampas — artificial "floating" fields that hugely increased agricultural capacity. There were anti-flood dykes, reservoirs for fresh water, and causeways linking the island to the shore. The city was designed to inspire awe in visiting nobles (WHE).

The Templo Mayor dominated the Sacred Precinct at the city's heart, a double pyramid representing Coatepec, the sacred snake mountain of myth. Its ruins, discovered in the centre of Mexico City, have yielded thousands of objects. The famous Sun Stone (the "Aztec Calendar Stone") shows the five cosmic ages; the Stone of Tizoc and the throne of Motecuhzoma II present imperial ideology and link rulers to cosmic events.

Aztec art was eclectic: from miniature carved precious objects (amethyst, rock crystal, gold, silver, exotic feathers) to colossal stone sculptures such as the Coatlicue statue. Turquoise mosaic was especially prized, as in the mask of Xiuhtecuhtli. Cholula ware, a fine painted pottery, was much admired (WHE).

VIII. Technology, Science & Inventions

The Aztecs were brilliant engineers. The chinampa system was among the most productive farming systems of the ancient world, turning shallow lakebed into fertile, irrigated garden plots. They built causeways and aqueducts, such as the one that carried fresh water from Chapultepec to Tenochtitlan.

In astronomy and time-keeping they developed two interlocking calendars and accurately tracked the planet Venus. Their system of the "five suns" (cosmic ages) is recorded on the Sun Stone. In medicine they practised herbal remedies and used a form of anaesthesia; in writing they recorded history, tribute and ritual in fold-out codices.

They had a sophisticated system of pictographic writing and a numerical system based on 20. The macuahuitl — a wooden club edged with obsidian — was a fearsomely effective weapon, and the atlatl (spear-thrower) hurled darts with great force.

IX. Warfare & the Military

The Aztec army was the instrument of the empire. It was bolstered by conscription of all adult males, by troops supplied by allied and conquered states, and by elite orders such as the Eagle and Jaguar warriors. Elite warriors wore spectacular feathered and animal-skin costumes and headdresses to signify rank, and carried the chimalli shield and the macuahuitl.

Warriors wore padded cotton armour and a wooden or reed shield covered in hide. They wielded the macuahuitl, the atlatl, and bow and arrows. Battles were concentrated around major cities; when a city fell, the victors claimed the whole territory, extracted regular tribute, and took captives back to Tenochtitlan for ritual sacrifice (WHE).

The "Flower Wars" were special ceremonial conflicts fought to capture prisoners for sacrifice and to test warriors. The empire was held together by appointed officials, intermarriage, gift-giving, invitations to ceremonies, monument-building, and the constant threat of military intervention. Some states were buffer zones against hostile neighbours like the Tarascan civilization (WHE).

X. Culture, Writing & Learning

The Aztecs spoke Nahuatl, a Uto-Aztecan language still spoken by over a million people in Mexico. Their writing was pictographic and ideographic, recorded in codices made of folded bark paper or deerskin. A small number survive — notably the Codex Borgia — because the Spanish destroyed most of them.

Education was universal: boys went to the telpochcalli for military training or the calmecac for priestly and noble education. Poetry and song were highly valued; Aztec poets such as Nezahualcóyotl are celebrated for their philosophical verse. The famous ollamaliztli ballgame had deep ritual importance.

Their calendar, mathematics (base-20), and astronomical observation were remarkable. Much of what we know comes from the Florentine Codex, compiled by the Spanish friar Bernardino de Sahagún with indigenous informants.

XI. Decline & Legacy

The empire had always faced rebellions, typically when new rulers took power at Tenochtitlan, but these were usually crushed. The tide turned when the Aztecs were heavily defeated by the Tlaxcala and Huexotzingo in 1515. When the Spanish arrived under Hernán Cortés, rebel states seized the chance to break away (WHE).

Relations with Motecuhzoma II were initially friendly and gifts exchanged, but turned sour after Spanish soldiers were killed at Tenochtitlan. The Aztec warriors overthrew Motecuhzoma and set Cuitláhuac as tlatoani. Cortés returned and, after the disastrous Noche Triste (30 June 1520), gathered indigenous allies and laid siege. Ravaged by smallpox and lacking food, the city finally fell on 13 August 1521 under the last tlatoani, Cuauhtémoc (WHE).

Tenochtitlan was sacked and its monuments destroyed. From its ruins rose Mexico City, the capital of New Spain. The long line of Mesoamerican civilizations from the Olmec to the Aztec had ended abruptly. Yet the Aztec legacy endures — in the Nahuatl language, in the name of Mexico, in the food of the world (chocolate, tomatoes, avocados, chillies), and in the monumental art and calendar of the Sun Stone.

The Founding

c. 1325
Click to reveal the story
c. 1325 — The Founding of Tenochtitlan

According to Mexica tradition, the wandering people of Aztlán were led by their patron god Huitzilopochtli to a small marshy island in the middle of Lake Texcoco. There they beheld the sign the god had promised: an eagle perched on a cactus devouring a serpent. On that spot they built their new home, naming it Tenochtitlan — "the place of the prickly pear cactus." The date is traditionally given as 1325.

The site was far from ideal: it was a small island in a lake, short of fresh water and farmland. But it was also nearly impossible to attack, and it gave the Mexica a defensive base at the heart of the valley. They began a long climb. At first they served as mercenaries and tribute-payers to the powerful altepetl around the lake. Over the next century they dredged the shallow lakebed to build chinampas, raised causeways, and engineered aqueducts. By 1428 they had become the dominant power of the Valley of Mexico, and their island city would grow into the largest metropolis of the pre-Columbian Americas.

The eagle-and-cactus symbol is still the national emblem of Mexico, a living memory of the city's mythic founding.

The Tepanec War

1428
Click to reveal the story
1428 — The Tepanec War & the Triple Alliance

By the early fifteenth century the Valley of Mexico was dominated by two rivals: Texcoco (capital of the Acolhua) and Azcapotzalco (capital of the Tepanec). In 1428 war broke out. Tenochtitlan, under its brilliant ruler Itzcoatl, allied with Texcoco and the small Tepanec city of Tlacopan to crush Azcapotzalco.

The victory was decisive. The three victors formed the Triple Alliance, a confederation that would become the engine of the Aztec Empire. They divided the spoils of war and began a campaign of aggressive expansion. Tribute flowed in from conquered peoples, and the alliance's armies — equipped with the macuahuitl, the atlatl and the bow — swept aside rivals.

Over time the balance of power shifted. Although the alliance was formally equal, Tenochtitlan came to dominate, and its ruler took the title Huey Tlatoani, "Great Speaker." Itzcoatl, who had launched the war, is rightly regarded as the true founder of the Aztec Empire — the man who turned a lake island into the heart of a conquest state.

Motecuhzoma I

1440–1469
Click to reveal the story
1440–1469 — The Reign of Motecuhzoma I

Motecuhzoma I ("He Who Makes Himself a Lord") inherited an empire already on the rise and transformed it into a true imperial power. He was a great conqueror, extending Aztec control far beyond the valley to the Gulf Coast, the Huastec lands, and into Oaxaca. Under him the tributary system was consolidated, and the Huey Tlatoani became the unquestioned master of central Mexico.

Motecuhzoma I was also a builder. He commissioned the great aqueduct carrying fresh water from Chapultepec to Tenochtitlan, and began the causeways that linked the island city to the mainland. He ordered the enlargement of the Templo Mayor, the great double pyramid dedicated to Huitzilopochtli and Tlaloc.

His reign was a turning point: what had been a loose alliance of city-states became a centralised empire with a powerful capital, a professional army, and an ideology that linked the emperor's authority to cosmic destiny. He set the pattern that his successors — Axayacatl, Tizoc, Ahuitzotl — would follow, until the empire reached its greatest extent and met the Spanish.

Ahuitzotl

1486–1502
Click to reveal the story
1486–1502 — Ahuitzotl, the Greatest Expansionist

Ahuitzotl ("Water Monster") was the most expansionist of the Aztec tlatoque. Under him the empire reached its maximum territorial extent, stretching from the Pacific to parts of the Gulf Coast and deep into Oaxaca and Guatemala. He was a ruthless and masterful general.

Ahuitzotl is most famous for the great rededication of the Templo Mayor in 1487. The ceremony, held to inaugurate the enlarged pyramid, is described by some sources as involving thousands of ritual sacrifices over four days — a terrifying display of Aztec power and piety intended to awe both the gods and the empire's enemies.

He also built extensively, including the aqueduct and causeways. But his reign ended with a devastating flood of Tenochtitlan in 1502, and he died the same year. He was succeeded by his nephew Motecuhzoma II, the last great emperor before the Spanish arrival.

The Spanish Arrive

1519
Click to reveal the story
1519 — The Spanish Arrive

In April 1519, a small fleet commanded by Hernán Cortés landed on the Gulf Coast of Mexico. Cortés had been sent from Cuba on an expedition that had been cancelled, but he defied his orders, burned his ships, and marched inland. He was an outcast with a few hundred men, horses, cannons and steel weapons — but he was a master of diplomacy and opportunism.

Cortés found a land in ferment. The Aztec Empire ruled by force and tribute, and many subject peoples — especially the Tlaxcalans, the Aztecs' unconquered enemy — were eager for a chance at revenge. Cortés also stumbled into the Aztec legend of Quetzalcoatl, the feathered-serpent god whose return was prophesied. Later Spanish accounts claimed the Aztecs mistook Cortés for the god; most historians now consider that story a later invention, but the fear and uncertainty it represented were real.

By November 1519, Cortés had marched on the Tenochtitlan and was received by Motecuhzoma II in the capital — a meeting that would set in motion the downfall of the greatest empire in Mesoamerica.

The Noche Triste

30 June 1520
Click to reveal the story
30 June 1520 — The Noche Triste

Relations between the Spanish and the Aztecs had collapsed. Motecuhzoma II had been taken hostage, and then was killed during a revolt — whether by the Spanish or by his own people is debated. The Aztec warriors, furious at Motecuhzoma's passivity, had installed Cuitláhuac as tlatoani and risen against the Spanish, besieging them in the palace.

On the night of 30 June 1520, Cortés decided to abandon the city and flee. The Spanish and their Tlaxcalan allies tried to slip out along a causeway under cover of darkness, but they were discovered. Fighting broke out in the dark; hundreds of Spanish soldiers and thousands of allies were killed or drowned in the canals, laden with gold. Cortés reportedly wept at the loss and later called it the "Noche Triste" — the "Sad Night."

The Spanish were driven out, but the victory was hollow. The Aztecs had been devastated — not just by the fighting, but far more by the conquistadors' invisible ally. European diseases, especially smallpox, swept through the city; by the time Cortés returned, the population had been decimated. Cuitláhuac himself died of the disease, and the last tlatoani, Cuauhtémoc, took the throne in a city already dying.

The Fall of Tenochtitlan

13 August 1521
Click to reveal the story
13 August 1521 — The Fall of Tenochtitlan

Ten months after his flight, Cortés returned to besiege Tenochtitlan. He had gathered a vast army of indigenous allies — Tlaxcalans, Texcocans and others who saw an opportunity to destroy their oppressor. The Spanish built small ships (bergantines) to control the lake, and cut the city's causeways and aqueduct.

The siege lasted months. The city, starving under blockade and ravaged by smallpox, put up a desperate defence under the last tlatoani, Cuauhtémoc. The Aztec warriors, armed with macuahuitl and atlatl, fought the Spanish steel, guns and horses street by street, but the odds were hopeless. On 13 August 1521, after the last of the city fell, Cuauhtémoc was captured while fleeing by canoe.

The city was sacked and razed. Over the next decades, the lake was drained and Mexico City was built on the ruins. The great Mesoamerican tradition that stretched back to the Olmec came to an end. Modern scholarship stresses that the Spanish won not only by steel and disease, but because they harnessed indigenous resentment of Aztec rule.
