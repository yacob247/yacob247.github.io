The Maya Civilization

The masters of the ancient Americas in writing, mathematics and astronomy — inventors of the zero, keepers of the Long Count calendar, builders of Tikal, Calakmul, Palenque and Chichén Itzá. The Maya World at a Glance

Name & People
The Maya — dozens of related peoples speaking some 30 Mayan languages. They never called their whole world one empire; each city was a separate ajaw-ruled kingdom.
Homeland
The Maya lowlands and highlands — modern southern Mexico, Belize, Guatemala, Honduras and El Salvador.
Period
Preclassic from c. 2000 BC; Classic florescence AD 250–900; Postclassic to the Spanish conquest 1524–1546 and the fall of Nojpetén in 1697.
Government
Divine kingship. Each city-state ruled by a holy lord, the kʼuhul ajaw, advised by nobles (sajals) and sustained by tribute and war.
Religion
Polytheistic and calendrical; creator gods Itzamna and Chaahk; the Popol Vuh creation epic; bloodletting and ballgame ritual.
Writing & Science
The only full writing system of the pre-Columbian Americas — Maya glyphs; base-20 mathematics with zero; eclipse prediction and the 260-day Tzolkʼin.
Greatest Cities
Tikal, Calakmul, Palenque, Copán, Yaxchilán, Uxmal and Chichén Itzá — each with its own dynasty and emblem glyph.
Fall
The Classic cities of the southern lowlands were largely abandoned in the 9th century; northern cities flourished until the Spanish conquest.

I. Overview

The Maya world (c. 2000 BC–AD 1500) was never a single empire but a mosaic of sixty or more rival kingdoms sharing one script, one calendar and one pantheon — stretching from the highland valleys of Chiapas and Guatemala to the limestone plains of Yucatán, across modern Mexico, Belize, Guatemala, Honduras and El Salvador. At its Classic peak (AD 250–900) cities such as Tikal, with perhaps 60,000–100,000 inhabitants, and Calakmul, with 50,000 or more, were among the largest cities on earth — dwarfing contemporary London or Paris, which held only tens of thousands (World History Encyclopedia, “Maya Civilization”; Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Maya”).

What sets the Maya apart from every other civilization of the ancient Americas is the life of the mind. They developed the only full writing system of the pre-Columbian New World — more than 800 distinct hieroglyphic signs, now roughly ninety percent deciphered — and used it to record history, astronomy, ritual and myth on stone stelae, painted vases, jade ornaments and screenfold bark-paper codices. Their mathematicians employed an explicit shell-sign for zero within a base-twenty system centuries before Europe adopted the Hindu-Arabic zero; their astronomers tracked the 584-day cycle of Venus and predicted eclipses with naked-eye observatories such as the Caracol at Chichén Itzá (WHE).

Politically the Classic Maya lived under divine kingship. Each major city was ruled by a kʼuhul ajaw — a “holy lord” who claimed descent from gods, embodied the maize god in dance and ritual, and sustained his court by tribute, trade and war. Great overlords such as Yuknoom Chʼeen II of Calakmul (reigned 636–686) “oversaw” the accessions of client kings across twenty courts; their rivals at Tikal answered with shattering reversals such as the battle of 5 August 695, when Jasaw Chan Kʼawiil broke the Snake kingdomʼs power forever. The inscriptions record some two centuries of such hegemonic struggle in extraordinary detail — the most richly documented political history of the ancient Americas.

The southern lowland cities were largely abandoned in the ninth century AD — the famous “Classic collapse,” driven by some combination of prolonged drought, warfare, soil exhaustion and revolt — yet Maya civilization did not end. Northern cities such as Uxmal and Chichén Itzá flourished for centuries more; the Itzá kingdom of Nojpetén held Lake Petén until 13 March 1697, 176 years after the fall of Tenochtitlan. Today more than seven million Maya people live on, speaking some thirty Mayan languages — the heirs of the scribes who wrote the sky in stone (Britannica).

II. Geography & Environment

The Maya world divides into three great zones. The southern lowlands — the Petén of Guatemala, Belize, and the Usumacinta basin — are rainforest over porous karst limestone: there are almost no surface rivers, and rain vanishes into the rock. Maya engineers answered with vast reservoirs, dammed bajos (seasonal swamps), canal systems and raised fields; Tikalʼs six giant reservoirs held nearly a million cubic metres of water, and the cityʼs plazas were sloped to feed them (WHE, “Tikal”).

The highlands of Chiapas and Guatemala — volcanic ridges, pine forest, obsidian flows at El Chayal and San Martín Jilotepeque, jade in the Motagua valley, cacao on the Pacific slope — supplied the lowlands with tools, prestige stone and chocolate money. The northern lowlands of Yucatán are dry scrub with no rivers at all: life depended on cenotes, natural sinkholes piercing to groundwater, which the Maya revered as portals to the watery underworld Xibalbá. Chichén Itzáʼs Sacred Cenote — 60 metres wide — received centuries of offerings: jade, gold, copal, and human victims (WHE, “Chichén Itzá”).

Detailed treatment of each zone — soils, rainfall, trade corridors, and how environment shaped collapse — is given in the companion volume Preclassic Origins and in The Classic Collapse.

III. Origins & the Preclassic (c. 2000 BC–AD 250)

Farming villages appear along the Pacific coast and in Belize by c. 2000 BC. By 1000 BC the lowlands held E-Group observatory plazas — a western pyramid facing a long eastern platform tracking solstices — and triadic pyramids with three shrines honouring the three hearthstones of creation. The first great Maya state was El Mirador (c. 300 BC–AD 150) in the Mirador basin: its Danta complex, 72 metres high and 2.8 million cubic metres in volume, is among the largest pyramids ever built anywhere, linked to sister cities by 40-kilometre raised causeways (WHE, “El Mirador”).

El Miradorʼs fall around AD 150 — drought, soil exhaustion, perhaps revolt — emptied the basin for centuries but seeded the Classic order: Tikal and Calakmul inherited its causeways, its triadic gods and its Long Count calendar. Then, on 16 January 378, came the entrada: Siyaj Kʼakʼ (“Fire is Born”), a warlord of distant Teotihuacan, entered Tikal; its king Chak Tok Ichʼaak died the same day, and a new dynasty under Yax Nuun Ahiin, son of Teotihuacanʼs king, took the throne. Mexican talud-tablero architecture, goggle-eyed Tlaloc imagery and atlatl warfare spread from Copán to Palenque — the full story in Vol. 1: Preclassic Origins.

IV. Political History & the Tikal–Calakmul Wars

Classic Maya politics was hegemonic rather than territorial. A great kʼuhul ajaw did not annex his neighbours; he “oversaw” (yichnal) their accessions, married his daughters to their kings, demanded tribute and captives, and humiliated defectors in Star Wars — great Venus-timed battles recorded with a star-shell glyph. For two centuries the lowlands were divided between two superpowers: Tikal (Mutal) in the east and Calakmul (Kanul, the Snake) in the north, each encircling the other with clients (WHE, “Calakmul”).

After Tikalʼs defeat in 562 and the 130-year “hiatus” that followed, Calakmulʼs Yuknoom Chʼeen II the Great (636–686) built the largest political network in Maya history: some twenty courts from Naranjo to Piedras Negras “held the Snake aloft,” and in 657 his ally Dos Pilas trampled Tikal itself and sent its king into exile. The system lasted one reign too long. On 5 August 695 Jasaw Chan Kʼawiil I of Tikal met the Snake army in the Bajo El Laberinto and annihilated it, seizing the Calakmul kingʼs palanquin, gods and regalia; his son Yikʼin Chan Kʼawiil completed the revenge in 743–744 by breaking El Peru-Waka, Calakmulʼs western shield. The full campaign narratives, inscriptions and battle archaeology are told in Vol. 2: The Tikal–Calakmul Wars.

The west and south-east followed their own arcs. At Palenque, Pakal the Great (615–683) rebuilt a twice-sacked city into the loveliest in the Maya world; his sons Kan Bʼalam II and Kʼan Joy Chitam II completed his Cross Group temples and then lost his glory when Toniná captured the younger brother in 711 (Vol. 3). On the Usumacinta, Shield Jaguar of Yaxchilán (681–742) and his son Bird Jaguar IV (752–768) carved their legitimacy across door-lintels with queen Lady Xookʼs bloodletting visions. On the south-eastern frontier, Yax Kʼukʼ Moʼ founded Copán in 426 with Mexican warriors; his thirteenth heir 18 Rabbit (695–738) made it the Athens of the Maya — then was captured by his own vassal Quiriguá and beheaded on 3 May 738 (Vol. 4).

V. Society & Daily Life

Maya society ran from the holy lord and his court — sajals (warrior-nobles governing secondary towns), aj kʼuhuun (priests), scribes and astronomers — through merchants, artisans and the great mass of farmers. The farmerʼs year turned on the milpa: maize, beans and squash planted in forest clearings, farmed a few seasons, then left fallow for a decade. Terraces, drained fields and household gardens supplemented it; cacao beans served as money (a turkey cost 100 beans), while jade, quetzal feathers, Spondylus shell and obsidian marked rank (Britannica).

Women ruled as queens and regents — Lady Sak Kʼukʼ crowned her son Pakal in 615; Lady Six Sky of Naranjo commanded wars — though daily law favoured men. Captives became slaves; debtors and criminals joined them. Children were named in bloodletting rites, teeth filed and heads bound to slope nobly. Markets, sweatbaths, ballcourts and causeways bound city to farm. Full treatment — houses, food, dress, marriage, childhood, markets, law — in Vol. 8: Society & Daily Life.

VI. Religion & Mythology

The Maya godscape was calendrical and agricultural. Old creator Itzamna, rain-god Chaahk with his lightning axe, the sun god Kʼinich Ajaw, the maize god Nal (whom kings embodied in dance), the moon goddess Ix Chel, and the Hero Twins Hunahpu and Xbalanque of the Popol Vuh — who outwitted the lords of death in the ballcourt of Xibalbá — head a pantheon of hundreds. Kings fed them with their own blood, drawn from tongue, ear and foreskin onto bark paper and burned so the smoke conjured the Vision Serpent; captives were beheaded atop pyramids and their hearts offered to the sun (WHE, “Maya Religion”).

The ballgame (pitz) was cosmology played with a solid rubber ball struck only with hips and shoulders; stone yokes, panels at Chichén Itzá and the Popol Vuh agree that losing could mean decapitation — death that fed the gods and guaranteed the rains. Burials placed kings in vaulted tombs with jade masks, vessels and sacrificed companions for the nine-level descent. Full pantheon, epic, ritual and afterlife in Vol. 7: Religion & Mythology.

VII. Art, Architecture & Engineering

Each city built in its own voice. Tikal raised six temple-pyramids over the Great Plaza — Temple I (47 m, tomb of Jasaw) and Temple IV (70 m, tallest ancient American building) with giant roof-combs visible 30 km. Calakmulʼs Structure II (55 m) is a mountain of twenty construction phases. Palenque stuccoed its pyramids white and threaded the Otolum stream in an aqueduct beneath its plazas. Copán carved the Hieroglyphic Stairway — 2,200 glyphs, the longest Maya text — and buried the painted Rosalila temple whole inside a larger pyramid. Yaxchilán set carved door-lintels over the Usumacinta; Uxmalʼs Governorʼs Palace wears a 100-metre mosaic of 20,000 stones; Chichén Itzáʼs Kukulkán pyramid casts equinox serpent shadows down its stair (WHE).

Sculpture (stelae portraits, altar tableaux, lintel narratives), painted vases telling myths sip by sip, jade masks and bark-paper books complete the corpus. Reservoirs, causeways, terraces and corbelled vaults — the Maya never used the true arch — show engineers matching artists. Full city-by-city survey in Vol. 10: Art, Architecture & Warfare.

VIII. Writing, Calendar & Science

Maya scribes wrote with 800+ signs — logograms and syllables combined — painted on vases, carved on jade and bone, and inked into screenfold codices of fig-bark paper. Bishop Diego de Landa burned “many books” at Maní in 1562; only four survive (Dresden, Madrid, Paris, Grolier), yet the stone and ceramic corpus plus the 1980s–2000s decipherment breakthroughs (Knorozov, Proskouriakoff, Schele, Stuart) now let scholars read some ninety percent of texts (WHE, “Maya Writing”).

The Long Count counted days from 11 August 3114 BC through kins, uinals, tuns, katuns and baktuns; the 260-day Tzolkʼin meshed with the 365-day Haabʼ every 52 years in the Calendar Round that fixed every ritual. Dresden Codex eclipse tables predict eclipses to the day; the Caracol tracked Venusʼs 584-day cycle to hours over centuries; E-Groups and the Chichén Caracol aligned solstices and zenith passages. Mathematics used dot-bar numerals with shell zero in base twenty — sufficient to date creation itself. Full decipherment story and astronomy in Vol. 9.
