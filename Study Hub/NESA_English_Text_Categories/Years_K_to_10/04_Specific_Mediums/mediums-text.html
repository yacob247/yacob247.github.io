What Is a ‘Medium’ and Why It Matters

A medium is the material channel through which a text travels — the body, the page, the air, the screen. The same words change their meaning and power depending on the medium. “You’re fired” feels different as a voice mail, an email, a broadcast headline, a meme or a courtroom address — not because the words differ, but because the medium carries tone, permanence, reach and context. In English, students learn to see the medium itself as part of the meaning, not as an invisible wrapper.

The curriculum groups mediums as spoken, print, visual, media, multimedia and digital. These are not airtight boxes — a podcast is spoken and digital, a picture book is print and visual — but naming the medium focuses our attention on the affordances (what it can do) and constraints (what it cannot do) of each channel, and on the sign systems each one uses.

The Mediums in the NSW K–10 Syllabus

Throughout the NSW English K–10 Syllabus, students are required to “experience and compose texts using” a range of mediums, audiences and purposes. The syllabus explicitly names spoken texts, print texts, visual texts, media texts, multimedia and digital texts. It also expects students to recognise how “the meaning of a text depends on its medium” and to make deliberate choices of medium to suit purpose and audience. The Australian Curriculum: English similarly describes learning across written, spoken, visual, multimodal and digital modes.

In practice this means: a student must be able to respond to a spoken announcement, a board game, a news report, an infographic, a picture book and a website — and to compose in spoken, written, visual and digital forms. The higher stages expect students to combine them knowingly (a website with print, image, sound and interactivity) and to reflect on why the combination works.

Two points classroom teachers should stress. First, most real texts straddle mediums (this page is print and digital). Second, the medium is a choice with consequences — a topic chosen to be a TikTok is a different text from one chosen to be a 4000-word essay, because the medium chose the audience and depth more than the writer did.

The ‘Grammar’ of Each Medium

Just as sentences have grammar, each medium has a visual/audio ‘grammar’ that produces meaning. Teaching students to name it turns vague impressions into precise analysis:

  • Spoken grammar: volume, pace, pause, pitch, emphasis, silence; plus eye contact, gesture, posture and facial expression (kinesics). A pause can be the most persuasive sentence in a speech.
  • Print grammar: sentence and paragraph structure, typography (font, size, weight), headings, white space, layout and lists. A headline in 72-pt black is a different claim from the same words in 10-pt grey.
  • Visual grammar: colour, line, shape, framing, balance, focal point, perspective, symbol. In an infographic, the size of a circle is doing the arithmetic; in a photograph, the camera angle is the writer’s voice.
  • Media grammar: shot type (close-up, wide), camera angle, lighting, editing, music, sound effects, narration and the “grammar of film” — how cuts and transitions carry time and emotion.
  • Multimedia & digital grammar: navigation, hyperlinks, menu, buttons, scroll, animation, interactivity, user path. In a website, the structure of the links is part of the argument: what is one click away versus buried?

Multimedia & Digital: The Hybrid Mediums

Multimedia and digital texts deserve special attention because they stack several grammars at once. A single webpage might combine print (headline), visual (photo and graph), media (embedded video) and digital (interactive map and share buttons). This stacking is powerful but also where analytical discipline matters most: a strong response does not list everything present; it explains how the elements cooperate — the video shows the problem, the graph quantifies it, the button invites the reader to act.

Digital texts also change the relationship with the audience. They are interactive and networked: readers become users, and meaning can be altered by the reader’s path through links. This is why digital literacy is part of English — students must evaluate reliability, recognise algorithms, understand echo chambers and compose responsibly. A claim on a blog is not automatically a fact because it is online; the medium demands the same critical reading as any other.

A Routine for Analysing Any Medium

Give students one repeatable routine so they can approach any medium with confidence. The M–M–A routine:

  1. Medium first. Name the medium (or mediums) and its affordances and constraints.
  2. Meaning makers (M). Identify the sign systems at work — words, image, sound, layout, interactivity — and the key features of each grammar.
  3. Analysis (A). Explain how each feature produces meaning, for which audience, and whether the medium serves the composer’s purpose — or works against it.

The difference between describing and analysing is the difference between “the photo shows a beach” and “the saturated colour and low camera angle present the beach as a place of safety and abundance, which is what sells the holiday.” Always push for the why and the for whom.

Worked Example, Band 6 Sample & Annotation

Below is a short but complete band-6-style analysis of a multimodal digital text — a charity’s (fictional) donation landing page — showing how a top student reads across mediums and explains cooperation.

Model analysis — “Read the Landing Page”

This single screen is a media text disguised as a print one. Moving top to bottom, it builds a case in the digital grammar of the scroll. The lead is a full-width photograph — a child in soft golden light, shot at eye-level so she is our equal, not our pity — and the medium-level caption does not plead but reports: “88 cents of every dollar feeds a child in your region.” Twenty words carry the whole logos: the exact figure borrows credibility the eye cannot verify, which is precisely the point. The body switches to large, short print sentences — one idea per line, pace set for a thumb, not an eye — and a scrolling donation bar turns the reader into a participant, the interactivity the paper leaflet cannot offer. Cooperation is the engine: the photograph supplies pathos, the statistic supplies logos, and the button converts both into action. Medium, message and mode are one design.

Composing Across Mediums

Composing is where medium-sense becomes real. A strong process asks students to make the choice of medium first, as a design decision:

  • Match medium to message. Ask: who is my audience, what do I want them to feel/know/do, and which channel reaches them best? A safety warning may live forever on a printed sign and second by second as a spoken announcement; both are right for different moments.
  • Use the grammar. Choose typefaces and spacing for print, pace and pause for speech, framing and light for images, links and interactivity for digital.
  • Write for the scroll/ear/eye. A webpage has different rhythm from an essay; a spoken text must be designed to be heard once.
  • Reflect on the choice. Top students can justify why this medium and what it can do that the alternatives cannot.

Encourage multimodal composing early and often — a picture book with a spoken narration, a poster with a QR code, a five-line pitch with one image. The goal is students who see the medium as a creative tool, not an afterthought.

Exam-Style Questions & Model Responses

These questions and models show how reading across mediums is assessed — the top response always names the medium, identifies its grammar, and explains how the elements produce meaning for an audience.

Question 1 (8 marks · ~15 minutes)

“The medium is as much a part of the message as the words.” Discuss with reference to one text you have studied.

Model response

The claim holds fully: a text’s medium is not a neutral wrapper but a set of affordances that shape what can be said and how it is received. In the studied text — a print poster carrying a web address — the choice of medium is the argument itself. A poster can persist, be pasted on a noticeboard and read at a glance, so the composer chooses bold heading and a single image for instant impact; but the inclusion of a QR code admits the print’s limit, that it cannot carry the full story or accept the reader’s response. The medium therefore decides both the reach and the depth of the message, and the careful combining of print and digital shows a composer thinking about what each channel can do rather than about words alone.

Question 2 (10 marks · ~20 minutes)

Compose a short multimodal text — for example a poster, a slide, or a short scripted announcement — on a topic of your choice. Then explain how your choice of medium and one element of its grammar achieves your purpose.

Model response

Composition: My poster urges students to join the walking-bus for safety and fresh air. A large photograph of a bright line of students crossing together leads the eye; a three-word headline, “Walk together, safely,” keeps the print to one breath; a small map at the base shows the route at a glance; at the bottom, a QR code links to the sign-up form. Reflection: I chose a photo over drawn art because real faces build the trust a scheme needs, and I used a low saturated, cheerful colour to signal that safety is not dreary. The map is the grammar that matters most — it converts an abstract promise (“it’s convenient”) into a visible route the reader can picture themselves walking. Medium and grammar, in this case, are the whole persuasion.

Case Study: One Message, Three Mediums

Nothing teaches the power of medium more sharply than holding it still while the medium changes. Take a single message — “the school bus departs at 3:15, five minutes earlier than usual, this Friday” — and imagine it travelling through three channels.

As a spoken announcement, the message exists only for the moment of its saying, so its strength (immediacy, a warm voice to soften the disruption) is also its weakness (no record — anyone who sneezed misses it). A good speaker repeats the key time, slows across “five minutes earlier,” and invites a question. The quality of the voice is the quality of the message.

As a printed note, the message is permanent and precise: the time can be set in bold, the date isolated on its own line. But print cannot warm or repeat itself, so the writer must build emphasis into layout — a bigger type, a space before the changed time — and must assume the reader will read once. The reader is now in control, scanning rather than listening, and the page must guide the eye.

As a digital push notification, the message is immediate, personal and networked: it reaches the reader’s pocket, can link to a calendar or map, and can be updated if plans change. Yet its brevity can flatten nuance, and its permanent pinging can fatigue. The composer gains reach but must earn attention, fitting the essential fact into a line and letting a link carry the rest.

Studying the same sentence across these mediums reveals the truth the whole pack rests on: the meaning is not identical in all three. Immediacy, permanence, precision, reach and warmth are distributed differently in each channel, and a composer who understands that distribution can choose the medium that best serves the reader for the moment. That judgement — matching medium to message, audience and moment — is the heart of effective multimodal composition.
