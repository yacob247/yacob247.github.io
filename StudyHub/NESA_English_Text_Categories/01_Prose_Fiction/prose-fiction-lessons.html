10-Lesson Prose Fiction Program

Ten exam-ready lessons — from narrative technique to Band 6 essay writing — each with teaching content, a stimulus, an exam-style question, starter sentences, a Band 6 sample answer, annotation and self-check criteria.

Lesson 1 · 55 min

The Six Elements & How They Interact

Intended learning: name the six core elements of prose fiction and explain how they interact to make meaning.

Teaching content

Every novel and short story is built from six interconnected elements: plot, character, setting, point of view, language, and theme. The HSC-relevant move is to see them as interacting systems rather than separate boxes. Character choices shape plot; point of view controls how we see setting; language colours theme. Reading prose analytically means tracing these links and being able to write about them under exam pressure.

Anchor this with a familiar opening: in almost any narrative, the first page establishes a character in a situation through a point of view and a set of details chosen to signal a theme — and the plot is what happens because the character wants something. Top students describe the machinery, not just the story.

Stimulus: the opening lines of a classic novel (e.g. The Great Gatsby — “In my younger and more vulnerable years…”) or your class novel’s first paragraph.
Question (8 marks · ~15 min): Using the opening passage, explain how at least three of the six elements already work together to create meaning.

Starter sentences

“The opening establishes…” · “Through the point of view of…, the reader is positioned to see…” · “This detail foreshadows…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

The opening welds character, point of view and setting into one engine of meaning. The first-person narrator presents himself as wounded but wiser, and that retrospective vantage point colours every event he describes — we trust his regret as a filter rather than a fact. The setting he describes glitters precisely because his tone is already nostalgic, so place becomes an extension of feeling. These three elements cooperate before any plot occurs, and the theme — the cost of longing for a past self — is present in the grammar of the very first sentence.

Names three elementsSelection and synthesisShows the markers of prose analysis: fewer elements, deeper links, not a checklist.

  • I named at least three elements.
  • I explained how they interact.
  • I quoted or pinpointed the text.
  • I linked it to a theme or response.

Worksheet — Lesson 1

Take one page of your novel. In a table, note: one detail of setting, one of character, one technique of point of view, and one line of language — then write one sentence of synthesis, and one theme word it points to.

Lesson 2 · 55 min

Characterisation & Voice

Intended learning: analyse how character is built through action, speech, reaction and choice, and how a distinctive voice emerges.

Teaching content

Characters are revealed, not stated. Teach the four channels of characterisation: what the character does (action), what they say (dialogue), how others react, and the choices they make under pressure. Also teach the distinction between flat and round and between static and dynamic characters — and that characters are the vehicle for theme: what the protagonist wants and fears is usually what the novel wants to explore.

Band 6 responses do not write “the character is kind”; they trace the evidence — a refused act, a line of dialogue, a decisive choice — and tie it to the effect on the reader.

Stimulus: a short scene from your novel where a character makes a revealing choice, or a character-in-action passage you supply.
Question (8 marks · ~15 min): Using two channels of characterisation, analyse what the character’s choices reveal, and connect it to theme.

Starter sentences

“What is most revealing is not what the character says but what she does when…” · “The choice to… exposes…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

Character emerges most sharply in the gap between speech and action. The protagonist protests that he feels nothing, yet the passage shows him sorting his father’s tools — not to use them, but to keep them polished, an act the narrator himself refuses to name. That contradiction is the point: his choices argue against his words, and the reader sees grief in what he does rather than what he says. Characterisation through this gap makes the emotion feel earned, and it quietly advances the novel’s theme that love survives even when it is unspoken.

Annotation

FeatureEffect
“gap between speech and action”Identifies the subtextual channel — analysis, not retelling.
“polished but not used”Uses a precise textual detail as evidence.
“theme that love survives unspoken”Links characterisation to the novel’s argument.
  • I used two channels of characterisation.
  • I quoted or pinpointed a choice.
  • I linked the character to theme.

Worksheet — Lesson 2

Find one moment where a character contradicts themselves; write a 150-word analysis of what the gap reveals.

Lesson 3 · 55 min

Point of View & Narration

Intended learning: analyse how point of view and narrative reliability create meaning and position the reader.

Teaching content

Point of view decides who tells and what we are allowed to know. Teach the modes: first person (subjective, close), third-person limited (inside one mind), third-person omniscient (god-like overview), and the rarer second person (thrusting the reader in). Then go deeper to the HSC-rewarding idea of unreliable narration — a narrator whose view we cannot fully trust — and ask what an unreliable narrator reveals about memory, bias or self-deception.

Stimulus: two short versions of the same event — one told in first person, one in third-person omniscient — or an unreliable-narrator passage from your text.
Question (8 marks · ~15 min): Analyse how point of view shapes what the reader knows and how far we trust the telling.

Starter sentences

“The first-person narration narrows our field of vision to…” · “Because the narrator is unreliable, we read his claims as…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

The first-person narration is the novel’s engine, because it makes every objective fact travel through a subjective mind. We know only what the narrator chooses to notice, and his self-image is at stake in every telling — so when he offers a flattering version of his own actions, the gap between the events and his account is where the real meaning sits. An omniscient narrator could have told us the truth plainly; limiting point of view instead forces us to deduce it, making the reader co-author and the theme of self-deception structure the entire book.

  • I named the point of view.
  • I explained its limits and effects.
  • I addressed reliability where relevant.

Worksheet — Lesson 3

Rewrite a short scene from your novel in a different point of view and note two ways the meaning changes.

Lesson 4 · 55 min

Setting, Symbol & Mood

Intended learning: read setting for mood and symbolism, and connect place to meaning.

Teaching content

Setting is never neutral. Teach three layers: physical (where/when), social (class, culture, power), and symbolic (what the place comes to mean). Then link to mood — the feeling the setting produces — achieved through sensory detail, light, weather and objects. A house can be a character; a road can be a fate. Band 6 writing shows how place is meaning rather than a backdrop.

Stimulus: a descriptive passage from your novel where an ordinary or symbolic space is loaded with feeling.
Question (8 marks · ~15 min): Analyse how the writer uses setting to create mood and symbolism, and connect it to theme.

Starter sentences

“The house/room/street functions as…” · “By loading this space with…, the writer makes place stand for…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

Setting here is symbolic before it is physical. The overgrown garden is described in feverish, drowning detail — heat, tangled weeds, a broken fence — so that it comes to stand for the family’s neglect; the place is the damage. Because the mood is sustained through every sense, the reader does not merely see the ruin but feels it, and when a single bright flower is later described, its small hope gains the force of an event. Setting, charged with symbol, becomes the novel’s quiet argument about what is ignored and what survives.

  • I distinguished physical, social or symbolic layers.
  • I tied sensory detail to mood.
  • I connected setting to theme.

Worksheet — Lesson 4

Find one place in your novel that carries symbolic weight; list three sensory details and state what the place stands for.

Lesson 5 · 55 min

Language & Imagery in Prose

Intended learning: analyse figurative language and imagery for their effect, and connect them to theme.

Teaching content

Prose means through language chosen with intent. Teach the toolkit — imagery, simile, metaphor, personification, symbol, motif, irony and sentence rhythm — and the disciplined habit of asking two questions: what image does this create? and what feeling or idea does it push toward? A motif (a repeated image) is especially powerful in novels: tracking a recurring image across the text reveals the theme accumulating.

Stimulus: a lyrical or image-heavy passage from your novel, or a passage rich in a recurring motif.
Question (8 marks · ~15 min): Analyse how one image or motif is developed and what it contributes to theme.

Starter sentences

“The image of… recurs at…, and each return deepens it into…” · “By comparing… to…, the writer makes the reader feel…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

Language here does not decorate the idea; it is the idea. The recurring image of water — a leaking tap, a rain-blind window, finally a flood — is developed so that each appearance adds a layer: what begins as domestic annoyance becomes, by the final chapter, an image of feeling the protagonist can no longer contain. Because the motif gathers meaning through repetition, the theme of suppressed grief is dramatised rather than stated, and the reader experiences the rising tide of emotion with the character.

  • I named the technique precisely.
  • I quoted or pinpointed the image.
  • I explained its effect on the reader.
  • I connected it to theme.

Worksheet — Lesson 5

Track one motif through three moments in your novel; write a sentence on each appearance and a synthesis sentence.

Lesson 6 · 55 min

Structure, Pacing & Genre

Intended learning: analyse the sequence of chapters/scenes, use of time, and genre expectations as tools of meaning.

Teaching content

Structure is the writer’s arrangement of time and focus. Teach the moves that shape meaning: chronological vs non-linear (flashbacks, foreshadowing, in medias res), ellipsis (what the author skips), pacing (long scenes that dwell, short scenes that rush), and parallelism (paired scenes that comment on each other). Then add genre — the set of expectations a reader brings — and how a novel fulfils, subverts or reworks its genre to say something new.

Stimulus: the chapter arrangement of your novel — its opening and closing chapters, and one structural choice (a flashback, a gap, a parallel).
Question (8 marks · ~15 min): Analyse how a structural choice shapes meaning or engages genre expectations.

Starter sentences

“By opening in medias res…” · “The ellipsis between chapters implies…” · “Subverting the genre expectation that…, the novel…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

The novel’s gamble is structural. It opens at the end — the crime already committed — and then rewinds, so the reader reads not to learn what happened but to understand why, which changes every scene into explanation. The ellipsis between the two halves skips a decade in a paragraph, quietly insisting that what matters is not the years but the act of reckoning that binds them. By borrowing the detective genre’s mystery structure and refusing its solution, the novel reworks genre into a meditation on memory and guilt.

  • I named the structural technique.
  • I explained its effect on reading.
  • I addressed genre where relevant.

Worksheet — Lesson 6

Map your novel’s structure (linear/non-linear, key scenes, gaps); note one genre expectation it meets or subverts.

Lesson 7 · 55 min

Reading for Theme & Argument

Intended learning: move from “what happens” to a defensible statement of what the novel argues.

Teaching content

The biggest leap in senior analysis is moving from events to argument. A theme is not a single word (“power”) but a claim the novel keeps making (“power, when unexamined, corrupts its holder”). Teach students to build theme from evidence: a recurring pattern, a decisive image, the arc a character travels. The best thesis is contestable and specific, and every paragraph should serve it.

Stimulus: the ending of your novel, or two scenes that together point to its central idea.
Question (8 marks · ~15 min): State your novel’s central argument as a specific thesis, and support it with two pieces of evidence.

Starter sentences

“Through the trajectory of…, the novel argues that…” · “Far from…, the text insists…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

The novel does not simply depict ambition; it argues that ambition, when severed from love, becomes a kind of blindness. This claim is dramatised in two decisions: the protagonist’s choice to abandon his friend at the threshold of opportunity, and his later silent profiting from that abandonment. The first scene establishes the cost; the second shows the ambition dying of its own success. Together they give the reader a defensible thesis that goes beyond “ambition is bad” — it names the mechanism, which is what a Band 6 reading must do.

  • My thesis is specific and contestable.
  • I supported it with two pieces of evidence.
  • I connected the argument to technique.

Worksheet — Lesson 7

Write three candidate thesis statements for your novel; choose the strongest and list two supporting moments for each.

Lesson 8 · 55 min

Writing the Topic Sentence & TEEL Analysis

Intended learning: build coherent analytical paragraphs with a strong topic sentence and integrated evidence.

Teaching content

Analysis lives in the paragraph. Teach the TEEL structure — Topic sentence (the point), Evidence (a short quote or detail), Explanation (what the technique does and how it supports the thesis), Link (back to the thesis or forward to the next point). The topic sentence should be a mini-argument, not a topic label. Evidence should be woven in, not dumped, and the explanation is where the marks are.

Stimulus: a sample topic sentence (“In portraying the town’s silence as an active force, the novel argues that complicity is not passive but chosen”) and one quote to support it.
Question (10 marks · ~20 min): Write a complete TEEL paragraph from the topic sentence and evidence provided.

Starter sentences

“The author’s craft is evident in…” · “This matters because…” · “Consequently…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

Topic: In portraying the town’s silence as an active force, the novel argues that complicity is chosen rather than passive. Evidence: When the crowd “turns, as one body, and looks away,” the verb transforms a gesture into a decision. Explanation: By making the turning a collective, almost choreographed act, the writer shows silence being performed — each person actively choosing not to see — so that guilt becomes something done, not merely omitted. Link: This is why the novel refuses easy villains: it indicts the whole town, and thereby indicts the reader’s own capacity to look away.

  • My topic sentence argues, not just labels.
  • My evidence is short and woven in.
  • My explanation analyses effect.
  • My link returns to the thesis.

Worksheet — Lesson 8

Write a TEEL paragraph on a moment of your choice from your novel.

Lesson 9 · 60 min

Composing a Band 6 Essay

Intended learning: plan and draft a sustained analytical essay under exam-style conditions.

Teaching content

Bring the skills together into a timed essay. Teach the plan-first routine: unpack the question (what is it really asking?), write a contention that answers it directly, choose three body points from your evidence bank, and plan an introduction (hook + contention + roadmap) and a conclusion that returns to the hook. In a ~50-minute essay, spend roughly 5 planning, 40 writing, 5 checking.

Stimulus: “Stories can both challenge and confirm our sense of identity and belonging. To what extent do you agree, in relation to your prescribed novel?”
Question (20 marks · ~40 min): Write a sustained essay responding to the stimulus with your studied novel.

Starter sentences

“While identity is affirmed by…, the novel’s greater achievement is to…” · “This contention, explored through…”

Band 6 sample (introduction + one body paragraph)

Model response

Introduction: A novel does not simply mirror identity; it actively negotiates it. In [novel], the writer both confirms and unsettles its protagonists’ sense of belonging — affirming the comfort of home even as it exposes belonging as fragile, contingent and hard-won. Body: The novel confirms belonging most powerfully in its rendering of place, where the house is invested with memory until it seems to hold the family together permanently. Yet this comfort is deliberately undermined: the recurring image of the door left open lets in not only the world but the possibility of leaving, so that the very symbol of home becomes a question. Through this technique, the novel argues that identity is less a fixed inheritance than a practice — something its characters must actively rebuild, and rebuild again.

  • My contention directly answers the question.
  • My paragraphs connect thesis, evidence and effect.
  • I used an integrated structure and sustained argument.
  • I moved beyond retelling into interpretation.

Worksheet — Lesson 9

Complete your plan, then draft the full essay under timed conditions.

Lesson 10 · 55 min

Editing, Common Errors & Self-Assessment

Intended learning: edit an essay against criteria, correct common errors, and self-assess to Band 6.

Teaching content

Editing is where Band 6 is secured. Teach the three-pass edit: Argument — does each paragraph serve the thesis? Evidence & technique — does each quote have explanation, not just presence? Expression — is the tense consistent (always present), the voice confident, the prose precise? Then run the common-errors checklist: plot summary, “technique spotting,” unsupported claims, and over-quoting.

Stimulus: a draft essay paragraph that is heavy on summary and light on explanation.
Question (10 marks · ~20 min): Rewrite the paragraph to reduce summary and deepen analysis, and justify one change.

Band 6 sample

Model response

Original told the plot and named a technique without effect: “The author uses a metaphor here.” Revised: “The metaphor of the unlit house does not merely describe; it condemns. By choosing an image of a home never switched on, the writer dramatises a family who exist together without ever being present to one another — so the reader is made to feel their isolation before it is ever stated.” I cut the retelling, justified the metaphor through its effect, and let a single image carry the paragraph’s weight — the difference between summary and analysis.

  • I reduced summary and deepened analysis.
  • I corrected tense/register issues.
  • I justified at least one change.
  • My evidence serves the thesis.

Final self-assessment

Mark your full essay against the markers in the guide and set one goal for the next essay.

Assessment guidance: align to the NESA Stage 6 English marking guidelines for Advanced/Standard — reward a sustained personal response, precise textual reference, integrated technique analysis, and coherent expression. Confirm current requirements at NESA Stage 6 English.
