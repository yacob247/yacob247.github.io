The Complete Guide to Prose Fiction Analysis

A comprehensive course in analysing novels and short stories — from the six core elements to writing Band 6 responses with annotated sample analyses.

How to use this guide: Work through each element systematically. Learn to identify techniques, analyse their effects, and connect them to themes. Practice with the 10-Lesson Program for exam preparation.

The Six Core Elements of Prose Fiction

Every piece of prose fiction is built from six interconnected elements. Mastery means understanding how authors manipulate each one to create meaning.

Element 1

PLOT

Plot is the sequence of events that make up a narrative — not just what happens, but HOW events are arranged and revealed.

  • Traditional structure: Exposition → rising action → climax → falling action → resolution
  • Non-linear plot: flashbacks, flash-forwards, fragmented timelines
  • Pacing and tension: how quickly or slowly the story unfolds
  • Conflict: character vs. self, vs. others, vs. society, vs. nature
Element 2

CHARACTER

Character refers to the people (or beings) in the story. Analyse how they are developed and what they represent.

  • Protagonist, antagonist, and foil characters
  • Round vs. flat; dynamic vs. static characters
  • Direct characterisation (authorial description) vs. indirect characterisation (revealed through action, speech, thought)
  • Character motivation and development arcs
Element 3

SETTING

Setting encompasses time, place, and social context. It shapes mood, influences characters, and often reinforces themes.

  • Physical setting: location, environment, sensory detail
  • Temporal setting: historical period, season, time of day
  • Social setting: class structures, cultural norms, power dynamics
  • Atmosphere / mood: the emotional tone created by place and time
Element 4

THEME

Theme is the central idea or message explored in the text. Great works contain multiple interwoven themes.

  • Universal themes: love, death, identity, power, justice, freedom
  • Text-specific themes: unique combinations that address particular concerns
  • Explicit vs. implicit themes — stated directly or suggested through subtext
  • Thematic development throughout the narrative
Element 5

NARRATIVE VOICE

Narrative voice determines who tells the story and from what perspective.

  • First person (“I”) — limited, potentially unreliable
  • Third person limited — follows one character’s thoughts
  • Third person omniscient — access to all minds
  • Unreliable narrator — compromised credibility
  • Voice and tone — the distinctive attitude of the narration
Element 6

STYLE & LANGUAGE

Style encompasses the author's distinctive use of language and literary devices.

  • Diction (word choice), syntax (sentence structure)
  • Imagery, metaphor, simile, symbolism, irony, allusion
  • Motifs — recurring elements that reinforce theme
  • Register (formal/colloquial) and tone

How to Analyse Prose Fiction: A Step-by-Step Method

Step 1: First Reading — Experience the Text

Read for overall understanding. Note your emotional responses and first impressions. Let the story work on you before you begin to dissect it.

Step 2: Second Reading — Identify Techniques

Read with a pen. Mark examples of key plot turns, significant character moments, recurring symbols, striking language, and tonal or perspective shifts.

Step 3: Third Reading — Analyse Effects

For each technique ask: what effect does this create? How does it build theme? Why did the author choose it? How would the text differ without it?

Step 4: Synthesis — Build Arguments

Connect observations into coherent arguments about meaning and craft, always supporting every claim with specific textual evidence.

Band 6 Sample Analysis

Below is an excerpt from a Band 6 analysis of George Orwell’s 1984, showing how to integrate evidence with sophisticated commentary.

THESIS

George Orwell’s dystopian novel 1984 presents a harrowing vision of totalitarian control, using Winston Smith’s psychological deterioration to expose the mechanisms by which authoritarian regimes destroy individual thought and human connection.

Strong opening: A clear thesis identifying both the subject (totalitarian control) and the how (through Winston’s journey). Names author, text, and argument immediately.
TECHNIQUE + EVIDENCE

Orwell employs the unreliable first-person perspective to immerse readers in Winston’s fractured reality. The opening line — “It was a bright cold day in April, and the clocks were striking thirteen” — immediately establishes a world where even time itself has been corrupted; the anomalous “thirteen” signals that fundamental truths have been altered under Party rule.

Excellent integration: Names the technique, gives specific evidence (opening quote), and explains its significance. This is the evidence–analysis balance examiners reward.
THEMATIC DEVELOPMENT

The motif of surveillance permeates the text, culminating in the omnipresent Big Brother. Orwell writes that “the telescreen received and transmitted simultaneously,” capturing the dual nature of Party control: citizens are both watched and manipulated. This technological panopticon ensures that privacy — and therefore independent thought — becomes impossible.

Deep analysis: Moves beyond observation to broader implications. Uses the concept of the “panopticon” to show wider critical knowledge while staying focused on textual evidence.
CONCLUSION WITH INSIGHT

Through Winston’s final betrayal of Julia and his ultimate love of Big Brother, Orwell delivers his most devastating insight: totalitarianism’s greatest triumph is not forcing compliance, but convincing people to embrace their own destruction. The horror lies not in torture alone but in the annihilation of the self.

Sophisticated conclusion: Offers original insight beyond summary, connecting back to the thesis while adding new depth. This is Band 6 thinking.

What Makes This Band 6?

  • Precise terminology: “unreliable first-person perspective,” “motif,” “panopticon”
  • Seamless quotes: evidence woven naturally into sentences
  • Analysis over description: every quote is explained for its effect
  • Thematic coherence: all points serve the central argument
  • Original insight: goes beyond the obvious
  • Confident voice: no hedging or uncertainty

Common Errors to Avoid

  • Plot summary: retelling the story instead of analysing it
  • Technique spotting: naming devices without explaining purpose
  • Unsupported claims: assertions without textual evidence
  • Biographical fallacy: confusing the author’s intention with textual meaning
  • Tense errors: always use present tense (“Winston thinks,” not “thought”)
  • Over-quoting: letting the citation list the work instead of your analysis
