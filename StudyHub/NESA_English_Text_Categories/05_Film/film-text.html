The Complete Guide to Film

Understanding the language of cinema — shot, sequence, sound and story — and how to write a nuanced film analysis.

How to use this guide: Learn the elements of film and a reliable method for analysing a scene. Apply it in the 10-Lesson Program.

Camera and the Shot

Camera work is the grammar of film. Key choices include:

  • Framing: the long shot (setting), medium shot (relationship), close-up (emotion), and extreme close-up (detail)
  • Angle: high angle (diminish), low angle (power), eye-level (neutral), canted (disequilibrium)
  • Movement: pan, tilt, dolly, tracking, handheld (energy), and crane (overview)
  • Focus: shallow (isolate), deep (connect), racking focus (redirect attention)
  • Point of view: subjective camerawork that aligns the audience with a character

Mise-en-Scène

Everything placed in front of the camera: setting, props, costume, lighting, colour and blocking. These create mood and symbolism — for example, a single source of hard light can suggest entrapment, while warm colour palettes suggest safety.

Editing

Editing joins shots into meaning:

  • Continuity editing: invisible cuts that sustain realism
  • Montage: compression of time and an idea through rapid image
  • Cut, fade, dissolve: each implies a different passage of time or tone
  • Pacing: beat frequency controls energy and suspense
  • Juxtaposition: the collision of two shots creates a third meaning

Sound and Music

  • Diegetic sound: sound the characters hear (dialogue, ambient noise)
  • Non-diegetic sound: soundtrack and score heard by the audience alone
  • Silence: a powerful device that foregrounds tension
  • Sound bridges: audio that links scenes and time

How to Analyse a Scene

  1. Shots: list the framing, angle and movement of each shot
  2. Mise-en-scène: what is in frame and what it means
  3. Editing: how cuts order the shot and control pace
  4. Sound: music, effects and silence and their effect
  5. Purpose: how all elements combine to position the audience toward a theme
