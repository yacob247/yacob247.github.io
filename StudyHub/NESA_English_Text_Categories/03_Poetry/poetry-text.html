The Complete Guide to Poetry

A practical course in reading, analysing and writing poetry — from key terminology to Band 6 close analysis.

How to use this guide: Begin with terminology, then form, then figurative language. End with context and writing your own verse. Practise with the 10-Lesson Program.

Essential Terminology

TermDefinition
StanzaA group of lines forming a unit in a poem
MetreThe rhythmic pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables
Iambic pentameterFive beats per line, each an unstressed-stressed pair
Rhyme schemeThe pattern of end rhyme, e.g. ABAB
EnjambmentWhen a line runs over into the next without pause
CaesuraA pause within a line
VoltaThe ‘turn’ or shift in argument (common in sonnets)

Poetic Forms

  • Sonnet: 14 lines, iambic pentameter, with a volta; Shakespearean (rhyme ABAB/CDCD/EFEF/GG) or Petrarchan (octave + sestet)
  • Villanelle: 19 lines, two refrains and two rhymes
  • Ode: an elevated address in praise of a subject
  • Free verse: no fixed metre or rhyme; structure created by line, punctuation, and white space
  • Spoken word: performance poetry written for the voice

Figurative Language

  • Metaphor / simile: comparing across domains (simile uses ‘like’ or ‘as’)
  • Personification: giving human qualities to the non-human
  • Symbolism: an object standing for an idea
  • Allusion: a reference to another text, event, or figure
  • Irony / paradox: contradiction or surprise that reveals truth

A Method for Analysis

  1. First impression: read aloud; note the feel, mood, and initial subject
  2. Structure: count stanzas and lines; identify the form and its traditions
  3. Sound: metre, rhyme, enjambment, alliteration, assonance
  4. Imagery: collect the senses; track metaphors and their extensions
  5. Voice: who speaks, to whom, and with what tone
  6. Meaning: how all these cohere into theme — and what is left unsaid

Band 6 Close Analysis

Model reads (Robert Frost’s ‘Stopping by Winters’): In the closing stanza Frost reverses expectations with the line “The woods are lovely, dark and deep,” where the trip to ‘lovely’ is held against “dark and deep’. The internal rhyme pulls the sound toward seduction even as the sense warns of danger. The final repetition — “And miles to go before I sleep / distance befoh” — italicises the burden of duty through simple echo. Frost leaves meaning deliberately open: sleep and death are never equated explicitly, only on the reader to complete the metaphor.

Writing Your Own Poetry

  • Begin by imitating a poet you admire (copy the form, change the content)
  • Keep a journal of exact observations — poetry begins in precision
  • Read work aloud; the ear hears what the eye misses
  • Revise relentlessly; cut what is vague or sentimental
