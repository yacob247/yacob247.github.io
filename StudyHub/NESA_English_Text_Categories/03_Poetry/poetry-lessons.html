Lesson 1 · 55 min

Reading a Poem: First Impressions to Analysis

Intended learning: move from an initial response to a disciplined first analysis of any poem.

Teaching content

Poems reward being read aloud and read slowly. Teach the reading sequence: read for sound and feeling first (what do you notice before you understand it?), then for words and lines (which words are stressed or repeated?), then for meaning and structure (what is the poem saying, and how is it shaped?). This layered reading turns an impression into a defensible analysis.

Stimulus: a short poem (e.g. a lyric or sonnet) read aloud twice — once without, once with the text in front of you.
Question (6 marks · ~12 min): Describe your first response, then identify three words or structural choices behind it.

Starter sentences

“My first impression is one of…” · “The repetition of ‘…’ leads me to…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

My first impression is one of stillness edged with threat — a quiet poem that is not peaceful. The clue is in three choices. The word “held” returns twice, but each time it means differently — once as tenderness, once as constraint — so the poem turns the same verb into its own accusation. The long lines slow the reading to a hush, but the single short line near the end breaks that hush like a held breath released. And the poem ends not with a full stop but with an image that refuses to close, “the gate that was always open.” My emotion was there before I could name it, but naming the words that made it is the first act of analysis.

  • I articulated an initial response.
  • I identified specific choices behind it.
  • I connected feeling to craft.

Worksheet — Lesson 1

Read a new poem twice; write your first response, then three craft observations.

Lesson 2 · 55 min

Form & Structure

Intended learning: identify poetic forms and analyse how structure — stanzas, lineation and shape — makes meaning.

Teaching content

Form is meaning. Teach the common frames (sonnet, haiku, ballad, free verse, concrete/shape poems) and the structural toolbox: stanzas as paragraphs of feeling, line breaks as rhythm and emphasis, enjambment (running over) versus end-stopped lines, and the volta (turn) that shifts a poem. A poem’s shape is a choice — ask why.

Stimulus: two short poems or drafts on the same idea — one in tight form, one in free verse.
Question (8 marks · ~15 min): Analyse how the form and structure of one poem contribute to its effect.

Starter sentences

“The sonnet’s volta…” · “By enjambing these lines, the poet…” · “The irregular line length suggests…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

The poem’s form is part of its argument. It is a sonnet, but the poet keeps the traditional rhyme scheme only through the octave before letting it dissolve in the sestet — a structural mirror of the poem’s theme of order giving way to feeling. The line breaks work the same way: the tight end-stopped couplets of the opening, like statements made and closed, become enjambed and breathless toward the end. Form here is not a container; the poem uses the sonnet’s discipline to stage its own loss of discipline. Reading structure is reading this argument.

  • I named the form.
  • I analysed stanzas, lineation or rhyme.
  • I linked structure to meaning.

Worksheet — Lesson 2

Take a free-verse poem, break two lines differently and comment on the change in emphasis.

Lesson 3 · 50 min

Rhythm, Metre & Sound

Intended learning: hear and name the sound machinery of a poem — metre, rhyme, beat and echo — and connect it to meaning.

Teaching content

Poetry is a sound event before it is a page event. Teach the key sound tools: metre (the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables: iambic unstressed–stressed, trochaic stressed–unstressed, spondaic double stress), the line beat (tetrameter, pentameter, hexameter), rhyme (perfect, slant/half-rhyme, internal) and sound repetition — alliteration, assonance, consonance and onomatopoeia. Teach how caesura (a pause inside a line) and enjambment (carrying over) become the two breathing rhythms of a poem. The key exam move is not to name a device for its own sake but to ask what the sound does: a hard spondee jolts attention, a trailing assonance unites ideas, slant rhyme can murmur unease.

Stimulus: a short passage read aloud with deliberate stress — feel where the beat is regular, where it falters, where it snaps.
Question (8 marks · ~15 min): Explain how the poet combines rhythm and sound to create a particular effect in one poem.

Starter sentences

“The regular iambic beat lulls the reader until…” · “The slant rhyme on ‘…’ and ‘…’ voices a discord…” · “Through the caesura, the line stops to…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

The poem makes its argument in sound before it makes it in sense. Its even beat is almost metronomic — a steady iambic line that feels like a task being carried — but at the line where the subject turns from labour to memory the poet snaps the beat with a spondaic halt and a hard caesura: the poem itself stops, as the speaker stops. The slant rhyme of “day” with “stair” refuses the ease of full rhyme, so the ear registers something unfinished where the sense is finished too. Sound here is not decoration; it is the second, more honest narrator of the poem.

  • I named a metre and sound device precisely.
  • I showed sound creating or questioning feeling.
  • I treated rhythm as argument, not garnish.

Worksheet — Lesson 3

Scan a poem (mark / stressed and u unstressed), then write three sentences on how the beat answers the content.

Lesson 4 · 55 min

Imagery & Figurative Language

Intended learning: identify and interpret a poem’s images and the figurative moves that build them.

Teaching content

Imagery is where a poem thinks in the senses. Teach the toolkit: simile and metaphor, the extended metaphor that runs across a poem, personification (human agency given to the non-human), symbol (an object standing for an idea it never states), synaesthesia (blending the senses) and the concern — an elaborate sustained comparison. Ask students to read an image twice: once for what it pictures, once for what it carries. The Band 6 habit is to track an image through a poem rather than listing images; a rose, window or road that changes each time it recurs reveals the poem’s argument about change itself.

Stimulus: a poem built on one central image that the poem revisits in order to extend it.
Question (8 marks · ~15 min): Analyse how the central image of a poem is developed and what it comes to mean.

Starter sentences

“The image begins as… but is extended until…” · “By personifying ‘…’, the poet…” · “The symbol is never named; instead…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

The poem is built on one image carried almost to breaking point. It begins with the sky kept as a “wardrobe” — a container for the weather — but by the second stanza that storage has stopped being a metaphor and become a literal suffocation, the dresser “stuffed with storms.” The turn of the image is the argument: what begins as a comfortable ordering of nature curdles into nature being imprisoned. The poet never steps outside the conceit, and that constraint is the point — we are locked inside the wardrobe too. By following a single image across the poem, a description became a statement.

  • I identified the image and its repeated forms.
  • I traced its development, not just its presence.
  • I showed the image carrying theme.

Worksheet — Lesson 4

Highlight every recurrence of one image in a poem and describe what each occurrence adds to the last.
