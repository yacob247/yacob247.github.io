Lesson 1 · 40 min

What Is Persuasive Writing?

Intended learning: define persuasive writing, identify its purpose, and recognise its key features.

Teaching content

Persuasive writing is a mode of composition whose aim is to convince the reader to accept a position or take an action. It advances a clear contention (thesis) and builds a case through evidence, reasoning and emotional appeal. The writer is an advocate and the text is a case.

Persuasion surrounds us — in speeches, opinion pieces, advertisements, editorials and political commentary. Recognising how the mode works helps you both analyse others' texts and craft your own. In Module C, persuasive writing rewards a clear, sustained and sophisticated argument with strong end-to-end control.

Stimulus: "Everyone has the right to a good education — so why does opportunity still depend on postcode?"
Exam-style question (5 marks · ~8 min): Write a persuasive opening sentence that states a clear contention on the stimulus.

Starter sentences

  • "A child's future should never be decided by the street they grow up on."
  • "We say every child matters — it is time our schools actually proved it."
  • "Educational equality is not a dream; it is a choice we keep refusing to make."

Practice worksheet

Task 1. Define persuasive writing in two sentences.

Task 2. List three features of persuasive writing.

Task 3. Write a clear contention for the stimulus.

Band 6 sample answer

Model contention

Every child deserves the same chance to succeed, yet in Australia that chance is still measured out by postcode — and it is time we treated that inequality as the national emergency it is.

Marking criteria / self-check

  • I defined persuasive writing accurately.
  • I stated a clear, arguable contention.
  • My language is forceful and confident.
  • I kept my focus on convincing the reader.

Lesson 2 · 40 min

Contention & Thesis

Intended learning: craft a clear, arguable contention that anchors a persuasive piece.

Teaching content

The contention is the central claim of a persuasive text — the position the writer sets out to prove. A strong contention is clear (the reader knows exactly where you stand), arguable (it takes a position someone could dispute), and specific (it points to how the argument will unfold). Everything in the piece serves the contention.

Weak contentions are vague ("education is important"), unarguable ("the sky is blue") or too broad ("schools should change"). A Band 6 contention is sharp, confident and grounded.

Stimulus: "Social media has more power for good than harm in young people's lives."
Exam-style question (6 marks · ~10 min): Write a strong contention for this stimulus, then rewrite a weak contention into a strong one.

Starter sentences

  • "For all its dangers, social media remains one of the most powerful tools for connection the young have ever had."
  • "The harms of social media are real — but they are outweighed by the communities it builds."

Practice worksheet

Task 1. Write a contention for the stimulus.

Task 2. Rewrite "education is important" into an arguable contention.

Task 3. State one reason that supports your contention.

Band 6 sample answer

Model contention

Critics point to the dangers of social media, and they are not wrong — but these risks are outweighed when young people use these platforms to build communities, find solidarity and make their voices heard.

Marking criteria / self-check

  • My contention is clear and specific.
  • It is arguable — someone could disagree.
  • It guides the rest of my argument.
  • It is stated confidently.

Lesson 3 · 45 min

Ethos, Pathos & Logos

Intended learning: use the three classical appeals — credibility, emotion and logic — and balance them in a persuasive piece.

Teaching content

Since Aristotle, persuasion has been understood through three appeals. Ethos builds the writer's credibility and trustworthiness; pathos moves the reader emotionally; logos appeals to logic and reason with evidence and reasoning. The most powerful persuasive texts weave all three together — a purely emotional appeal can feel manipulative, while a purely logical one can feel cold.

Stimulus: "Climate change is the greatest challenge of our generation."
Exam-style question (10 marks · ~18 min): Write a persuasive paragraph that uses all three appeals ethically, and label each.

Starter sentences

  • "As the science makes clear, the time for debate is over." (logos)
  • "Picture the world we will leave our children." (pathos)
  • "We are the generation with both the knowledge and the duty to act." (ethos)

Practice worksheet

Task 1. Write one sentence using ethos.

Task 2. Write one sentence using pathos.

Task 3. Write one sentence using logos.

Task 4. Combine all three into one persuasive paragraph.

Band 6 sample answer

Model paragraph

The world's climate scientists — 99% of them — agree that human activity is warming the planet at an unprecedented rate [logos]. This is not a partisan opinion but a conclusion supported by decades of evidence. And yet behind every data point is a child who will inherit the consequences [pathos]. As citizens, we have a responsibility to act on what we know [ethos] — the question is no longer whether climate change is real, but whether we have the courage to answer it.

Marking criteria / self-check

  • I used ethos, pathos and logos.
  • My appeals are ethical, not manipulative.
  • I balanced all three appeals.
  • Each appeal serves the contention.

Lesson 4 · 45 min

Structure of an Argument

Intended learning: organise a persuasive piece into a logical, powerful structure.

Teaching content

A persuasive piece is built to win. Its structure moves the reader from engagement to agreement: an engaging opening with a clear contention, context, a series of supported arguments, a counterargument with refutation, a reinforcement, and an emphatic conclusion that issues a call to action. Each body paragraph makes one point, states it clearly, supports it with evidence, and links it back to the contention.

Stimulus: "All students should complete community service to graduate."
Exam-style question (8 marks · ~15 min): Outline the structure of a persuasive response to this stimulus, listing the contention and three arguments.

Starter sentences

  • "Requiring community service would teach students what no classroom can."
  • "Graduation should mean more than passing tests — it should mean contributing."

Practice worksheet

Task 1. Write the contention.

Task 2. List three arguments, each with one piece of support.

Task 3. State one likely counterargument and how you would refute it.

Band 6 sample answer

Model outline

Contention: Community service should be a graduation requirement because it builds character, community and real-world skill. Arg 1: It develops empathy (support: studies). Arg 2: It strengthens communities (support: examples). Arg 3: It teaches employable skills (support: evidence). Counter: Some students are too busy — but flexible programs address this. Conclusion: Call to require it as a rite of emerging adulthood.

Marking criteria / self-check

  • My contention opens the piece clearly.
  • Each body paragraph has one focused point.
  • I addressed and refuted a counterargument.
  • My conclusion issues a strong call to action.

Lesson 5 · 45 min

Rhetorical Devices

Intended learning: deploy rhetorical devices purposefully to strengthen persuasion and analyse their effect.

Teaching content

Rhetorical devices are the tools that make persuasion vivid and powerful. They shape rhythm, emphasis and emotional response. Key devices include rhetorical questions (whose answer is implied), anaphora (repeating a phrase for emphasis), emotive language (charged words), the rule of three (a trio for rhythm), hyperbole (deliberate exaggeration), and direct address (speaking to "you"). Use them with purpose — a device without purpose is decoration, not persuasion.

Stimulus: "School should start later for teenagers."
Exam-style question (8 marks · ~15 min): Write a persuasive paragraph that uses at least three rhetorical devices, and name them.

Starter sentences

  • "How many more students must struggle before we listen to the science?"
  • "Later starts mean better sleep, better learning, better lives." (rule of three)

Practice worksheet

Task 1. Write a rhetorical question on the stimulus.

Task 2. Write a sentence using the rule of three.

Task 3. Write a paragraph using anaphora, emotive language or hyperbole.

Band 6 sample answer

Model paragraph

How many of our teenagers wake each morning to a body that is simply not ready? [rhetorical question] We ask them to perform, to concentrate, to succeed — on a schedule that fights their biology [anaphora]. Later starts are not a luxury or a convenience. They are better health, better results, better futures [rule of three]. The evidence is in; the choice, finally, is only ours to make.

Marking criteria / self-check

  • I used at least three rhetorical devices.
  • Each device serves the argument.
  • My devices create emphasis, not clutter.
  • I can name each device I used.

Lesson 6 · 45 min

Evidence & Reasoning

Intended learning: select strong evidence and build logical reasoning (logos) to support a contention.

Teaching content

An argument is only as strong as its evidence and reasoning. Evidence can be statistical, factual, anecdotal, expert opinion or real-world example — but it must be relevant, accurate and explained. Reasoning connects the evidence to your claim: after citing evidence, explain what it proves and why it matters. Avoid logical fallacies such as hasty generalisation, straw man, circular reasoning, and appeals to emotion alone.

Stimulus: "Public libraries are more important than ever."
Exam-style question (10 marks · ~18 min): Write one argument paragraph that states a claim, provides evidence, and explains its reasoning.

Starter sentences

  • "Libraries are the great levellers — free to all, open to everyone."
  • "Consider what the local library offers beyond books: computers, classes, a warm space."

Practice worksheet

Task 1. Write a claim about libraries.

Task 2. Provide one piece of evidence (statistic, fact or example).

Task 3. Write two sentences of reasoning linking evidence to claim.

Band 6 sample answer

Model argument paragraph

Public libraries are the great levellers of opportunity [claim]. In an age of rising inequality, libraries remain free, open and universal — offering books, internet access, and literacy programs to anyone, regardless of income [evidence]. This matters because the digital divide is a real divide: a child without home internet is locked out of schooling, jobs and citizenship. A library is the one public door that stays open [reasoning].

Marking criteria / self-check

  • I stated a clear claim.
  • My evidence is relevant and accurate.
  • I explained the reasoning, not just cited evidence.
  • I avoided common logical fallacies.

Lesson 7 · 50 min

Counterargument & Refutation

Intended learning: acknowledge opposing views fairly and refute them to strengthen your own argument.

Teaching content

Addressing a counterargument makes a persuasive piece credible and strong. First acknowledge the opposing view fairly — this shows you understand the issue and builds ethos. Then refute it by pointing out weaknesses in evidence, logic or relevance, and reassert your own position. Phrases like "Some argue that… however…" or "While it is true that… it does not follow that…" frame the move. Never ignore the opposition or misrepresent it (straw man).

Stimulus: "Australia should ban single-use plastics."
Exam-style question (8 marks · ~15 min): Write a paragraph that acknowledges one counterargument and refutes it.

Starter sentences

  • "Sceptics argue a ban is costly and inconvenient — but the cost of inaction is far higher."
  • "Some say alternatives are too expensive; the evidence says otherwise."

Practice worksheet

Task 1. State a counterargument to the ban.

Task 2. Refute it with evidence or reasoning.

Task 3. Write a full acknowledgement-and-refutation paragraph.

Band 6 sample answer

Model paragraph

Critics of a plastic ban argue it would inconvenience consumers and burden businesses with costly alternatives. This concern deserves a fair hearing — transitions are never effortless [acknowledge]. Yet the weight of evidence is against them: the clean-up of plastic waste already costs taxpayers far more than any transition, and reusable alternatives have fallen strikingly in price [refute]. The real burden, in other words, is the one we are already paying — in our rates, our oceans and our future [reassert].

Marking criteria / self-check

  • I stated the counterargument fairly.
  • I refuted it with evidence or reasoning.
  • I avoided the straw man fallacy.
  • I reasserted my contention strongly.

Lesson 8 · 45 min

Audience & Purpose

Intended learning: shape a persuasive text for a specific audience and clear purpose.

Teaching content

Persuasion works on a specific audience. Before writing, ask: Who am I addressing? What do they already believe? What will move them? The purpose — to convince, to call to action, to warn, to advocate — shapes tone, language and structure. A text aimed at teenage peers uses different language and appeals than one aimed at a school board or a government minister. Skilled writers adapt ethos, pathos and logos to their audience's values and concerns.

Stimulus: "Homework should be banned in high school."
Exam-style question (10 marks · ~18 min): Write a persuasive opening aimed at ONE specific audience (choose: students, parents, or teachers), and explain three choices you made for that audience.

Starter sentences

  • "Let's be honest — homework eats your evenings and teaches you very little." (to students)
  • "Every night, our families sit down to a battle over homework." (to parents)
  • "We ask students to work all day, then send them home with more." (to teachers)

Practice worksheet

Task 1. Name your chosen audience and purpose.

Task 2. Write an opening sentence aimed at that audience.

Task 3. Explain two language/appeal choices and their effect on the audience.

Band 6 sample answer

Model (aimed at parents) + explanation

"Every night, millions of Australian families sit down to the same argument: the homework battle. It steals the hours that should be for rest, for sport, for simply being a family." Choices: I used the inclusive "our/we" to build shared identity (ethos); I named a familiar emotional scene (pathos); and I offered a clear, personal stake to draw parents in.

Marking criteria / self-check

  • I identified a specific audience.
  • My language suits that audience.
  • My appeals match their values.
  • My purpose is clear throughout.

Lesson 9 · 45 min

Persuasive Openings & Conclusions

Intended learning: craft powerful openings that hook and state the contention, and conclusions that issue a strong call to action.

Teaching content

The opening must capture attention and establish your contention quickly. Strong options include a compelling statistic, a vivid anecdote, a provocative question, or a bold statement. The conclusion must leave the reader persuaded and moved: it restates the contention, gathers the key ideas, and issues a clear call to action — telling the reader what to believe, feel or do. Unlike a discursive "quiet landing," a persuasive conclusion is emphatic and decisive.

Stimulus: "Voting should be compulsory for everyone over 16."
Exam-style question (10 marks · ~18 min): Write a persuasive opening AND a persuasive conclusion for this stimulus.

Starter sentences

  • "Democracy is not a spectator sport."
  • "At eighteen we are trusted with the vote — so why not at sixteen?"

Practice worksheet

Task 1. Write a hook and contention opening.

Task 2. Write a conclusion with a clear call to action.

Task 3. Contrast how a discursive and a persuasive conclusion would differ.

Band 6 sample answer

Model opening + conclusion

Opening: "Democracy is not a spectator sport — it is a promise that every voice counts. And yet we exclude sixteen-year-olds, who will inherit every decision we make today, from the most fundamental act of citizenship." Conclusion: "Lowering the voting age is not a radical step; it is a sign of trust in the young people whose futures we claim to value. Let us give them the ballot, and with it a real seat at the table — after all, it is their democracy too."

Marking criteria / self-check

  • My opening hooks and states the contention.
  • My conclusion restates and reinforces.
  • My conclusion includes a clear call to action.
  • I differentiated persuasive from discursive endings.

Lesson 10 · 45 min

Marking Criteria & Self-Assessment

Intended learning: understand the Band 6 criteria for persuasive writing and self-assess a draft.

Teaching content

A Band 6 persuasive response is clear, sustained and sophisticated. Core qualities NESA rewards: a clear contention and strong audience awareness; sustained control of the persuasive register; sophisticated and purposeful use of rhetorical craft; strong, well-supported reasoning; and an effective, decisive conclusion. Common pitfalls: an unclear or shifting contention, weak or unsupported evidence, an emotional appeal that overrides logic, and a conclusion that fades instead of driving.

Stimulus: "Australia should make renewable energy its top priority."
Exam-style question (20 marks · ~40 min): Write a persuasive response to the stimulus, then self-assess it against the criteria below.

Starter sentences

  • "On reflection, my strongest element was…"
  • "Where my argument could sharpen is…"
  • "If I rewrote this, I would…"

Practice worksheet

Task 1. Self-mark your draft (0–2 each on the criteria).

Band 6 sample self-assessment

Model reflection

On reflection, my strongest element was my contention and the ethos I built through measured, authoritative language. My weakest area was logos — I cited an idea but did not always explain the reasoning fully. If I rewrote this, I would add a precise statistic to each body argument and spell out the link between evidence and claim, sharpening the logic without losing the emotional drive.

Marking criteria / self-check

  • I can state the Band 6 descriptors for persuasive writing.
  • I self-assessed honestly against the grid.
  • I identified a strength with evidence.
  • I identified a weakness and a specific fix.
  • I know the common pitfalls.
