What Is Discursive Writing?

Discursive writing is a mode of expression in which the author explores a topic from multiple angles without committing wholesale to a single conclusion. The word derives from the Latin discursus — literally "a running to and fro" — which neatly captures the way a discursive essay moves back and forth between competing ideas, weighing evidence, considering perspectives, and only lightly settling on a nuanced viewpoint. It is the intellectual practice of thinking out loud on the page: the writer lays out the landscape of an argument, walks the reader through its peaks and valleys, and invites them to form a reasoned judgment.

In the context of the New South Wales Higher School Certificate (HSC) syllabus, discursive writing occupies a distinctive place within Module C. It is valued far more for the sincerity and balance of its exploration than for the ferocity with which it defends a position. Where a persuasive essay strives to convert, a discursive essay strives to illuminate. The reader should finish a discursive piece feeling that they have been shown the full picture — the strengths and weaknesses, the tensions and harmonies — rather than herded toward a predetermined verdict.

This distinction matters for students. Many young writers arrive at discursive writing after perfecting the persuasive form: a strong thesis, three bulletproof body paragraphs, and a triumphant conclusion. Discursive writing asks them to unlearn a little of that confidence. It demands intellectual humility — the recognition that most meaningful questions are genuinely difficult, that evidence frequently pulls in opposite directions, and that the most sophisticated response is often to hold two ideas in tension rather than to choose between them.

At its heart, discursive writing is driven by sincere engagement with ideas. The best discursive writers are not detached cataloguers of arguments; they are curious, thoughtful partners in dialogue. They pose sharp questions, acknowledge uncomfortable contradictions, and treat the audience as intelligent equals. This tone of genuine inquiry is what separates a dry, list-like exposition from a living, breathing piece of discursive prose.

How It Differs From Other Text Types

To understand discursive writing fully, it helps to place it on a spectrum of related forms. On one end sits persuasive writing, whose entire purpose is to convince the reader of a single proposition through emotional appeal, rhetorical devices and an argumentative stance. On the other end sits informative writing, which conveys facts and explanations without advancing any particular viewpoint. Discursive writing sits deliberately in the middle: it neither shouts a conclusion at the reader nor catalogues facts indifferently. It is a thoughtful, even-handed exploration of a question.

Consider how each form opens the same debate. A persuasive piece might begin, "The internet must be regulated without delay, for the safety of our children demands it." A discursive piece begins differently: "Whether the internet should be regulated is a question with no easy answer; unregulated spaces allow the free exchange of ideas, yet they also expose the young to genuine dangers." The persuasive opening declares allegiance; the discursive opening opens a door.

Key Distinction: Persuasive writing argues; discursive writing explores. A discursive essay may reach a mild preference, but it earns that preference by exposing the reader to every reasonable position along the way.

Discursive writing also shares territory with reflective and personal essays. It frequently draws on the author's experience and voice, as reflective writing does, but keeps its gaze wider than the purely personal. Personal material becomes a springboard into broader, more universal questions rather than the final subject. This blending of the personal and the analytical is why discursive essays feel so alive: they combine human warmth with rigorous thought.

For students, understanding this fault line between the forms is not merely a theoretical exercise. Assessment criteria reward candidates who understand the register they have been asked to align with. A student who produces a glorious persuasive essay when the prompt demanded a discursive response will be marked down regardless of the quality of the writing, simply because they misread the genre. Awareness of genre, therefore, is a form of intellectual honesty.

Core Features of Discursive Writing

A successful discursive piece rests on a handful of recognisable features. When we read an essay and immediately sense that it is discursive rather than persuasive, we are responding to these markers working together. Understanding them consciously allows you to build the qualities into your own writing with intention rather than luck.

1. Balance and Fairness

The defining characteristic of discursive writing is its insistence on exploring all sides of an issue. This is not the same as being wishy-washy or refusing to have an opinion. Rather, it means treating every perspective with genuine seriousness before weighing them. Balanced writers make the strongest possible case they can for each position, even — perhaps especially — the position they ultimately disagree with. This generosity of spirit is what earns the reader's trust.

Pro Tip: When you raise a counterargument, steelman it — present the most persuasive version you can, not a straw man. Readers immediately recognise a distorted argument, and it undermines the credibility of the whole piece.

2. A Sincere, Personal Voice

Discursive writing is distinguished by the presence of a genuine human voice behind the argument. The writer speaks in the first person, shares observations and experiences, and is honest about their own uncertainties. This sincerity is not self-indulgence; it is a strategic choice that makes the exploration feel authentic. When a writer admits that a question troubled them, the reader leans in.

3. The Journey Over the Destination

In a persuasive essay the destination — the thesis — usually comes first and everything else marches toward it. In a discursive essay the reverse is true: the journey is the point. The author sets out from a question or a curiosity and allows the reader to accompany them through the twists and turns of their thinking. The conclusion, when it arrives, feels earned precisely because the reader has travelled the road.

4. Evidence Used as Fuel, Not Weaponry

Both persuasive and discursive writers use evidence, but they do so differently. A persuasive writer marshals facts like ammunition against an opponent. A discursive writer offers evidence as material for shared reflection. Statistics, expert opinion, anecdote and analogy are all welcome, but they are presented to illuminate the complexity of the issue rather than to deliver a knockout blow.

5. A Reflectively Structured Form

Discursive writing is not unstructured rambling. It is a carefully considered form in which each section leads naturally to the next. Transitions are gentle, ideas build on one another, and the overall shape moves from broad question, through exploration, to a considered, moderate conclusion. The absence of a rigidly signposted thesis is a feature, not a flaw.

The Anatomy of a Discursive Piece

Although discursive writing resists rigid formulas, most effective pieces share a broad organisational shape. Thinking of this shape as a flexible framework — rather than a box to be filled — gives you a reliable map while leaving room for creativity.

Opening: The Hook and the Question

A discursive essay most often opens with something that draws the reader in — a striking image, a personal anecdote, a provocative observation — and then quickly locates the question or tension at the heart of the piece. The opening is the moment of orientation: it tells the reader what terrain we will be exploring, but it deliberately withholds any tidy answer. The question, not the answer, is introduced first.

Body: The Exploratory Middle

The body of a discursive essay is where the exploration happens. Here the writer moves through different perspectives on the question, considering each in turn. A common approach is to devote a section to one major viewpoint, then turn to its opposite, then weigh a third or synthesize the two. The body is characterised by transitions like "on the other hand," "and yet," and "some would argue." These signal that the writer is genuinely entertaining multiple lines of thought.

Crucially, the body is not the place for one-sided advocacy. Even when the writer leans toward a particular view, they pause to acknowledge the force of the opposing side. This is what gives discursive writing its mature, trustworthy quality: the sense that the author has done the hard work of really listening.

Conclusion: The Quiet Landing

The conclusion of a discursive essay rarely thunders. Instead, it lands quietly, gathering the threads of the exploration into a considered reflection. It may express the writer's tentative lean, offer a caution, pose a final question, or gesture toward what remains unresolved. The key is moderation: a discursive conclusion resists the false certainty of a rallying cry. It closes with integrity, acknowledging the complexity the essay has revealed.

Pro Tip: A powerful discursive conclusion might end with a question rather than an answer, leaving the reader to continue the conversation in their own mind. This mirrors the essay's whole spirit of open inquiry.

A Sample Skeleton

  • Hook: An image or anecdote that makes the topic feel human and urgent.
  • Question: A clear statement of the central question or tension.
  • Perspective A: The strongest statement of one side, fairly offered.
  • Perspective B: The strongest statement of the opposing side.
  • Weighing: A synthesis that considers limits, nuances and overlaps.
  • Reflection: A moderate, sincere closing that leaves room for further thought.

The Band 6 Structure (NESA Module C)

In the New South Wales HSC, discursive writing is assessed through Module C: The Craft of Writing (for English Standard and English Advanced) and through closely related outcomes in the Extension and EAL/D courses. A "Band 6" discursive response is one that reaches the top achievement level of the NSW Education Standards Authority (NESA) marking scale — roughly 45–50 marks out of 50. To reach this band, a response must demonstrate the specific qualities NESA rewards rather than merely being "good writing."

What NESA's Rubric Requires

NESA's Module C rubric rewards students who demonstrate "increasing confidence and sophistication" in their writing. Within the broad outcomes English Standard (EN12-1 to EN12-5) and English Advanced (EA12-1 to EA12-5), the key expectations for a discursive response are:

  • Personal, reflective and imaginative voice: The composer is present; the piece feels authentic, not formulaic.
  • Sustained control of the chosen form: The discursive register is maintained from opening to close — it never slides into persuasion or plain exposition.
  • Sophisticated use of stylistic devices and language: Deliberate, purposeful choices of syntax, imagery, hedging, and structure.
  • Sincere exploration of ideas: A genuine engagement with complexity rather than a rehearsed argument.
  • Strong awareness of audience and purpose: The piece is clearly shaped for a reader and a reason.
  • Effective, integrated and sustained use of the craft: The writer's techniques are woven throughout, not bolted on.
Band 6 marker: Examiners distinguish Band 6 responses from Band 5 by sustained sophistication — every sentence earns its place, the voice is distinctive, and the exploration of the idea feels genuine and controlled, right to the final line.

Key Statistics & Facts About the HSC

  • Module C weighting: In both English Standard and English Advanced, Paper 2 (where Module C appears) is worth 25 marks out of 125 in the total HSC English examination.
  • Marking band: A Band 6 corresponds to marks of 45–50 out of 50 on the course, representing a "highly developed" and "sophisticated" level of achievement.
  • Time allocation: Students typically have roughly 40 minutes to plan and write the Module C response in exam conditions.
  • Real writing focus: Module C is distinctive because students write createatively and reflectively — it is one of the few HSC components that directly assesses the craft of writing itself.

Language & Tone Toolkit

The language of discursive writing is one of measured reflection. It avoids the shrill, the absolute, and the aggressive, preferring instead words and structures that keep the door open for nuance. Building a toolkit of these linguistic moves is one of the quickest ways to make your writing feel genuinely discursive.

Hedging Language

Hedging is the deliberate softening of claims to signal that they are probabilistic rather than absolute. Words like "tends to," "may," "arguably," "it seems," and "to some extent" tell the reader that the writer is open to exception. Hedging is not weakness; it is intellectual honesty about the limits of certainty. Notably, this is precisely how rigorous researchers speak about their findings — describing associations and tendencies rather than absolute certainties (see Twenge, 2017).

Instead of: "Social media destroys young people's mental health."

Write: "Emerging research suggests that heavy social media use may be associated with a decline in some young people's reported wellbeing."

The second version is discursively appropriate — it signals uncertainty, acknowledges nuance, and avoids overgeneralising.

Concession and Counterpoint

Concessive structures — sentences that grant a point before qualifying it — are the grammatical heart of discursive writing. Phrases like "while it is true that," "admittedly," "granted," and "nevertheless" allow you to show balance in a single breath. They demonstrate that you have heard the other side and chosen to respond thoughtfully rather than dismissively.

Rhetorical Questions

The rhetorical question is a powerful discursive tool because it models the very act of inquiry. By asking "But is that really the case?" or "Who, in the end, pays the price?" the writer invites the reader to pause, reflect, and participate in the exploration. Used sparingly and sincerely, rhetorical questions humanise the essay and draw the reader into the thinking.

Concrete, Sensory Detail

Because discursive writing often draws on the personal, it benefits enormously from concrete, sensory detail. Rather than abstractly discussing "the pressures of modern life," a writer might paint a specific scene. This concrete imagery grounds the intellectual exploration in lived experience, making abstract ideas feel vivid and real.

Avoiding Excess

Just as important as what to include is what to avoid. Discursive writing steers clear of loaded emotional language, sweeping absolutisms ("always," "never," "everyone knows"), and sarcasm directed at opposing views. The register is thoughtful and measured, never shrill. Even when discussing issues that provoke passion, the discursive writer keeps a calm, reflective temperature.

"The pen is mightier than the sword" is often quoted, but a discursive writer knows that the pen's true power lies not in cutting down the opposition, but in carefully, honestly tracing the truth wherever it leads.

Worked Examples

The best way to understand discursive writing is to see it assembled. Below are three original, full-length discursive pieces. Read each slowly, noticing how the writer balances perspectives, uses a personal voice, and lands on a moderate conclusion. Try to identify where each essay could have slipped into persuasion — and applaud it for resisting.

Example One: The Question of Smartphones in Schools

On my first day of high school, my head of year stood before the assembly and asked a question I have never forgotten. "Raise your hand," she said, "if you have ever felt left out because you did not have the right phone." Nearly every hand in the room went up. It was a small question, asked at a routine assembly, but it captured a tension that haunts our education system: the phone is at once an essential tool of belonging and a persistent source of division.

For decades now, schools have wrestled with how to treat the smartphone. On one side stand those who would ban it outright. Their case is substantial. Research increasingly links heavy screen use to reduced attention spans and disrupted sleep (Twenge, 2017). Teachers report that a single glowing device can fracture the focus of an entire classroom. And the social costs are real: cyberbullying does not stop at the school gate, and the pressure to compare oneself to curated online lives can corrode adolescent self-esteem. When my school trialled a ban, attendance at lunchtime sport soared — children who had been glued to screens suddenly rediscovered the football field.

Yet the opposing case refuses to be dismissed. Our students are preparing for a world in which digital literacy is not optional but essential. To banish the phone entirely is to pretend that this world does not exist. Many argue that the more productive approach is not prohibition but education — explicitly teaching young people to navigate their devices with intention and discipline. And there is something troublingly patronising about assuming that an entire generation cannot learn to manage a technology and instead must be separated from it. When I was a student, adults said the same of calculators and, earlier, of the pocket book that threatened to make children unable to memorise.

Where, then, does this leave us? I do not believe there is a single answer that suits every school, every age group, or every child. But it seems to me that the extremes are rarely the wisest course. A total ban treats young people as passive and helpless; no policy at all abandons them to an environment they did not choose. The most thoughtful path may be a middle one: some restrictions during class time, honest conversations about digital wellbeing, and trust in the capacity of the young to grow into the adults we hope they become. On the question of the phone in the pocket, perhaps the wisest posture is not certainty, but ongoing, patient conversation.

Example Two: Should We Fear Artificial Intelligence?

The first time I asked a chatbot a question, I felt a small thrill that was quickly followed by a larger unease. It answered quickly, fluently, and almost entirely correctly. Somewhere in that fluent exchange, I realised I was witnessing something genuinely new. Whether that newness is a gift or a threat, however, is a question that resists a simple answer.

The fear is easy to understand. Every week brings a headline about machines displacing workers, about software writing essays students were meant to write themselves, about algorithms that seem to know what we want before we do. The dystopian novel has taught us to be wary of the machine that grows smarter than its maker. Behind this fear lies a legitimate concern: that in handing our thinking to machines, we might slowly surrender the very capacities that make us human — our judgment, our creativity, our ability to question our own conclusions.

But fear, left unexamined, becomes its own kind of blindness. After all, humanity has faced such moments before. The typewriter did not end writing; the calculator did not end mathematics. Each new tool changed those crafts, certainly, and each raised the same anxious question — will this machine make us redundant? Yet in every case, human beings adapted, and the tool became a partner rather than a replacement. There is no inevitable reason artificial intelligence must be different. Used thoughtfully, it might handle the tedious and the routine, freeing our attention for the work that genuinely requires imagination and empathy. It might even, as some educators and researchers now report, help us see patterns and expand our thinking rather than only replace it (UNESCO, 2025).

Perhaps the truest answer is that the machine is neither gift nor threat in itself, but a mirror of our own choices. The same technology that can automate away jobs can also be marshalled to solve problems. The question "should we fear artificial intelligence?" may, in the end, be the wrong question. The better question might be "how do we want to use it?" On that, I am neither anxious nor credulous — I am attentive, watchful, and hopeful, in roughly equal measure.

Example Three: Is Social Media Bringing Us Together or Tearing Us Apart?

My grandmother keeps a photograph of her old school friends in a silver frame above the fireplace. They are smiling, arms linked, frozen in a moment from the early 1960s. She has stayed in touch with exactly two of them, writing letters that took a fortnight to arrive. When I showed her my own phone, where several hundred "friends" are reachable in the time it takes to type a sentence, she was quietly amazed. "So many people," she said, "and yet, sometimes, I wonder if you are ever really together."

Her remark has stayed with me, because it captures so neatly the paradox at the heart of social media. On the one hand, the technology is a staggering instrument of connection. Families scattered across continents can meet in a video call; activists can organise movements that topple governments; a teenager in a small town can find, for the first time, a community of people who share their passions or struggles. For the lonely, the marginalised, and the geographically isolated, social media has been an unqualified gift.

And yet the evidence of its shadow is impossible to ignore. The same platforms that connect us also seem, at times, to isolate us — trading face-to-face warmth for disembodied validation. Researchers have documented rising rates of loneliness alongside rising rates of screen time, with a large share of teens now describing their use as "almost constant" (Pew Research Center, 2023), and if correlation is not causation, it is at least a question we are obliged to take seriously. Meanwhile, the algorithms that feed us content are engineered to hold attention, and attention withheld from our neighbours is attention that cannot be given to friendship. My grandmother's two letters, slow as they were, carried a weight that a thousand notifications cannot match.

Perhaps the truth is that social media is neither the great uniter nor the great divider, but a tool shaped by the hands that use it. It can be a stage for vanity and outrage, or a window into lives we would otherwise never glimpse. The choice, as ever, lies with us. I suspect the wisest response is neither to embrace it uncritically nor to flee it in fear, but to use it consciously — to reach out, to listen, to remember that every glowing profile is a real person with a real life. My grandmother keeps her photograph in a silver frame. Perhaps the lesson of social media is that we, too, must learn to hold our many connections with care.

Studying Real Discursive Texts

The most powerful way to internalise the craft of discursive writing is to study how established composers do it. The three texts analysed below are widely used in senior English classrooms and are strongly aligned with NESA's Module C "prescribed/related texts" approach: Omar Sakr's memoir essay A Second Home, Mabel Kwong's reflective essay What Does It Mean to Be Asian Australian?, and the personal/cultural essay Travel as the Ultimate Revenge. These are studied as discursive texts — they explore ideas with balance, sincerity and personal voice rather than arguing a one-sided case.

Text One: Omar Sakr — A Second Home (memoir-essay)

In A Second Home, the Australian writer Omar Sakr reflects on the public library as a site of belonging, refuge and identity for a young child of an immigrant family. It is discursive because it does not argue that "libraries are good" in a persuasive sense; instead, it explores what it means to belong to a public institution, the tensions of that belonging, and the way books offered a sense of coherence and inclusion that the world outside did not.

Text Two: Mabel Kwong — What Does It Mean to Be Asian Australian?

Mabel Kwong's reflective essay poses the identity question directly in its title — a fundamentally discursive move. Kwong does not resolve what it "means" to be Asian Australian; instead she explores competing definitions, personal experience, and the difficulty of holding a hyphenated identity.

Text Three: Travel as the Ultimate Revenge

This personal essay begins with travel anecdotes, then shifts to explore the cultural phenomenon of "revenge travel" after COVID-19. It uses the structure many Band 6 students study: anecdotal opening, an issue introduced, factual grounding, a turn to rival viewpoints, and a resonant personal close.

How to Analyse a Discursive Text (Full Method)

Analysing a discursive text for Module C requires you to move beyond identifying techniques and into explaining how the composer's choices work to achieve sincere, balanced exploration. Use this five-stage method to build a sustained, sophisticated analysis.

Stage 1: Identify the Mode and Purpose

Begin by confirming the text is discursive. Ask: Does it explore a question rather than argue a single thesis? Does the writer present more than one perspective fairly? Is there a sincere personal voice? Name the purpose — to illuminate, to contemplate, to invite reflection.

Stage 2: Track the Structure and the Journey

Unlike a persuasive essay with a thesis-first structure, a discursive text follows a journey. Map it: anecdotal opening → question introduced → perspectives explored → turning points / concessions → synthesis → quiet landing. Notice where the writer pivots and how they signal the pivot ("and yet," "but consider," "admittedly").

Stage 3: Isolate the Craft (Technique + Example)

Select the most significant techniques — metaphor, anecdote, hedging, rhetorical questions, concession, sensory detail, aphorism — and quote the precise evidence. Do not quote everything; choose what earns analysis.

Stage 4: Analyse Effect (the Band 6 Engine)

For every technique, answer the "so what?" in three layers:

  1. What it does: The literal effect of the technique.
  2. How it shapes meaning: What idea, tension or perspective it develops.
  3. Why it matters for the mode: How it contributes to the balanced, sincere, exploratory quality of the discursive register.

Stage 5: Synthesise (Connect to Module C / Course)

Tie your analysis back to NESA's outcomes: how does the composer demonstrate "craft," "sophistication," a "personal and imaginative" voice? In a comparative task, connect the text to your prescribed text or to the ideas of other studied texts.

Syllabus by Course (Standard / Advanced / Extension / EAL&D)

Discursive writing is assessed differently across the four English courses. Below is a course-by-course guide, each aligned to its relevant NESA syllabus outcomes. Always confirm full details in the current official NESA syllabus documents.

English Standard — Module C: The Craft of Writing

  • Relevant outcomes: ES12-1 (responds to texts), ES12-3 (uses language to shape meaning), ES12-4 (crafts own texts), ES12-5 (thinks imaginatively, creatively and interpretively).
  • Course focus: Students examine how writers craft texts and apply these skills to their own writing, including a discursive piece.
  • Assessment style: Imaginative, reflective and discursive writing; often with a stimulus.
  • Band 6 focus: "Accurate, clear and sustained" crafting, strong control of register, and a distinctive personal voice.

English Advanced — Module C: The Craft of Writing

  • Relevant outcomes: EA12-1 to EA12-5, with particular emphasis on EA12-3 (language choices) and EA12-4 (crafting of texts).
  • Course focus: Similar to Standard but with sophistication and originality expected; students craft a discursive response demonstrating "control and sophistication."
  • Assessment style: Often requires students to reflect on their own writing choices in a statement of intention alongside the piece.
  • Band 6 focus: "Highly developed, sustained and sophisticated" crafting; original, assured voice.

English Extension — Craft of Writing component

  • Course focus: Extension courses emphasise independent, sophisticated composition; the "craft of writing" is developed through sustained composition and reflection on process.
  • Extension 1 / 2: Students engage in creative and analytical writing that demonstrates rigorous, self-aware composition and critical reflection.
  • Band 6 focus: Synthesis of concept and execution; a distinctive, self-aware authorial voice; sophisticated manipulation of form.

English EAL/D — Module C (and related reflective writing)

  • Course focus: EAL/D students develop the craft of writing with attention to language conventions, register and audience, including discursive and reflective forms.
  • Distinctive element: Emphasis on clear communication while still demonstrating inventive language and control of form.
  • Band 6 focus: Sustained control of register and grammar, effective crafting, and successful engagement of audience despite the additional demands of learning English as an additional language or dialect.
Important: The requirements, text lists, and assessment designs for each course can change. Always consult the current NESA syllabus and your school's assessment task notifications as the authoritative source.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Because discursive writing sits so close to other forms, it is easy to slip over the line without noticing. Watching for these recurring pitfalls will help you stay true to the genre.

  • Sliding into persuasion. The most common mistake is beginning in a balanced, exploratory tone and then slowly turning the screw until the essay becomes a one-sided argument. Check each body paragraph: are you still seriously entertaining the other side, or are you simply performing fairness before demolishing it?
  • Vagueness disguised as balance. Some writers avoid committing because they fear choosing a side, producing an essay of bland platitudes that never says anything. Balance is not cowardice. A good discursive essay still has a point of view; it simply arrives at it honestly through exploration rather than asserting it from the start.
  • Overusing emotive language. Words designed to inflame — "outrageous," "sabotage," "shameful" — belong to the persuasive register. When they appear in a discursive essay, they break the measured tone and betray a descent into advocacy.
  • Forgetting the personal voice. Discursive writing draws strength from sincerity and lived experience. A piece that reads like a dispassionate report card, with no trace of the writer behind it, has missed the point. Do not be afraid to let your own observations and uncertainties in.
  • Ending with a false certainty. A triumphant, thunderous conclusion undoes everything the essay has built. If you have genuinely explored complexity, end with integrity — a moderate reflection, even a lingering question, rather than a hollow rallying cry.
Remember: The mark of a mature discursive writer is the ability to sit with uncertainty and still write with confidence about the journey of exploring it.

Final Checklist

Before you submit your discursive piece, run through this checklist to make sure you have honoured the genre.

  • I have opened with a question or tension rather than a rigid thesis.
  • I have seriously entertained more than one perspective, fairly and generously.
  • My evidence illuminates the issue rather than merely attacking a side.
  • My personal voice and honest uncertainties are present throughout.
  • My language is measured — hedged, concessive, and free of shrill emotive excess.
  • My conclusion lands quietly and with integrity, resisting false certainty.
  • Any sources or ideas borrowed from others are clearly cited.

Discursive writing is one of the most mature and rewarding forms of expression a student can master. It asks you to think, to listen, and to hold complexity without flinching — and, in doing so, it teaches you something the best essays always teach: that the deepest answers to our hardest questions are rarely simple, but always worth the search.
