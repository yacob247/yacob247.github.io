10-Lesson Imaginative Texts Program

Ten lessons — from story elements to composing a complete narrative — each with teaching content, an exam‑style question, a Band 6 model, analysis and self‑check criteria.

Program map (each ±45–50 minutes)
  1. What Makes a Story a Story?
  2. Characters and Their Wants
  3. Setting the Scene
  4. Plot and the Narrative Arc
  5. Fairy Tales and Traditional Stories
  6. Poetry: Sound, Image and Emotion
  7. Plays and Performance
  8. Narrative Film and Moving Images
  9. Show, Don’t Tell: Composing a Story
  10. Revising and Publishing Your Story
Lesson 1 · 45 min

What Makes a Story a Story?

Intended learning: identify the defining elements of a story and separate story from mere event.

Teaching content

A list of events is not yet a story. A story has character, setting, a problem and a shape — and usually something at stake. Model with a familiar fairy tale: name who, where, what goes wrong, and how it resolves. Introduce the idea that a story is events selected and arranged to mean.

Stimulus: “The cat sat. The cat slept. The cat woke. The cat ate.” vs. “The cat, who had never caught anything, saw a bird.”
Question (5 marks · ~10 min): Explain why the second group of sentences feels more like “a story” than the first.

Band 6 model

Model response

The first list simply records events in time — no stakes, no inner life. The second begins to be a story because it gives the cat a longing (“never caught anything”) and a moment of chance (the bird). Trouble is the seed of plot: once there is a want and a risk, a reader starts asking what happens next, and that question is what makes a story.

  • I named at least two story elements.
  • I explained “stakes” or “want.”
  • I linked the elements to “what happens next.”

Worksheet — Lesson 1

Turn a one-line event list into a mini-story by adding a want and a problem.

Lesson 2 · 45 min

Characters and Their Wants

Intended learning: build characters with wants and obstacles, and reveal them through action and dialogue.

Teaching content

Characters are the engine of plot. Teach the trio want – obstacle – choice: a character who wants something, meets a barrier, and makes a choice that shows who they are. Reveal character by showing — a gesture, a line of dialogue, a decision — rather than telling (“she was brave”).

Stimulus: two lines of dialogue that reveal character without naming it.
Question (6 marks · ~12 min): Write three sentences showing a stubborn character through action and speech, without the word “stubborn.”

Band 6 model

Model response

“I’m not lost,” Leo said, folding the map along the wrong crease for the third time. He walked forty metres the wrong way, then back, grinning. “You wanted the scenic route,” he said, which was both a lie and not a lie. The fold, the grin, the re-label — each action carries the stubbornness, so the reader feels it rather than being told.

  • My character has a clear want.
  • I showed it through action or speech.
  • I avoided telling adjectives.

Worksheet — Lesson 2

Write a “want – obstacle – choice” plan for a character of your own.

Lesson 3 · 45 min

Setting the Scene

Intended learning: use setting to create mood and drive the story, not just locate it.

Teaching content

Setting is where and when — and how it feels. Model how a place can be the mood (a cupboard is lonely; a rainy schoolyard is bleak) or can drive plot (a locked room creates the obstacle). Teach students to choose sensory details that echo the feeling they want.

Stimulus: describe one ordinary room twice — once as a refuge, once as a trap — changing only the details.
Question (6 marks · ~12 min): Write two short descriptions of the same kitchen that create opposite moods.

Band 6 model

Model response

Refuge: Morning light crossed the table like a slow invitation; the kettle hummed a round of comfort and the same four mugs waited on the rail, familiar as a chorus. Trap: The same table, but the light was too bright, sharp off the tiles; the kettle’s hum seemed to grow impatient, and the four mugs hung like a row of staring eyes. Nothing has moved, only the feeling — which is exactly how setting works: it is weather for the soul.

  • I used sensory detail.
  • The same place produced two moods.
  • I explained how detail creates feeling.

Worksheet — Lesson 3

Describe your classroom as a place of adventure, then as a place of dread.

Lesson 4 · 45 min

Plot and the Narrative Arc

Intended learning: plan stories on a narrative arc and use pacing for effect.

Teaching content

Introduce the story mountain — orientation, complication, rising action, climax, resolution. Teach pacing: where to dwell (the build-up) and where to move fast (a chase). Then show how slowing the climax increases suspense and how an abrupt ending can refuse easy comfort.

Stimulus: a one-sentence premise — “a key found in the sand on the last day of school.”
Question (8 marks · ~15 min): Plan the arc of a short story from this premise, marking the climax.

Band 6 model

Model response

Orientation: last day, a beach everyone is abandoning. Complication: the key fits nothing in sight. Rising: three attempts — the shed, the locker, the old boat. Climax: the lock clicks open on the boat, and inside is last year’s message. Resolution: the sand swallows the key again as the tide comes in. Slowing the three attempts builds suspense by making each failure mount; the tide’s cover at the end allows the mystery to survive rather than be explained to death.

  • My arc has all five stages.
  • My climax is clear.
  • I explained a pacing choice.

Worksheet — Lesson 4

Draw a story mountain for your premise and label each stage.

Lesson 5 · 45 min

Fairy Tales and Traditional Stories

Intended learning: read the patterns, archetypes and lessons of traditional stories.

Teaching content

Fairy tales are the purest story machine. Teach their patterns: repeated numbers (three brothers, three tries), archetypes (helper, villain, fool), thresholds (forests, doors), and the moral or cultural root. Then ask the higher-order question — why do we keep telling these? — and compare two versions to see how culture reshapes a tale.

Stimulus: two versions of a well-known tale (e.g. a European and an Asian retelling of a clever-child tale).
Question (8 marks · ~15 min): Identify the pattern and archetypes in one version, then say what the other version changes and why.

Band 6 model

Model response

Both versions run on the classic engine: a small hero, a clear threshold, three rising trials and a helper. But the retelling swaps the helper from a magical stranger to a community elder, and the prize from treasure to the right to speak at the village council. The pattern is the skeleton — the retelling refills it with a different value — which is why fairy tales survive: they are sturdy bones dressed in each culture’s skin.

  • I named the pattern and archetypes.
  • I compared the two versions.
  • I said what the change means.

Worksheet — Lesson 5

Rewrite a fairy tale’s ending from the villain’s point of view.

Lesson 6 · 45 min

Poetry: Sound, Image and Emotion

Intended learning: read and compose poetry through sound, imagery and compression.

Teaching content

Poetry means by how it sounds and where it breaks. Teach rhythm, rhyme, repetition, imagery and line breaks, and how compression makes each word work harder. Model reading a short poem aloud to hear the feeling before analysing it.

Stimulus: a two-line verse — “Late rain / on the roof / unmaking / my homework / into ink / again.”
Question (6 marks · ~12 min): Explain how sound and line breaks create the poem’s mood, then add two lines of your own.

Band 6 model

Model response

The short, breaking lines make the reader slow down as the speaker does, and the soft fricatives (“rain”, “roof”, “homework”) stay inside the mouth, quiet and resigned. “Unmaking” does double work, turning rain into a gentle vandal. I would add: “the dog yawns / in sympathy / and the lamp / gives up.” My lines continue the music — single pauses, small surrenders — so the feeling stays compressed rather than explained.

  • I commented on sound and imagery.
  • I explained line breaks.
  • My added lines extend the mood.

Worksheet — Lesson 6

Compose a five-line poem about a small moment, then annotate two sound choices.

Lesson 7 · 45 min

Plays and Performance

Intended learning: see drama as story through dialogue and action, and read stage directions.

Teaching content

A play has no narrator — everything must come through dialogue, action and stage directions. Teach how a playwright shows feeling through what characters say and do, and how a reader must imagine staging. Then try performing: voice and movement carry the meaning a narrator would.

Stimulus: a two-line scene — “KIT: I packed your bag. // DAN (stops): You packed my bag?”
Question (6 marks · ~12 min): Add stage directions that change the feeling, then explain the choice.

Band 6 model

Model response

KIT (turning away, quiet): I packed your bag. (beat) DAN (stops, then very slowly): You packed my bag? [not a question but a discovery] The pause and direction “not a question but a discovery” change the line from surprise to tenderness — the actor now delivers it softly, and the audience understands the relationship without being told. In a play, a stage direction is a sentence you cannot hear, and it can say the most.

  • I used stage directions deliberately.
  • I explained what they change.
  • I saw the scene as performance.

Worksheet — Lesson 7

Write a four-line scene that shows a friendship falling out, using only speech and directions.

Lesson 8 · 45 min

Narrative Film and Moving Images

Intended learning: read shot, editing, sound and mise-en-scène as a film’s storytelling language.

Teaching content

Films tell stories in pictures and sound. Teach the basic grammar: shot type (close-up, wide, medium), camera angle (high, low, level), lighting, editing (how cuts shape time), sound (music, effects, silence) and mise-en-scène (everything placed in the frame). A close-up is a thought; a wide shot is a fate.

Stimulus: a 30-second scene — a child alone in a big kitchen, light through a high window, one slow wide shot to close-up, faint music.
Question (8 marks · ~15 min): Explain how shot choice, lighting and sound build the feeling of the scene.

Band 6 model

Model response

The opening wide shot dwarfs the child against the kitchen, so the room becomes an ocean and she a small ship — loneliness without a word. The slow cut to close-up then pulls us to her trembling hands, and the faint music fills the silence with something sad and almost hopeful. The filmmakers never say she is lonely; they place her in a cavernous frame and quiet the sound, letting picture do the telling.

  • I named shot type and angle.
  • I discussed lighting or sound.
  • I explained effect, not just labels.

Worksheet — Lesson 8

Storyboard a four-shot scene that builds anxiety.

Lesson 9 · 50 min

Show, Don’t Tell: Composing a Story

Intended learning: plan and draft a complete story using imagery, dialogue and a clear arc.

Teaching content

Consolidate the craft into one composition. Model the “show, don’t tell” rule in action: turn “she was nervous” into a detail (swallowing, a dry palm, a glance at the door). Then run a plan-draft cycle: choose a want and obstacle, plan the arc, draft with imagery and dialogue, and aim for a single clear effect.

Stimulus: the premise — “a gift that was never meant to be opened on the first day.”
Question (15 marks · ~25 min): Write a complete short story from this premise with a clear arc and at least one moment of “showing.”

Band 6 model (excerpt)

Model response

Milo unwrapped it at the bus stop, where the wind kept lifting the paper like a tongue tasting the gift. Inside was his mother’s folding compass, the brass warm as a held hand, and a note in her square, unhurried hand: for when you find north. He slid it into his pocket and pressed his palm over it all the way to school, so that when the first bell rang and the hall swallowed him, he walked with his hand holding something the way a magnet holds a needle — steadfast, pointed, his. My arc moves from arrival (the gift is a surprise) through rising wonder to a quiet climax of belonging; the compass and the note show memory and hope without a word of explanation.

  • Clear arc with a climax.
  • Imagery and dialogue that show.
  • One consistent emotional effect.

Worksheet — Lesson 9

Complete the plan, then draft the full story.

Lesson 10 · 45 min

Revising and Publishing Your Story

Intended learning: edit a draft against criteria, give kind-and-useful feedback, and polish for publication.

Teaching content

Editing is where a good story becomes a great one. Teach a three-pass edit: (1) Story — is the want clear and the arc complete? (2) Craft — did I show rather than tell, and does every detail earn its place? (3) Polish — read aloud for rhythm, tighten, fix punctuation. Peer feedback should name the effect and suggest one improvement.

Stimulus: a strong-but-flabby first draft paragraph with over-telling.
Question (8 marks · ~15 min): Revise the paragraph: cut one “telling” line, strengthen one image, and justify each change in a sentence.

Band 6 model

Model response

Original said “Nora felt deeply sad that she was leaving” — telling. Revised: “Nora packed the last box, and it was lighter than going had ever felt heavy, so she put the photo back in, wrapped it twice, and let it go.” I cut the stated emotion and let the weight of a single box carry the feeling, and I made the photo—wrapping twice do the work of goodbye. The reader now feels her grief instead of being told its name.

  • I cut telling and added showing.
  • I justified each change.
  • My revision keeps the effect.

Final task

Publish your story (display, anthology, or reading aloud) and reflect using the guide’s criteria.

Assessment guidance: align to NSW K–10 English outcomes for composing imaginative texts — reward clear elements, deliberate technique, controlled structure and a distinctive voice.
