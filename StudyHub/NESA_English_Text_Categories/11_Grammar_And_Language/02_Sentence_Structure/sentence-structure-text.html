The Complete Guide to Sentence Structure

The four sentence types, clause architecture, syntactic techniques, rhythm and pace, faults to avoid, and the Band 6 method of syntax analysis.

Core idea: sentence structure is a technique. Markers reward students who can say not only what a text says but how its architecture makes them feel it.

1. The Four Sentence Types

Type Recipe Effect when used deliberately
Simple One independent clause Clarity, finality, shock. "He died."
Compound Independent + independent (coordinating conjunction or semicolon) Equality or accumulation of ideas; relentless momentum with polysyndeton.
Complex Independent + one or more dependent clauses Hierarchy and nuance; subordinated ideas feel assumed or backgrounded.
Compound-complex Two+ independents + one+ dependent Density; mirrors tangled thought — ideal for stream-of-consciousness.

Same event, four architectures

  • Simple: "The ship sank." — blunt catastrophe.
  • Compound: "The ship struck the reef, and the water rose fast." — sequence, cause-and-chain.
  • Complex: "Although the crew fought all night, the ship sank before dawn." — effort foregrounded, defeat inevitable.
  • Compound-complex: "The crew fought while the water climbed the stairs, and by the time the captain gave the order, the deck was gone." — simultaneity, chaos.

2. Clause Architecture

Every sentence is built from clauses (see the Grammar pack). Three architectural choices matter most:

  • Fronting / inversion: putting the adverbial, object or complement first ("Never had I seen such ruin") — creates formality, drama, or Yoda-like strangeness in voice work.
  • Subordination depth: stacking dependent clauses delays the main verb, building suspense ("Because the letter, which she had sworn to burn, still lay unopened…").
  • Sentence length rhythm: long-short alternation mimics breathing and controls emotional pacing.

3. Syntactic Techniques Bank

Technique Definition Canonical example Analytical use
Anaphora Repeated opening words across sentences/clauses "We shall fight… we shall fight…" (Churchill, 1940) Builds incantatory momentum in speeches.
Asyndeton Omitting conjunctions between items "Veni, vidi, vici" (Julius Caesar) Speed, breathlessness.
Polysyndeton Repeating conjunctions ("and…and…and") …in Hemingway's prose throughout Absorbing accumulation, childlike wonder.
Tricolon Three parallel units rising in weight "of the people, by the people, for the people" (Lincoln, 186) Climactic authority.
Rhetorical question Question expecting no answer "Are we not men?" Enlists audience assent.
Antithesis Balanced contrast within a clause "That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind" (Armstrong, 1969) Memorable dialectic.
Fragment Deliberately incomplete sentence "Never again." (advertising; memorial rhetoric) Punch; isolation of idea.
Periodic vs loose sentence Main clause last vs main clause first Periodic: "Despite every warning, he went." Loose: "He went, despite everything." Periodic suspends; loose conversational.
We shall go on to the end, we shall fight in France, we shall fight on the seas and oceans…— Winston Churchill, speech to the House of Commons, 4 June 1940 — anaphora + polysyndeton fused.

4. Common Faults

  • Comma splice: joining two independents with a comma alone. Fix: semicolon, full stop, or conjunction.
  • Run-on/fused: no punctuation at all between independents.
  • Fragments (unintentional): "Which caused outrage." — fine as deliberate technique, wrong as accident in formal essays.
  • Faulty parallelism: "She liked hiking, to swim, and cycling" → make forms match.
  • Garden-path syntax: "The horse raced past the barn fell" — grammatical but unreadable; clarity is part of control.

5. The Band 6 Syntax Analysis Method

Use F-E-L: Feature → Evidence → Effect/Link. Upgrade your verbs: punctuates, suspends, accumulates, mirrors, enacts.

Band 6 sample paragraph (syntax focus)

The passage's syntax performs its panic. Long periodic sentences pile circumstance upon circumstance while the main verbs stay withheld, so that meaning itself feels postponed — until a three-word fragment lands like a slammed door: "Then silence." The architecture does not describe fear; it produces it in the reader's own reading pace.
