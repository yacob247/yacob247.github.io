Lesson 1 · Foundation level

Subjects, Verbs & the Simple Sentence

Intended learning: locate subject and verb reliably; explain why simple sentences create emphasis.

Teaching: Every sentence needs a subject (who/what) and a finite verb. Simple sentences are not "baby sentences" — masters deploy them for impact after complexity.

Stimulus: the closing of any thriller chapter ending in a short simple sentence.
Jesus wept.— John 11:, the shortest verse in the King James Bible — maximum grief per syllable.
Question (5 marks): Explain how one simple sentence in a studied text creates its effect.

Starters: "The abruptness of…" · "Placed after longer sentences, the simple clause…"

Band 6 sample

"He died." Two words, placed after pages of elaborate hope, function as narrative guillotine: the syntax permits no appeal, enacting for readers the finality the characters cannot escape.

  • I identified subject/verb.
  • I explained contextual effect, not just brevity.

Worksheet 1

Find five simple sentences in your reading; note where each appears in its paragraph and what effect placement creates.

Lesson 2 · Core level

Compound Sentences & Coordination

Intended learning: join independents correctly and analyse coordination's equalising or accumulating force.

Teaching: Coordinators (FANBOYS) and semicolons join equals. Coordination says "these ideas weigh the same" — or, repeated ("and…and"), becomes polysyndeton.

Question (6 marks): Analyse how a compound structure balances two ideas in a studied text.
  • Correct conjunction use in own writing.
  • Analysis links balance to meaning.

Worksheet 2

Rewrite three comma-spliced pairs as compound sentences using different FANBOYS and once a semicolon.

Lesson 3 · Core level

Complex Sentences & Subordination

Intended learning: use subordinators to rank ideas; analyse how texts background information.

Teaching: Subordinators (although, because, while, since, whereas) mark dependent clauses. What is subordinate is rhetorically demoted. Compare: "Although he lied, she stayed." vs "She stayed although he lied."

Question (6 marks): Explain how moving a dependent clause changes a sentence's argument.

Band 6 sample

Fronting the concession makes forgiveness the headline; burying it makes betrayal the frame. Same words — opposite ethics. Skilled composers exploit exactly this lever.

  • Named subordinator type.
  • Explained foreground/background shift.

Worksheet 3

Write six complex sentences; circle dependent clauses; arrow which idea dominates and why.

Lesson 4 · Core level

Compound-Complex & Mapping

Intended learning: build compound-complex sentences deliberately; map clause structure visually.

Teaching: Bracket each clause [I] independent, [D] dependent. Compound-complex mirrors interiority — ideal when analysing stream-of-consciousness passages (Woolf, Joyce).

Question (7 marks): Map and analyse one long sentence from a stream-of-consciousness text.
  • All clauses labelled.
  • Syntax linked to consciousness representation.

Worksheet 4

Compose one compound-complex sentence about your week; bracket clauses; then compress it into a simple sentence and compare effects.

Lesson 5 · Core level

Fragments & Deliberate Incompleteness

Intended learning: distinguish accidental fragments from rhetorical ones; deploy fragments purposefully.

Teaching: Fragments isolate. Advertising lives on them ("Because you're worth it", L'Oréal). In creative writing they mimic thought. In essays, avoid unintentional ones.

Question (5 marks): Analyse a famous fragment slogan: why is incompleteness persuasive?
  • Distinguished deliberate vs error.
  • Explained persuasive effect.

Worksheet 5

Write a 100-word dramatic moment that ends in a fragment. Justify the fragment in one sentence.

Lesson 6 · Advanced / Trial HSC level

Fronting, Inversion & Periodic Sentences

Intended learning: analyse word-order manipulation for drama, formality and suspense.

Teaching: Fronted elements set frames; inversion adds gravity ("Blessed are the meek"); periodic sentences withhold the main clause until the end.

Stimulus: Charles Dickens, A Tale of Two Cities opening — anaphoric antithesis parade.
It was the best of times, it was the worst of times…— Charles Dickens, A Tale of Two Cities (1859)
Question (8 marks): Analyse how Dickens' parallel architecture produces his theme of contradiction.
  • Named parallelism/antithesis precisely.
  • Linked syntax to theme.

Worksheet 6

Invert five ordinary sentences ("I have never seen such rain" → "Never have I seen such rain"); note register change.

Lesson 7 · Advanced / Trial HSC level

Anaphora & Tricolon in Speeches

Intended learning: identify and analyse repetition structures in canonical speeches.

Teaching: Anaphora builds rhythm memory; tricolon delivers climactic closure. Combine with modality analysis from the Grammar pack.

Stimulus: Churchill (1940); Lincoln (1863); MLK (196).
Question (8 marks): Compare two speeches' structural repetition and audience positioning.
  • Precise naming of structures.
  • Comparison sustained across both texts.

Worksheet 7

Write a 6-line speech on a school issue using anaphora twice and one tricolon.

Lesson 8 · Advanced / Trial HSC level

Asyndeton vs Polysyndeton

Intended learning: explain pace control through conjunction density.

Teaching: Asyndeton = breathless speed ("I came, I saw, I conquered"); polysyndeton = rolling absorption. Track conjunction density across a passage.

Question (7 marks): Contrast pace in two passages via their conjunction choices.
  • Counted/demonstrated density.
  • Connected pace to mood.

Worksheet 8

Rewrite one paragraph twice: all conjunctions removed, then doubled. Reflect in 50 words.

Lesson 9 · Trial HSC / Band 6 level

Fixing Faults

Intended learning: repair splices, run-ons and faulty parallelism instantly under exam conditions.

Teaching: Splice fixes: full stop / semicolon / FANBOYS+comma / subordinate one clause. Parallelism rule: items in a list must share grammatical form.

Question (10 marks): Correct ten faulty sentences and justify each fix.
  • All corrections valid.
  • Justifications name the rule.

Worksheet 9

Hunt your last essay for splices and parallels; correct them; list patterns in your errors.

Lesson 10 · Trial HSC / consolidation

Band 6 Syntax Analysis Under Exam Conditions

Intended learning: write a timed syntax-analysis paragraph from an unseen text.

Teaching: Routine: read twice → mark sentence boundaries → find longest + shortest sentence → ask what the contrast does → write F-E-L paragraphs.

Band 6 model close

The unseen passage's single long sentence — forty words before its first main verb — suspends resolution exactly as its protagonist suspends decision; when the verb finally arrives, it arrives as anticlimax. The syntax has already told us the character will fail to act.

Assessment guidance: reward criteria mirror NESA marking guidelines: precise reference, integrated technique-effect analysis, sustained control. Check current guidelines at NESA.

Final self-check

Complete one timed (15 min) syntax paragraph weekly until trials; self-mark against Lesson 9's criteria.
