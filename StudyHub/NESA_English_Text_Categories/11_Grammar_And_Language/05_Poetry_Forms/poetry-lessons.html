Lesson 1 · Foundation level

First Reading: Title, Voice & Gist

Intended learning: complete three reading passes and state a poem's gist in one sentence.

Teaching: Titles are contracts. "The Road Not Taken" promises a road AND a refusal. Read aloud — poetry is scored for breath.

Stimulus: any short prescribed or classic poem (e.g., Frost's "The Road Not Taken", 1916).
Question (5 marks): In one sentence each: what happens, who speaks, and what does the title add?
  • Gist accurate and compressed.
  • Title's contribution identified.

Worksheet 1

Three-pass annotation sheet on a new poem: pass 1 feelings, pass 2 structure map, pass 3 device mining.

Lesson 2 · Core level

Imagery & the Controlling Metaphor

Intended learning: identify the controlling metaphor organising a whole poem.

Hope is the thing with feathers.— Emily Dickinson, poem 254
Question (8 marks): Explain how the controlling metaphor shapes the entire poem's meaning.

Band 6 sample

The bird conceit governs every subsequent choice: hope sings wordless tunes, survives foreign lands, never demands payment — so the abstraction inherits avian virtues of persistence without appetite, which is Dickinson's quiet thesis about hope's dignity.

  • Metaphor named + tracked across stanzas.
  • Thesis drawn from it.

Worksheet 2

Metaphor web: central image in centre; every related image radiating out with page refs.

Lesson 3 · Core level

Meter & Scansion Basics

Intended learning: scan lines for iambic/trochaic patterns and interpret deviation.

Teaching: da-DUM marking; count feet; hunt the break. Deviation = meaning spike.

Question (7 marks): Scan four supplied lines; explain how one metrical break intensifies its moment.

Band 6 sample

The line runs iambically until "never" lands as two stresses in collision — the metre itself refuses smoothness exactly where the speaker refuses comfort, making rhythm carry refusal before diction confirms it.

  • Scansion mostly accurate.
  • Deviation interpreted, not just spotted.

Worksheet 3

Scan eight lines from any Shakespeare sonnet; circle every substitution and note its local effect.

Lesson 4 · Core level

Rhyme Schemes & Closure

Intended learning: label schemes; analyse rhyme's role in resolution or unease.

Teaching: Perfect rhyme closes; slant rhyme unsettles; couplets verdict. Dickinson's slant rhymes are theological doubt made audible.

Question (6 marks): Analyse how rhyme choices shape a supplied stanza's emotional close.
  • Scheme labelled correctly.
  • Rhyme-effect argued.

Worksheet 4

Rewrite a slant-rhyme stanza with perfect rhymes; write 60 words on what was lost.

Lesson 5 · Advanced / Trial HSC level

Lineation

Intended learning: analyse enjambment, caesura and breaks as deliberate technique.

I heard a Fly buzz — when I died —— Emily Dickinson, poem 591
Question (7 marks): Choose three line-breaks; argue what each accomplishes at the turn.

Band 6 sample

Enjambment here is ambush: the sense completes only past the margin, so each turn delivers a small afterthought — grief structured as an endless series of things remembered too late.

  • Breaks located precisely.
  • Turn-effects articulated individually.

Worksheet 5

Refill a free-verse poem as prose; then re-lineate differently; compare meanings produced.

Lesson 6 · Advanced / Trial HSC level

The Sonnet Family

Intended learning: distinguish Shakespearian vs Petrarchan architecture; locate voltas.

Question (8 marks): Analyse how a sonnet's form stages its argument, volta to couplet.

Band 6 sample (Sonnet 116)

Quatrains attempt definitions — star, bark, error — each failing upward into abstraction until the couplet stakes the poet's whole corpus on love's indefinability. The form doesn't contain the argument; it escalates it.

  • Formal parts named accurately.
  • Volta located + interpreted.

Worksheet 6

Annotate Sonnet 130: mark quatrains' argument shifts; explain why the final couplet subverts the blazon tradition.

Lesson 7 · Advanced / Trial HSC level

Refrain Forms: Ballad & Villanelle

Intended learning: explain how repetition-with-variation generates meaning in fixed refrain forms.

Question (8 marks): Analyse Thomas' villanelle: how do the refrains transform across the poem?

Band 6 sample

"Do not go gentle" begins as advice and ends as incantation — each return strips another layer of composure, so the form itself performs the son's escalating desperation while the second refrain ("Rage, rage") converts sorrow into command.

  • Both refrains tracked through all appearances.
  • Form-emotion link explicit.

Worksheet 7

Chart the villanelle's A1/A2 appearances in a grid; note contextual shift per return.

Lesson 8 · Advanced / Trial HSC level

Free Verse

Intended learning: treat free verse lineation as designed form rather than absence of form.

Teaching: Whitman's long cataloguing lines vs Williams' short variable ones — both are choices. Ask: what does THIS break do HERE?

Question (7 marks): Analyse how one free-verse poem's layout creates meaning no prose version could.
  • Layout treated as technique.
  • No 'no rules = no analysis' fallacy.

Worksheet 8

Write a 10-line free verse piece where every break is defensible; annotate three of them.

Lesson 9 · Trial HSC / Band 6 level

Voice, Persona & Tone

Intended learning: distinguish poet/speaker/persona; grade tone precisely.

Teaching: Tone ladders: not "sad" but elegiac/wry/defiant/resigned. Persona masks let poets speak otherwise (Duffy's The World's Wife). Unreliable lyric voices reward suspicion.

Question (8 marks): Characterise the voice using precise tone vocabulary and support with evidence.
  • Poet vs speaker distinguished.
  • Tone words precise + evidenced.

Worksheet 9

Tone audit: five tone adjectives for your set poem, each tied to a quoted phrase.

Lesson 10 · Trial HSC / consolidation

Comparative Poem Essay (Timed)

Intended learning: plan and draft a 40-minute comparison of two poems.

Teaching: Compare by IDEAS not poems-in-turn: thesis → similarity/difference in treatment → technique evidence from both → evaluation of which treatment is more compelling.

Question (20 marks): "Poems about loss succeed when they resist comforting us." Compare two studied poems.
Assessment guidance: aligns to NESA Module A/B-style criteria: sustained comparison, integrated evidence, conceptual control. Verify at NESA.

Final self-check

Mark your timed essay against the criteria; identify your weakest formal element (meter? lineation?) and drill it next week.
