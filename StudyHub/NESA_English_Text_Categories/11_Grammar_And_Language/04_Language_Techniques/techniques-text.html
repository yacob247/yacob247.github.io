The Complete Guide to Language Techniques

Every major technique by family — with examples, effect vocabulary and Band 6 analysis formulas.

The golden rule: naming a technique earns nothing by itself. Marks come from effect + purpose + link to question. Every entry below ends with an analysis sentence you can adapt.

1. Figurative Language (Imagery Family)

Extended metaphor
Technique Definition Example / Source Band 6 analysis sentence
Simile Comparison using 'like'/'as' "My love is like a red, red rose" — Robert Burns (1794) "The simile naturalises passion, making love feel organic, seasonal, and doomed to wilt."
Metaphor Direct identification of unlike things "Hope is the thing with feathers" — Emily Dickinson "The metaphor gives hope a body — something that perches, sings, survives storms — making abstraction feel resilient."
Metaphor sustained across a text "All the world's a stage" — Shakespeare "By extending the theatrical conceit through seven ages, the speech makes life's roles feel scripted — agency itself is questioned."
Personification Human traits given to non-human things "Death lays his icy hand on kings" — James Shirley "Personifying death as a cold aristocrat democratises mortality while chilling it."
Symbolism Object standing for larger idea Gatsby's green light — F. Scott Fitzgerald (1925) "The green light condenses longing into a single luminous point the reader can watch yearn."
Motif Recurring image/idea across a text Blood in Macbeth "Each return of blood thickens guilt until the motif becomes the play's conscience."
Oxymoron Contradiction compressed into phrase "Parting is such sweet sorrow" — Romeo and Juliet "The oxymoron holds love's two truths in one breath — pleasure and pain inseparable."
Paradox Seemingly impossible truth "I must be cruel only to be kind" — Hamlet "The paradox forces readers past surface logic toward moral complexity."
Hyperbole Deliberate exaggeration "I've told you a million times" "Hyperbole registers emotional intensity rather than factual claim — read the feeling, not the number."
Allusion Reference to another text/event T.S. Eliot's The Waste Land (1922) alludes throughout to Dante and myth. "The allusion imports an entire tradition's authority into a few words."
Pathetic fallacy Nature mirroring mood Storms at regicide in Macbeth "The disturbed cosmos externalises moral disorder."
Juxtaposition Placing contrasts side by side Wealth/poverty montage openings "Juxtaposition argues without commentary — the gap between images is the meaning."
Irony (verbal/dramatic/situational) Gap between appearance and reality Dramatic irony in Oedipus "Dramatic irony makes audiences complicit in a truth the speaker cannot see."
Sibilance Repetition of s-sounds (also sound family) "cease…sense…silence" "The hissing sibilance slithers under sense, unsettling as it soothes."

2. Sound Devices

Device Definition Effect vocabulary
Alliteration Repeated initial consonants ("wild wind whispers") murmuring, insistent, hypnotic
Assonance Repeated vowel sounds ("the light of fire is a sign") resonant, keening, mournful
Consonance Repeated internal/end consonants ("pitter patter") ticking, percussive, mechanical
Onomatopoeia Word imitating sound ("buzz", "crackle") vivid, immediate, sensory
Rhyme (end/internal) Sound echo binding, fateful, nursery-simple or ironic when forced
Rhythm/meter Patterned stress (iambic pentameter etc.) heartbeat, march, chant, breakdown when broken

3. Structural Techniques

  • Flashback/analepsis: reorders time to explain motive.
  • Foreshadowing: plants future dread; rewards rereading.
  • In medias res: opens mid-action; instant momentum.
  • Circular structure: ending echoes opening; fate implied.
  • Frame narrative: story inside story; reliability questioned (Wuthering Heights, Heart of Darkness).
  • Cliff-hanger/enigma: withheld information sustains engagement.
  • Shift/reversal (volta): pivot in argument or tone; in sonnets usually line 9.

5. Visual Techniques (for Images/Film/Multimodal)

  • Salience: what your eye lands on first; ask why it was made dominant.
  • Vectors: lines of gaze/motion directing attention.
  • Colour symbolism: red = danger/passion conventions — but note deliberate subversion.
  • Camera angle: low angle empowers subject; high angle diminishes; eye-level equalises.
  • Close-up vs wide shot: intimacy vs context/isolation.
  • Mise-en-scène: everything in frame as designed meaning.
  • Montage/cutting rhythm: juxtaposition over time.
  • Typography & layout (media): font weight, headline size = editorial voice before any word is read.

6. The Effect Vocabulary Bank

Precise effect verbs beat vague praise: foregrounds, marginalises, implicates, destabilises, naturalises, amplifies, collapses, suspends, mirrors, indicts, seduces, inoculates. Pair each technique with one of these verbs plus a reader-positioning clause ("positions the audience to…", "invites us to…", "forces recognition that…").
