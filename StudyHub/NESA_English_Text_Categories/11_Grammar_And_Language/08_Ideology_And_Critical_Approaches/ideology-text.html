The Complete Guide to Ideology & Critical Approaches

How texts encode ideology and how to read deliberately through the major critical lenses.

1. What Is Ideology?

An ideology is a system of values and assumptions about how the world works — and how it should. Texts are never neutral: every selection (whose story, whose vocabulary, what is left out) encodes ideology. Roland Barthes called the naturalising of ideology "myth": when a text presents its worldview as simple common sense, it has succeeded ideologically (Mythologies, 1957).

Key analytic move: ask of any text — What does this take for granted? Who benefits from that assumption being taken for granted? Who is absent or voiceless?

2. The Major Lenses

2.1 Marxist Criticism

Key thinkers: Marx & Engels (The Communist Manifesto, 1848); Antonio Gramsci on hegemony (consent manufactured rather than imposed); Louis Althusser on ideological state apparatuses.

Guiding questions: Who owns/controls production in this world? How does class shape speech, desire, fate? Does the text endorse or expose the economic order?

Worked example: In The Great Gatsby, Gatsby's wealth can never buy entry — East Egg's old money recognises new money instantly. Fitzgerald exposes class as performance plus pedigree: the American Dream's promise of mobility is shown as a myth that requires believers.

2.2 Feminist Criticism

Key thinkers: Virginia Woolf (A Room of One's Own, 1929); Simone de Beauvoir ("One is not born, but rather becomes, woman", The Second Sex, 1949); Judith Butler on gender performativity (1990).

Guiding questions: How are gender roles constructed, enforced or subverted? Who speaks, who is spoken about? What does the text assume about women's labour, desire, agency?

2.3 Postcolonial Criticism

Key thinkers: Frantz Fanon (The Wretched of the Earth, 1961); Edward Said (Orientalism, 1978) on how the West imagines 'the Orient'; Gayatri Spivak ("Can the Subaltern Speak?", 1988).

Guiding questions: How are colonised peoples represented — as subjects of their own stories or scenery in others'? What languages are privileged? What hybridity or resistance appears?

2.4 Ecocriticism

Key thinkers: Rachel Carson (Silent Spring, 1962) as cultural catalyst; Timothy Morton on 'dark ecology'.

Guiding questions: Is nature setting or actor? How does the text imagine human/non-human relations — dominion, stewardship, kinship? Where does environmental cost appear and who bears it?

2.5 Psychoanalytic Criticism

Key thinkers: Freud (the unconscious, repression, the uncanny — Das Unheimliche, 1919); Lacan (mirror stage, lack, desire).

Guiding questions: What is repressed and how does it return (symptom, dream, motif)? Where do characters act against stated desire?

2.6 Reader-Response Theory

Key thinkers: Wolfgang Iser (gaps readers fill); Stanley Fish (interpretive communities). Meaning happens in reading, not merely on page.

Guiding questions: What gaps does the text leave? How do different audiences legitimately read differently — and why?

3. Using Lenses Without Theory-Dumping

The cardinal error: writing "From a Marxist perspective…" then doing ordinary analysis with theory names sprinkled in. Markers call this name-dropping. Instead: let the lens sharpen ordinary technique analysis, and mention the theorist only when genuinely illuminating.

Weak vs strong lens use

  • Weak: "Said argues the Orient is constructed. This poem constructs nature similarly."
  • Strong: "The land exists grammatically only as Goal — cleared, fenced, named — never as Actor; Said's insight that representation licenses possession explains why this silencing matters."

4. Combining Lenses

Extension responses often braid two lenses: feminist + postcolonial (how colonialism restructures gender), Marxist + ecocritical (who profits from extraction). Keep one lens dominant so the argument stays coherent; use the second to complicate, not dilute.
