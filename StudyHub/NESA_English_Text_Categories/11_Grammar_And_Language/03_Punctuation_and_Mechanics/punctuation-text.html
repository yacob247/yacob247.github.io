The Complete Guide to Punctuation & Mechanics

Rules, exceptions and literary effects for every punctuation mark — plus capitals, spelling strategy, and exam-proof quoting mechanics.

1. The Full Stop (.)

Ends declarative sentences. Effect in literature: finality. Cormac McCarthy's near-abandonment of commas makes full stops carry enormous weight; a page of short declaratives reads like verdicts.

2. The Comma (,)

The most misused mark. Its four legitimate jobs:

  • List separator: "apples, pears and quinces" (the Oxford comma before "and" is optional but often clarifying: "I thank my parents, Beyoncé and God").
  • Joining independents with a coordinator: "She sang, and the room stilled."
  • Enclosing parentheticals: "The novel, published posthumously, shocked critics."
  • After introductory elements: "Exhausted by the march, the soldiers slept."
Never: join two independent clauses with only a comma (comma splice).

3. The Semicolon (;)

Joins two closely related independent clauses without a conjunction: "The war ended; the grief did not." Also separates complex list items. Literary effect: balanced tension — the semicolon holds two thoughts in the same breath so they argue.

4. The Colon (:)

Announces: a list, an explanation, a quotation. Rule of thumb: a complete clause must precede it. Effect: anticipation — whatever follows is framed as revelation or summary.

5. Dashes (— –)

Em dash: interruption, emphasis, dramatic aside — like this. Emily Dickinson capitalised on it: her dashes hold breath, refuse closure, keep poems perpetually unfinished. En dash: ranges (1914–1918) and compound modifiers (the Sydney–Melbourne route).

Because I could not stop for Death —
He kindly stopped for me —— Emily Dickinson, poem 479 (c. 186)

6. Apostrophes (')

  • Possession: singular "the boy's coat"; plural ending in s "the boys' coats"; irregular plural "children's books".
  • Contraction: "don't", "it's" (= it is). Never use apostrophes for simple plurals ("1980's" is wrong for decades in formal writing: write 1980s).
  • Its vs it's: possessive pronouns (its, hers, theirs) take no apostrophe.

7. Quotation Marks (' ' / " ")

Australian convention favours single quotes for direct speech with double quotes nested inside (and vice versa in US style). In essays, integrate quotes grammatically: The narrator admits she was "willing to deceive", rather than dumping quotes. Ellipsis (…) marks omission; square brackets [ ] mark your insertions or changes like [sic] or verb-tense adjustments.

8. Question Mark, Exclamation Mark, Hyphen

  • ? : direct questions only; indirect questions take a full stop ("He asked where she was.").
  • ! : ration it — one per essay maximum, if ever; overuse reads as hysteria.
  • - (hyphen): joins compound modifiers before nouns ("a well-known author") but not after ("the author is well known").

9. Brackets, Parentheses, Ellipsis

Parentheses whisper asides; brackets shout editorial interventions; ellipsis trails off — in poetry analysis, note when a text ends mid-thought and what that refusal to conclude means.

10. Capitalisation & Mechanics

  • Capitalise: sentence starts, proper nouns, titles of texts (italicise novels; quote poems/short stories/articles), first word of direct quotes.
  • Numbers: spell out below ten in prose; be consistent.
  • Spelling strategy: Australian English (analyse, colour, -ise endings). Build a personal error list from marked work.

11. Punctuation as Technique (Analysis)

Band 6 sample sentences

  • "Dickinson's end-stopped dashes withhold syntactic closure, enacting a speaker who cannot complete her own thought about death."
  • "McCarthy's refusal of inverted commas collapses narration into dialogue, denying readers any stable voice to trust."
  • "The colon before the final statistic frames data as revelation — the ad's entire persuasive architecture funnels to this announcement."
