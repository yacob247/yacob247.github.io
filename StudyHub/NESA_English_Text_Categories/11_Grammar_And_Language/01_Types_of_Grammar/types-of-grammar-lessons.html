Lesson 1 · Foundation level

What Grammar Is & Why It Matters

Intended learning: define grammar as a meaning-making system and explain why composers manipulate it.

Teaching: Grammar = word level + phrase level + clause level + text level. Deviation from the norm is itself meaningful (poets break rules against an expected pattern). Link to NESA outcome of understanding "language forms and features".

Stimulus: e.e. cummings, "anyone lived in a pretty how town" — notice nouns used as modifiers ("pretty how town").
He sang his didn't he danced his did.— E.E. Cummings, "anyone lived in a pretty how town" (1940)
Question (5 marks): Explain how cummings' grammatical deviation creates meaning in one line of the poem.

Starters: "By converting X into Y, the poet forces the reader to…" · "This deviation signals…"

Band 6 sample

Cummings converts adverbs ("didn't", "did") into nouns, so abstract behaviour becomes a possession someone can own. The reader must rebuild the grammar to read at all — and that rebuilding enacts the poem's theme that love is a private language, legible only to its participants.

Feature Effect
"converts adverbs into nouns" Precise grammatical naming — not vague 'wordplay'.
"reader must rebuild the grammar" Effect on reader explicitly stated.
  • I named a grammatical feature precisely.
  • I quoted evidence.
  • I explained effect on meaning/reader.

Worksheet 1

Rewrite "He ran quickly down the hill" three ways: change the verb, delete the adverb, front the adverbial. Note how each change alters pace and emphasis.

Lesson 2 · Core level

Traditional Grammar: Parts of Speech I

Intended learning: identify nouns, pronouns, verbs and their sub-types, and analyse their effects.

Teaching: Noun sub-types matter analytically: concrete vs abstract nouns track theme; collective nouns frame groups positively or negatively ("team" vs "mob"). Verb types: material (action), mental (thought), relational (being) — the seed of functional grammar.

Stimulus: Martin Luther King Jr., "I Have a Dream" (1963) — count abstract nouns in one paragraph.
Question (6 marks): Analyse how King's choice of noun and verb types constructs hope as achievable action.

Band 6 sample

King's dream is built from abstract nouns — justice, freedom, dignity — but each is anchored by material verbs: "we must rise", "hew out of the mountain of despair a stone of hope". The grammar refuses abstraction to remain passive; hope is something done.

  • I distinguished noun/verb sub-types.
  • I linked choices to purpose.
  • I quoted accurately.

Worksheet 2

Take any news paragraph. Highlight all verbs; classify them material/mental/relational; write one sentence about what the verb mix reveals.

Lesson 3 · Core level

Parts of Speech II & Phrase Types

Intended learning: classify adjectives, adverbs, prepositions, determiners and recognise phrase structures.

Teaching: Phrases are units around a head: NP ("the ruined chapel"), VP ("must have been waiting"), AdjP ("strangely calm"), PP ("beneath the floorboards"). Adjective order and stacking set tone; prepositional chains build cinematic space.

Question (6 marks): Explain how adjective stacking or prepositional layering in a given description shapes atmosphere.

  • I identified phrase heads correctly.
  • I explained tonal effect.

Worksheet 3

Describe your kitchen using: one stacked adjective sequence, two prepositional phrases, one sentence adverb. Then note the mood you created.

Lesson 4 · Core level

Prescriptive vs Descriptive Grammar

Intended learning: distinguish rules from conventions and analyse non-standard language respectfully and precisely.

Teaching: Prescriptive rules govern formal register; descriptive grammar records real usage. In texts, non-standard grammar constructs voice: dialect, idiolect, sociolect. Never mark a character's speech as "wrong" — analyse what it does.

Stimulus: dialogue excerpt using non-standard forms (e.g., Harper Lee's To Kill a Mockingbird, or an Australian text with Aboriginal English).
Question (7 marks): Analyse how a composer uses non-standard grammar to construct voice and identity.

Band 6 sample

The narrator's dropped auxiliaries ("she gone home") are not errors but signals: they locate the speaker inside a speech community whose trust the reader must earn. Standard narration frames this vernacular, letting the composer critique, rather than merely depict, who gets judged by their grammar.

  • I avoided deficit language ('bad English').
  • I connected grammar to identity construction.

Worksheet 4

List three prescriptive rules you were taught. For each, find a published author breaking it, and explain the effect.

Lesson 5 · Core level

Clauses: Independent & Dependent

Intended learning: identify clause types and explain how subordination backgrounds information.

Teaching: Independent clause = complete thought. Dependent clauses (adverbial, relative, nominal) cannot stand alone. Subordination creates hierarchy: main clauses carry foreground; subordinate clauses carry assumed truth. Compare "Although she lied, we forgave her" vs "We forgave her although she lied" — the concession shifts weight.

Question (6 marks): Using a supplied sentence pair, explain how moving the dependent clause changes emphasis.
  • I named clause types.
  • I explained emphasis shift.

Worksheet 5

Write five sentences each containing one independent and one dependent clause; underline clauses; note which idea feels dominant and why.

Lesson 6 · Advanced / Trial HSC level

Functional Grammar: Transitivity

Intended learning: analyse clauses into process/participants/circumstances and expose representation choices.

Teaching: Every clause answers: what's happening (process), who acts (Actor), what is affected (Goal), under what circumstances? Passive voice deletes Actors — crucial for media analysis: "Mistakes were made."

Stimulus: a news headline using passive voice, e.g., "Protesters shot by police" vs "Police shoot protesters".
Question (8 marks): Compare two headlines' transitivity choices and their ideological effect.

Band 6 sample

In the passive version the police appear only as an instrument ("by police"), while protesters retain Agent-like salience through position. The grammar redistributes blame before any fact is processed — evidence that syntax is never neutral.

  • I analysed process/participants accurately.
  • I explained ideological effect.

Worksheet 6

Rewrite five active headlines as passives and vice versa; annotate who becomes visible/invisible in each version.

Lesson 7 · Advanced / Trial HSC level

SFL: Modality & Theme

Intended learning: use modality (certainty) and theme/rheme analysis on persuasive texts.

Teaching: Modality scales from low ("might") to high ("must"); high modality asserts authority, low modality invites agreement. Theme = first element of the clause; it frames everything after. Speeches front inclusive pronouns for solidarity.

Stimulus: Greta Thunberg, UN Climate Action Summit speech (2019): "How dare you!" — high modality interrogative theme.
Question (8 marks): Analyse how modality and thematic choices position the audience.
  • I graded modality examples low/mid/high.
  • I identified themes of key clauses.
  • I linked to positioning.

Worksheet 7

Take a persuasive editorial; list every modal auxiliary; grade them; write a paragraph on the writer's certainty strategy.

Lesson 8 · Extension / Trial HSC level

Generative Insights: Ambiguity

Intended learning: explain structural ambiguity and analyse its deliberate literary/commercial uses.

Teaching: One surface string can map onto two deep structures: "Old men and women", "Flying planes can be dangerous". Poets and advertisers exploit ambiguity so multiple readings coexist.

Question (7 marks): Identify the two readings of a supplied ambiguous headline; explain why a writer might want both alive.
  • I paraphrased both readings clearly.
  • I justified the strategic value of ambiguity.

Worksheet 8

Find or invent three structurally ambiguous sentences; draw bracket diagrams showing both parses.

Lesson 9 · Trial HSC / Band 6 level

Grammar in Analysis: Band 6 Sentences

Intended learning: convert grammatical observation into analytical sentences that score marks.

Teaching: Formula: Feature → Evidence → Effect → Link (F-E-L). Ban "uses" alone; upgrade verbs: foregrounds, suppresses, juxtaposes, presupposes, modalises.

Model bank

  • "The fronted temporal adverbial ('After the funeral') presupposes grief as context, positioning readers within loss before characterisation begins."
  • "High-modality deontic verbs ('must', 'shall') cast the speaker as moral authority rather than participant."
Question (10 marks): Write four F-E-L sentences on any studied text, each using a different grammatical feature.
  • No sentence merely names a technique without effect.
  • Each links to a thesis-worthy idea.

Worksheet 9

Upgrade five weak sentences ("The author uses metaphors") into F-E-L analyses.

Lesson 10 · Trial HSC / consolidation

Editing with Grammar & Self-Assessment

Intended learning: edit drafts for comma splices, agreement, tense consistency; self-mark against criteria.

Teaching: Three-pass edit: argument, evidence, expression. Hunt: subject–verb agreement across long subjects, comma splices, dangling modifiers, inconsistent literary present tense.

Question (10 marks): Edit a supplied flawed paragraph; justify each correction in one line.
  • All splices fixed with correct punctuation.
  • Tense consistent in literary present.
  • Corrections justified, not guessed.
Syllabus alignment: matches NESA K–10 grammar outcomes and Stage 6 rubric demand for "sustained, controlled writing". Verify current wording at NESA.

Worksheet 10 — Final self-check

Mark your last essay against Lesson 9's model bank and list two grammar habits to fix before trials.
