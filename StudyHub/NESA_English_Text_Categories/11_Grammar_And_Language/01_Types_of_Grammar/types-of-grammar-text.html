The Complete Guide to Grammar & Its Types

What grammar is, the six traditions of grammar, word classes, phrases, clauses, functional grammar for analysis — with examples, citations and Band 6 application.

How to use this guide: Read sections in order the first time. Use the tables as reference during essay writing. Every section ends with a "Band 6 use" note showing how to turn grammar knowledge into analysis marks.

1. What Is Grammar?

Grammar is the system by which words combine into phrases, clauses and sentences to make meaning. It is not a set of arbitrary prohibitions; it is the operating system of English. When a poet breaks a rule deliberately — e.e. cummings writing "he danced his did" — the deviation only means something because the system exists. That is why NESA frames grammar knowledge as analytic power: students who can name what a sentence is doing can explain precisely how a text positions them.

Grammar operates at four levels. At the word level, we classify items into word classes (nouns, verbs, adjectives). At the phrase level, words group into units built around a head (noun phrase, verb phrase). At the clause level, phrases combine around a verb into clauses with functions (subject, object, complement). At the text level, clauses chain through cohesion devices (pronouns, conjunctions, lexical repetition) into paragraphs and whole texts.

Worked micro-example

Sentence: "Exhausted, the runner dragged herself toward the line."

  • Word level: "exhausted" is a participial adjective; "dragged" is a past-tense verb.
  • Phrase level: "the runner" = noun phrase (head noun "runner").
  • Clause level: one main clause; subject "the runner"; the fronted adjective phrase adds circumstance.
  • Text level: the pronoun "herself" ties back to "runner", creating cohesion.

Band 6 use: "By fronting the participial adjective 'exhausted', the writer foregrounds the runner's physical state before her action, positioning readers to admire endurance rather than speed."

Language most shows a man: speak, that I may see thee.— Ben Jonson, Timber, or Discoveries (1640)

2. The Six Major Types of Grammar

"Grammar" means different things in different traditions. Knowing the six types lets you answer the questions every senior student must face: which rules are real, which are conventions, and how do I use grammar analytically?

2.1 Traditional (School) Grammar

Descended from Latin teaching via eighteenth-century grammarians such as Lindley Murray (English Grammar, 1795), traditional grammar classifies the eight parts of speech: noun, pronoun, verb, adverb, adjective, preposition, conjunction, interjection. It treats the sentence as subject + predicate and judges constructions against Latinate norms.

  • Use it when: identifying word classes quickly, labelling parts of a sentence, or teaching foundational terminology.
  • Limitation: Latin categories fit English poorly (English has no future-tense inflection; "will go" uses an auxiliary).

2.2 Prescriptive Grammar

Prescriptive grammar states how language should be used: avoid double negatives, don't split infinitives, "between you and me" not "between you and I". Its authority is convention — often codified by usage guides like Fowler's Dictionary of Modern English Usage (1926).

  • Use it when: editing formal essays, résumés or exam responses where standard register earns credibility.
  • Critical insight: many prescriptive rules were borrowed from Latin on stylistic grounds. Splitting an infinitive ("to boldly go") was never ungrammatical — Star Trek made it iconic.

2.3 Descriptive Grammar

Descriptive grammar records how speakers actually use language without judgement. Modern linguists (from Leonard Bloomfield's Language, 193, onward) treat every systematic dialect — Aboriginal English, African American Vernacular English, Australian slang — as grammatically complete.

  • Use it when: analysing dialogue, voice and identity in texts; explaining why a character's non-standard speech carries meaning.
  • Band 6 use: "The composer's shift into colloquial syntax ('dunno, mate') constructs authenticity and class solidarity, inviting the reader to trust the speaker's perspective."

2.4 Functional Grammar

Functional grammar asks what each unit does. A clause is analysed into process (the verb), participants (who/what), and circumstances (when/where/how) — the "transitivity" system developed within Halliday's systemic-functional linguistics.

  • Use it when: analysing representation. Who acts? Who receives action? What is erased?
  • Example: compare "The government cut funding" (actor + material process + goal) with passive "Funding was cut" (agent deleted). Functional analysis exposes how passives hide responsibility — a staple of media analysis in Standard and Advanced Modules.

2.5 Systemic-Functional Linguistics (SFL)

Halliday's full framework (An Introduction to Functional Grammar, 1985) holds that every clause performs three meanings at once: the ideational (representing experience), the interpersonal (negotiating relationships — mood, modality: "might", "must"), and the textual (organising information via theme and rheme — whatever comes first frames the message).

  • Use it when: deconstructing persuasive and media texts; modality analysis ("we must act", "it seems possible") reveals degrees of certainty writers want audiences to share.
  • Why NESA likes it: SFL vocabulary appears throughout the K–10 syllabus's language features strand because it links form directly to purpose and audience.

2.6 Generative Grammar

Noam Chomsky (Syntactic Structures, 1957) proposed that humans possess an innate capacity generating infinitely many sentences from finite rules. Generative grammar models deep structure vs surface structure: "Visiting relatives can be boring" has one surface form but two underlying structures.

  • Use it when: understanding ambiguity in poetry and advertising — deliberate syntactic ambiguity is a technique, not an accident.
  • Band 6 use: "The slogan's ambiguous syntax permits two readings, letting each audience segment hear the promise they prefer."

4. Phrases and Clauses

A phrase is a group of words built around a head word without a subject–verb pair: the crumbling lighthouse (noun phrase), had been singing (verb phrase), wildly proud of her (adjective phrase). A clause contains a subject–verb pair. Independent clauses stand alone; dependent (subordinate) clauses cannot: "Although the storm passed" leaves us waiting.

Sentence architecture follows from clause combination — covered fully in the Sentence Structure pack. Here, note the analytic payoff: subordination creates hierarchy of importance (what is subordinate is backgrounded), and coordination creates equality or relentless accumulation (polysyndeton).

Common confusions: "Me and him went" (pronoun case — should be "He and I went"); dangling modifiers ("Walking home, the rain soaked me" — the rain wasn't walking); comma splices joining two independent clauses with only a comma.

5. Grammar as Analysis: The Band 6 Method

Grammar becomes marks when paired with effect and purpose. The formula: Name the feature → quote the evidence → explain the effect on meaning/reader → link to the question.

Sample Band 6 sentence using functional grammar

"Halliday's transitivity reveals the poem's politics: where the coloniser appears as Actor in active material processes ('he cleared, he drained, he fenced'), the land appears only as Goal, acted upon and voiceless — a grammatical silencing that mirrors its historical one."
