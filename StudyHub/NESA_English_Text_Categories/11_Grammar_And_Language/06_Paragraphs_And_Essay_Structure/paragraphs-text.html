The Complete Guide to Paragraphs & Essay Architecture

Paragraph formulas compared, thesis engineering, introductions and conclusions, cohesion and flow.

1. The Paragraph Formulas Compared

Formula Letters Best for Watch out for
TEEL Topic, Evidence, Explanation, Link Analytical essays (Standard/Advanced) Mechanical 'L' that just repeats the T
PEEL/PEAL Point, Evidence, Analysis/explain, Link Same as TEEL; Point should be an argument Treating P as topic label ("Themes of power")
PETAL Point, Evidence, Technique, Analysis, Link Forcing technique naming early in training Technique-t becomes a checklist item
IEL/SEA Idea, Evidence, Analysis (+ so what?) Sophisticated senior writing Requires confidence without scaffolding letters

All formulas are training wheels. Band 6 writing keeps their logic — claim, evidence, effect — while sounding like a person thinking.

2. Topic Sentences That Argue

A weak topic sentence labels: "Shakespeare uses imagery." A strong one claims: "The play's imagery of disease makes ambition itself seem contagious." Test every topic sentence with two questions:

  • Does it contain a claim someone could dispute?
  • Does it answer the question's key words directly?

3. Thesis Engineering

A thesis is a contestable answer to the question, not a restatement. Build it in three moves:

  1. Unpack the question: circle the directive (analyse/explain/to what extent) and the concept words.
  2. Take a position: agree, complicate, or refute — "to a large extent, but…" is fine if earned.
  3. Roadmap briefly: preview your two-to-four body arguments in one sentence.

Thesis upgrade ladder

  • L1 (topic): "This essay will discuss identity in the novel." — no marks for this.
  • L2 (position): "The novel presents identity as unstable." — better but generic.
  • L3 (argument): "By repeatedly staging identity as performance its characters cannot rehearse, the novel argues that belonging is not found but fabricated — and fabrication has costs." — Band 6.

4. Introductions

Keep them short (3–5 sentences): conceptual hook (optional), thesis, roadmap. Never define dictionary terms; never begin "Since the dawn of time". A strong opening move names the tension your essay explores.

Banned openers: dictionary definitions, rhetorical questions used as hooks in essays, plot summary, "In today's society…", "Throughout history…".

5. Body Paragraphs: The Full Anatomy

  1. Topic sentence — mini-thesis answering the question.
  2. Evidence 1 — short integrated quote + technique named.
  3. Analysis — effect on meaning/reader; how it proves the topic sentence.
  4. Evidence 2 (deepen) — second example extending or complicating point 1.
  5. Analysis — including nuance: does evidence 2 qualify or sharpen?
  6. Link — return to thesis AND question wording.

6. Conclusions

No new evidence. Do: restate thesis in evolved form, synthesise the body arguments into one insight, end on significance ("why this text still matters"). One strong final sentence beats three summary ones.

7. Cohesion & Flow

  • Cohesive ties: pronoun chains ("the composer… he… his verse"), lexical repetition of key concept words, synonym variation.
  • Signposting: "More troubling than…", "This failure of… points to…" — sentences that carry readers between paragraphs.
  • Old-to-new principle: start each sentence with known information, end with new — the single biggest readability upgrade available.
  • Question anchoring: reuse the question's exact key words once per paragraph.

8. Beyond TEEL: Discursive & Reflective Structures

Discursive writing (Standard Module C favourite) wanders deliberately — anecdotes, perspectives, voice — while holding a controlling idea. Reflective writing narrates your own process: situation, choice, evaluation. Both still need cohesion; they trade signposting for voice.

Fully annotated Band 6 paragraph

[Topic] The novel indicts not violence but the silence that licenses it. [Evidence+technique] When the crowd "turns, as one body, and looks away," the collective verb transforms omission into choreography. [Analysis] Complicity is shown being performed — guilt as action, not accident — which prepares the reader to judge bystanders as severely as perpetrators later in the narrative. [Deepening evidence] The town's single lamplighter, who "walks his rounds but lights nothing," literalises this economy of withheld action. [Analysis] His futile routine makes negligence visible: even duty performed can be duty betrayed. [Link] Thus the novel's true antagonist is never named on any page — it is the quiet agreement to look away.
