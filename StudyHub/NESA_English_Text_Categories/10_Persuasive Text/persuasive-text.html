The Complete Guide to Persuasive Writing

A step-by-step, from-the-basics course in how to write a Band 6 persuasive text — with starter sentences, the full structure, and two fully-annotated full-mark samples.

How to use this guide: Work through it in order. This is a teaching guide, not a revision sheet — start at "the very basics" and build up. Everything here is designed for students who have never formally been taught persuasive writing, all the way to full-mark HSC standard. The Very Basics: What Is Persuasive Writing?

Persuasive writing is writing whose job is to make the reader agree with you and, usually, do something about it. When you try to convince a parent to let you stay out later, or argue a friend into trying a new restaurant, you are already being persuasive. Writing it down is the same skill, made careful and deliberate.

The key difference from other kinds of writing is purpose. An informative text tells you facts. A discursive text explores an idea from both sides. But a persuasive text takes one side and fights for it. It does not sit on the fence. It says: "Here is what you should believe, and here is why."

In the NSW HSC, persuasive writing is assessed within Module C: The Craft of Writing and related components. A full-mark (Band 6) persuasive text is one that is clear (the reader always knows your position), sustained (it stays persuasive from first line to last), and sophisticated (the language and technique are deliberate and powerful).

The Building Blocks You Must Know First

Before you can write persuasively, you need four concepts. Learn these and everything else clicks into place.

1. The Contention (your "thesis")

The contention is your single main argument — the one-sentence answer to "what are you trying to prove?" Every sentence in a persuasive text serves this contention. If a sentence does not support it, cut it. Example: "Australia should make renewable energy its top priority."

2. The Audience (who you are talking to)

Persuasion works on a specific audience. You persuade a teenager and a politician differently because they care about different things. Before writing ask: who am I addressing, what do they already believe, and what will move them?

3. The Purpose (what you want to happen)

Your purpose might be to convince (change a mind), to call to action (get something done), or to warn or advocate. Knowing your purpose shapes your tone and your ending.

4. The Register (the "voice" of persuasion)

The persuasive register is confident, direct and emotive. It uses you to address the reader, strong verbs, and rhetorical devices. It is the opposite of the hesitant, hedged voice of discursive writing ("perhaps," "on the other hand").

Persuasive vs. Discursive: Do Not Confuse Them

This confusion loses more marks than almost anything else in Module C. If the task asks for persuasive and you write discursive — or vice versa — you are marking yourself down before a single word is judged, because the whole approach is wrong.

Starter Sentences (Your "First Lines" Kit)

Great persuasive writers hook the reader in the first line and land their contention quickly. Below is a bank of beginnings you can adapt to almost any topic. Pick one, put your own idea into the gap, and adjust the tone to your audience.

A bank of ready-to-adapt openings

  • "It is time we stopped pretending that…"
  • "Every day, ________; every day, we do nothing."
  • "Imagine a world where…"
  • "The evidence is in, and it is unmistakable: …"
  • "We cannot keep looking the other way."
  • "Ask yourself this: if not now, when?"
  • "They told us it could not be done. They were wrong."
  • "There is a quiet injustice happening all around us — and it is time we named it."
  • "For too long, we have accepted…"
  • "History will judge us not by what we said, but by what we did."
  • "The numbers are simple; the choice is harder."
  • "This is not about politics. It is about people."
  • "We have the power to change this — if only we dare."
  • "Would you accept this for yourself? Then do not accept it for others."
How to use them: These openers work because they either provoke emotion (pathos), assert authority (ethos), or point to logic (logos). Notice which one you have used and make sure the rest of your piece matches that appeal.

The Structure of a Persuasive Text

A persuasive text is built like a case presented in court: you open, you present your evidence, you deal with the other side, and you close with a demand. Follow this six-part shape for a full-mark response.

Stage 1 — Engaging Opening

Hook the reader with a vivid image, a daring statement, a question or a statistic, then state your contention clearly. The reader should know your position within the first two sentences.

Stage 2 — Context / Background

Briefly establish the issue so the reader understands why it matters now. Keep this short — it is scene-setting, not the argument itself.

Stage 3 — Arguments

Present your points, one per paragraph, in order of strength. Each paragraph: make the point, support it with evidence, and explain why it proves your contention (claim + evidence + reasoning).

Stage 4 — Counterargument & Refutation

Fairly acknowledge the opposing view, then dismantle it with logic or evidence. This is your "yes, but" moment and it makes your case stronger and more credible.

Stage 5 — Reinforcement

Return to your strongest appeal — the one that lands hardest — and restate your contention with fresh, powerful language.

Stage 6 — Call to Action / Emphatic Conclusion

End decisively. Restate the contention, and tell the reader exactly what you want them to believe, feel or do. A persuasive ending drives; it never drifts.

Remember: Persuasive structure culminates in a call to action. Discursive structure culminates in a reflective landing. The endings are where the two forms most clearly diverge.

Key Persuasive Techniques

A persuasive piece builds its case through rhetoric — the art of using language to persuade. The classical framework of ethos, pathos and logos is the backbone. Learn these, then add the devices that bring them to life.

The three appeals

  • Ethos — appeal to credibility and character. The writer establishes authority, trustworthiness and goodwill. Achieved through a confident, fair, measured tone.
  • Pathos — appeal to emotion. Vivid language, anecdote and imagery make the reader feel — anger, hope, fear, compassion.
  • Logos — appeal to logic and reason. Statistics, evidence and careful reasoning build a rational, unanswerable case.

The best persuasive writers weave all three together. A purely emotional piece feels manipulative; a purely logical one feels cold. Balance is the mark of sophistication.

Rhetorical devices (the tools)

  • Rhetorical questions — questions with an implied, obvious answer ("How many more must suffer?").
  • Anaphora / repetition — repeating a phrase for emphasis and rhythm ("We demand… We demand… We demand…").
  • Emotive language — charged words that sway feeling (choosing "tragedy" over "problem").
  • Rule of three — a list of three for rhythm and memorability ("life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness").
  • Direct address — speaking to "you" to draw the reader in and make the issue personal.
  • Hyperbole — deliberate exaggeration to stress a point.
  • Connotation — words that carry positive or negative associations.
  • Metaphor / simile — comparisons that make ideas vivid and persuasive.
Band 6 marker: A device is only worth marks if it is purposeful. Never decorate — every technique should make the reader more likely to agree, feel or act.

Two Full-Mark Samples, Annotated Line by Line

These two complete samples are written at Band 6 (full-mark) standard. This is like looking over a top student's shoulder — learn the moves, then make them your own.

Sample One: "Leave No Child Behind" (a persuasive opinion piece)

1 · Hook + contention

Every day, thousands of Australian children sit in classrooms where the roof leaks, the books are rationed, and the future is decided by postcode. It is time we stopped pretending that every child is given a fair start — and time we did something about it.

Why this works: The opening paints a powerful, concrete image (pathos) and immediately states the contention ("we stopped pretending… did something"). Two sentences accomplish both the hook and the thesis — the reader knows the position instantly. The word "pretending" challenges the reader and dares them to disagree.
2 · Direct address

You may believe the gap is someone else's problem. You may tell yourself that some children simply work harder, that opportunity finds those who deserve it. You would be wrong.

Why this works: "You" forces the reader to confront their own excuses (direct address, ethos). The three short clauses — "You may… You may… You would be wrong" — build a rhythm (rule of three) that lands like a verdict. The writer sounds fair yet firm, which builds credibility.
3 · Logos / evidence

The evidence is overwhelming. Study after study shows that a child's school results track their family's income more closely than their effort — that a child born into poverty is already behind before their first day of school, and that the gap only widens with every passing year.

Why this works: This is pure logos. The writer cites "study after study" and a clear, emotional-but-logical claim (results track income, not effort). The repeated "that" clauses build to a devastating point, backing the emotion of the opening with reason.
4 · Counterargument

Some will argue that money does not fix everything, that we cannot simply throw resources at the problem. They are partly right — funding alone is not enough. But to use that truth as an excuse for doing nothing is to abandon the very children we claim to care about.

Why this works: The writer fairly acknowledges the objection ("they are partly right") — this is strong ethos, showing the writer is reasonable. Then it pivots ("but") to refute it, exposing the objection as an excuse. This makes the argument almost impossible to attack.
5 · Reinforcement

Every child deserves a fair start — not because it is efficient, though it is; not because it is good economics, though it is; but because it is simply right.

Why this works: Anaphora ("not because… not because… but because") builds rhetorical momentum and lands on the moral heart of the argument. The rule of three tightening into a single moral claim is a hallmark of sophisticated persuasion.
6 · Call to action

So the question is not whether we can afford to give every child a fair start. It is whether we can afford not to. Write to your member. Vote. Speak up. Because the children sitting in those leaking classrooms are not statistics — they are us, only smaller, and they are waiting.

Why this works: The conclusion re-frames the issue ("not whether we can afford… but whether we can afford not to") and issues a direct call to action ("Write… Vote… Speak up"). The final image — "they are us, only smaller" — delivers a powerful emotional close (pathos) that lingers.

Sample Two: "Our Future in Our Hands" (a persuasive speech excerpt)

1 · Anecdotal hook

Two years ago, my grandfather showed me a photograph of this school, taken the day it opened — and in it, a classroom full of bright, hopeful students, every one of them certain their future would be brighter than their parents'. Today, I stand before you and ask: what happened to that promise?

Why this works: A personal anecdote (pathos) makes the issue feel real and urgent, and the ending question (a rhetorical question) turns the listener into a participant. The "promise" gives the speech a moral thread to pull through the whole piece.
2 · Contention + ethos

We have a choice to make, and we must not pretend otherwise. I say to you plainly: the future is not something that happens to us — it is something we build, starting now, starting here.

Why this works: The contention is stated with absolute confidence ("I say to you plainly"), building ethos. The claim "the future is not something that happens to us — it is what we build" is a memorable, quotable line that frames the whole argument as a call to agency.
3 · Logos / example

Consider what we know. When our town invested in early education, attendance rose, results rose, and the youth crime rate fell by a fifth within three years. That is not opinion. That is a fact, and it is a fact we can choose to repeat.

Why this works: A concrete local example (logos) grounds the argument in reality. The sharp contrast — "That is not opinion. That is a fact" — is emphatic and persuasive, and the final clause turns the evidence into a choice the audience can make. This keeps the logic driving forward.
4 · Counterargument

Some will say we cannot afford it, that our resources are finite. Finite they are; excuses, however, are infinite. The truth is that we cannot afford the future we are currently building — a future of lost potential, of promise broken.

Why this works: The writer concedes real limits ("finite they are") before striking back with a memorable aphorism: "Finite they are; excuses, however, are infinite." The balanced antithesis is elegant and devastating, reframing the cost objection as empty excuse-making.
5 · Reinforcement

My grandfather's classmates did not wait for permission to build a better future. They built it. Now it is our turn — and the eyes of the next generation are on us.

Why this works: The speech returns to the opening anecdote ("my grandfather's classmates"), creating structural unity. The short, punchy sentences ("They built it.") and the final image of "the eyes of the next generation" on the audience deepen the pathos and moral weight at the climax.
6 · Call to action

So I ask you, when you leave this hall: will you be the generation that waited — or the generation that built? Stand with us. Sign the pledge. Make the promise real. The future is not a gift; it is a choice, and the choice begins now.

Why this works: The closing returns to the choice framing from the start (unity), issues clear calls to action ("Stand with us. Sign the pledge."), and ends on a ringing, quotable line: "The future is not a gift; it is a choice." It is emphatic, personal, and impossible to ignore — the definition of a persuasive ending.

What Both Samples Share (the Band 6 recipe)

  • A clear contention in the first lines — the reader always knows the position.
  • A confident persuasive register — direct, emotive, never hesitant.
  • All three appeals balanced — ethos (fair, credible), pathos (human, moving), logos (evidence, reason).
  • Purposeful rhetorical devices — anaphora, rule of three, rhetorical questions, antithesis — each earning its place.
  • A fair counterargument, then a powerful refutation — the "yes, but" that makes the case credible.
  • Structural unity — the ending returns to the opening image or idea.
  • An emphatic call to action — the piece ends by demanding belief or action, never drifting.
