10-Lesson Nonfiction Program

Ten exam-ready lessons covering essays, speeches, biography, memoir and journalism — each with teaching content, a stimulus, an exam-style question, starter sentences, a Band 6 sample answer, annotation and self-check criteria.

Program map (each ±45–60 minutes)
  1. Recognising Nonfiction Forms
  2. Facts, Opinions & Judgement
  3. Ethos, Pathos & Logos
  4. Voice & Authorial Presence
  5. The Essay & Structure

  7. Memoir & Personal Truth
  8. Journalism: News vs Opinion
  9. Detecting Bias & Misinformation

Lesson 1 · 55 min

Recognising Nonfiction Forms

Intended learning: distinguish essay, speech, biography, memoir and journalism by purpose and features.

Teaching content

Nonfiction is a broad family held together by one promise: it deals with the real world. But the family has distinct forms, each with its own contract with the reader. The essay explores an idea; the speech persuades an audience in time; biography tells another person’s life; memoir tells the writer’s own life through reflection; journalism reports events with evidence and attribution. Teach students to identify a form by its purpose, voice and structure, and to notice that many real texts blend forms.

Stimulus: five short extracts — one essay, one speech, one biography, one memoir, one news report — on overlapping topics.
Question (6 marks · ~12 min): Identify the form of each extract and justify your answer using purpose, voice and structure.

Starter sentences

“The text’s purpose is clearly to…” · “Typical of the essay form, this piece…” · “The first-person reflection signals a…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

Extract A is an essay: its purpose is exploratory rather than conclusive, its voice measured and speculative, and its structure moves through linked reflections rather than a single argument or a chronological life. Extract D is a memoir because it narrates the writer’s own remembered experience in the first person and openly acknowledges the limits of memory — a hallmark of reflective life writing. The others fit the speech (direct address, anaphora), biography (third person, external authority), and news report (inverted pyramid, attribution). Recognising the form is not a label for its own sake; it tells us which contract with the reader we should judge the text by.

  • I named each form.
  • I justified with purpose, voice or structure.
  • I explained why the form matters.

Worksheet — Lesson 1

Collect one example of each form and note the cues that identify it.

Lesson 2 · 55 min

Facts, Opinions & Judgement

Intended learning: distinguish verifiable fact, evaluative judgement and reasoned inference.

Teaching content

Nonfiction’s power lies partly in how it blurs fact and judgement. A fact can be verified; an opinion is a preference; a judgement is an evaluation that rests on criteria; an inference draws a conclusion from evidence. The most persuasive nonfiction lets an implied judgement ride on a set of selected facts, so teaching students to separate the two is critical literacy in action.

Stimulus: a paragraph about a proposed development that mixes statistics, stated opinion and implied judgement.
Question (8 marks · ~15 min): Classify the claims as fact, opinion, judgement or inference, and analyse how the mixing positions the reader.

Starter sentences

“While the claim that… is factual, the judgement that… rests on…” · “The writer implies…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

The passage mixes categories so skilfully that the reader feels persuaded without being able to say by what. The statement that the proposal adds “2,400 dwellings and a park” is factual and verifiable, yet its inclusion alongside “will transform the suburb” — a judgement — makes the transformation seem as certain as the count. The writer never states that the change is good; they select the facts and arrange the judgement so that the reader supplies it. Distinguishing these moves is exactly the critical skill nonfiction demands: naming what is asserted, what is inferred, and what is quietly assumed.

  • I classified each claim.
  • I analysed the effect of the mixing.
  • I moved beyond “it’s biased” to how.

Worksheet — Lesson 2

Colour-code a nonfiction extract: blue for fact, red for judgement, green for language that blurs the two.

Lesson 3 · 55 min

Ethos, Pathos & Logos

Intended learning: identify the three rhetorical appeals and analyse how their mix shapes argument and audience.

Teaching content

Nonfiction, especially speeches and opinion writing, persuades through Aristotle’s three appeals. Ethos builds the speaker’s credibility; pathos moves the emotions; logos convinces through reason and evidence. Band 6 responses do not simply label each appeal — they explain how the mix and sequence positions the audience and serves the text’s purpose.

Stimulus: an extract from a famous speech or essay with clear appeals (e.g. a rhetorical, impassioned and reasoned passage).
Question (10 marks · ~20 min): Analyse how the text blends ethos, pathos and logos, and evaluate the effect of the blend.

Starter sentences

“By appealing to pathos through…” · “The writer’s ethos rests on…” · “Sequencing reason after emotion…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

The passage is engineered so that each appeal prepares for the next. It opens with ethos — the speaker’s own experience is named, establishing the right to speak — then swells into pathos, using concrete, suffering detail to make the audience feel the stakes; only then does it offer logos, a clean statistic that gives the emotion a shape to act on. The sequence matters: pathos alone would seem manipulative, and logos alone cold. By moving from trust to feeling to reason, the text makes the audience want to believe the argument and then makes believing reasonable, which is the most durable form of persuasion.

  • I identified each appeal with evidence.
  • I analysed the sequence or balance.
  • I evaluated the effect on the audience.

Worksheet — Lesson 3

Annotate a speech, tagging each paragraph for ethos, pathos or logos, and note the pattern.

Lesson 4 · 55 min

Voice & Authorial Presence

Intended learning: analyse how an author’s voice, register and stance are constructed and positioned.

Teaching content

Every nonfiction writer projects a persona — chosen, not accidental. Teach the ingredients of voice: sentence rhythm, vocabulary register (formal/colloquial), tone (ironic, earnest, detached), and stance (how the writer stands in relation to the reader and subject). Ask who this voice makes the reader feel like — peer, student, ally, jury?

Stimulus: two writers on the same topic in markedly different voices (e.g. a formal essayist and a conversational commentator).
Question (8 marks · ~15 min): Compare the voices in the two texts and analyse what each stance invites from the reader.

Starter sentences

“The writer’s colloquial register positions the reader as…” · “An ironic tone undercuts…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

The two voices stage very different relationships. The essayist’s formal register and measured, longer sentences project authority and distance, inviting the reader to regard the subject with calm scrutiny as a fellow thinker. The commentator’s short, conversational sentences and direct address close that distance, making the reader a confidant rather than an observer. Neither is “better”; each constructs a reader the writer needs — the essay asks for judgement, the commentary for agreement. Analysing voice is analysing this invisible negotiation between writer and reader.

  • I named the ingredients of each voice.
  • I analysed the stance.
  • I said what each voice asks of the reader.

Worksheet — Lesson 4

Rewrite one sentence of an extract in a deliberately different voice and note the change in effect.

Lesson 5 · 55 min

The Essay & Structure

Intended learning: read and compose the essay as a structured exploration, and analyse its form.

Teaching content

The essay is the oldest and most flexible nonfiction form — from the personal to the argumentative, from the formal to the conversational. Teach the essay’s bones: a compelling opening that establishes an idea or a question, a body that develops it through linked points and evidence, and a conclusion that returns with new understanding. Analyse how essays use structure as meaning — a disruptive anecdote early, a concession in the middle, a circling rather than an ending.

Stimulus: an essay extract (personal or argumentative) with a distinctive structure.
Question (10 marks · ~20 min): Analyse how the essay’s structure produces its argument or feeling, and comment on its voice.

Starter sentences

“The essay opens on a question and…” · “By conceding the opposing view in the centre, the writer…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

The essay’s structure is quietly argumentative. It opens with a small domestic image — not a thesis — and lets the idea grow out of the image, so the reader experiences the essay’s thinking as discovery rather than as a position being defended. In the centre, the writer pauses to concede a counter-example, which costs little and buys trust, and the essay ends by returning to the opening image transformed. This circular structure turns the essay into a journey: the same image, seen with new understanding, is both the hook and the point. Structure here is not a container for an argument; it is the argument made visible.

  • I traced the structure.
  • I linked structure to meaning.
  • I commented on the voice or stance.

Worksheet — Lesson 5

Map an essay’s moves (opening, development, concession, conclusion) and name the effect of each.

Lesson 6 · 55 min

Biography & Shaping a Subject

Intended learning: analyse how biography selects and shapes a life, and evaluate its claims to truth.

Teaching content

Biography appears objective but is always a construction: the biographer selects events, sources and a narrative shape, making implicit judgements about what a life means. Teach students to ask: what is included and excluded, which sources are trusted, what organising story is told (triumph, tragedy, rise and fall) — and how the subject is “shaped” by these choices.

Stimulus: two accounts of the same figure that tell different stories about their life and legacy.
Question (10 marks · ~20 min): Compare how the two biographies shape the same subject, and analyse the consequences.

Starter sentences

“In shaping the subject as…” · “The selection of… positions the reader to…” · “This biographer’s lens…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

The two biographies demonstrate that a life is not narrated so much as selected. The first shapes its subject as a pioneering visionary, foregrounding the breakthrough year and drawing on admiring colleagues — the organising story is triumph. The second opens with the same figure’s failures and draws on private letters, organising the life as a story of giftedness thwarted by circumstance. Both are “truthful” in their facts, yet they produce different subjects, because each biographer’s selection and emphasis carry an unstated judgement. Reading biography well is reading this construction — noticing not only what a life was, but the story its teller chose to tell.

  • I identified the construction of each subject.
  • I analysed selection and emphasis.
  • I reflected on the claims to truth.

Worksheet — Lesson 6

Compare two short accounts of one person and list the differences in selection, emphasis and shape.

Lesson 7 · 55 min

Memoir & Personal Truth

Intended learning: analyse the subjective, reflective nature of memoir and its emotional truth.

Teaching content

Memoir is not autobiography’s completeness; it is a shape made from memory. Teach the hallmarks: first-person narration, reflection on memory’s fallibility, selection of formative moments, and emotional (rather than factual) truth. Ask what the writer is remembering into meaning — and how the act of reflection shapes the events being told.

Stimulus: a memoir extract in which the past tense is interrupted by the present — the remembering self commenting.
Question (10 marks · ~20 min): Evaluate the role of memory and reflection in the memoir extract, and what emotional truth it builds.

Starter sentences

“Narrated through the filter of memory…” · “The memoir’s emotional logic…” · “By openly noting, ‘I misremember,’ the writer…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

The memoir’s truth is emotional, and it is built on an honest admission of memory’s limits. The writer narrates the childhood event in the past tense, but keeps interrupting with a present-tense, reflective voice — “or so I now believe” — which does two things at once. It concedes that the memory may be shaped, and in so doing it makes the account more, not less, credible. More than that, the gaps in memory become the subject: what the child could not understand, the remembering adult now can, and the memoir is the meeting of those two selves. This is the emotional truth memoir offers — not accuracy about events, but honesty about their meaning.

  • I analysed memory and reflection.
  • I distinguished emotional from factual truth.
  • I connected technique to meaning.

Worksheet — Lesson 7

Draft a short remembered moment, then add a reflective sentence that shows the remembering self.

Lesson 8 · 55 min

Journalism: News vs Opinion

Intended learning: distinguish news reporting from opinion, and analyse sourcing and framing.

Teaching content

Journalism claims objectivity but is shaped by selection and framing. Teach the inverted pyramid and the discipline of attribution (who said it, and are they named?). Then distinguish news (reports fact, separates it from comment) from opinion (argues a case), and notice how the same event is framed differently — by word choice, source selection and what leads.

Stimulus: two articles reporting the same event — one news, one opinion — or two news frames of one story.
Question (10 marks · ~20 min): Compare how the two texts report or comment, analysing sourcing and framing.

Starter sentences

“This article’s sourcing reveals…” · “The placement of opinion in…” · “By leading with…, the report frames the story as…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

The two articles handle the same facts to opposite effect. The news report leads with the event, attributes every claim to a named source and places any interpretation after the attribution — it lets the reader see who is saying what. The opinion piece, by contrast, opens with a verdict and selects sources to support it, so the framing — not the facts alone — tells the story. Sourcing is the giveaway: a report that must name its informants is constrained to what it can verify, while commentary is free to select. Analysing journalism is therefore analysing control — who is quoted, what leads, what is left out — and news-versus-opinion is the first, indispensable cut.

  • I distinguished news from opinion.
  • I analysed sourcing and attribution.
  • I identified the framing of each text.

Worksheet — Lesson 8

Collect two reports of one event and annotate differences in lead, sources and word choice.

Lesson 9 · 55 min

Detecting Bias & Misinformation

Intended learning: evaluate credibility, identify bias and verify claims as a critical reader.

Teaching content

In an age of infinite information, verification is a core English skill. Teach the markers of bias (loaded language, omission, one-sided sourcing, emotional appeal without evidence) and the discipline of verification: triangulate with independent sources, trace claims to originals, check dates and authors, and be alert to manipulation designed to feel like fact. Model evaluating a text’s credibility holistically rather than dismissing anything online.

Stimulus: a short, persuasive-but-unverified news item, and the same claim reported by a credible source.
Question (10 marks · ~20 min): Evaluate the credibility of the first text and explain how you would verify it.

Starter sentences

“The loaded term ‘…’ implies…” · “By omitting… the text…” · “To verify, I would…”

Band 6 sample

Model response

The first text should raise immediate flags: it is unsigned, undated, and carries no verifiable sources, while its single emotional statistic is stated without context — markers of persuasion overriding reporting. Verification would begin by locating the original claim in a documented source, then triangulating across at least two independent, credible outlets, and checking whether the number is accurate in context rather than merely repeated. The loaded word “crisis” and the omission of counter-evidence do the work of bias, and naming those moves — rather than simply declaring the text “fake” — is what critical reading requires. Credibility is judged, not assumed.

  • I identified markers of bias precisely.
  • I gave a verification method.
  • I evaluated, not just dismissed.

Worksheet — Lesson 9

Fact-check a short news item using three independent sources and record your process.

Lesson 10 · 60 min

Composing Nonfiction & Self-Assessment

Intended learning: compose an original nonfiction piece and evaluate it against Band 6 criteria.

Teaching content

Bring the analysis together into composition. Choose a form — a persuasive feature, a personal essay, a short news report or a speech — and match it to audience and purpose. Plan a clear structure, blend ethos/pathos/logos with intent, and control your voice. Then self- and peer-assess against the criteria: clear purpose, balanced appeals, sophisticated structure and register.

Stimulus: a current issue or personal topic of your choice suitable for an opinion feature or speech.
Question (15 marks · ~30 min): Compose your piece, then write a short reflective evaluation of two deliberate choices.

Starter sentences (for the reflection)

“I chose a speech because…” · “To move my audience I…” · “My main structural risk was…”

Band 6 model (excerpt + reflection)

Model response

Composition: A speech urging fellow students to treat the school library as a civic space. Reflection: I chose a speech over an essay because the topic is about shared public life, and live address lets me include the audience in the argument — I open with a question they must feel they answer. I deliberately led with an anecdote (pathos) before any statistic (logos), because my audience is more likely to be moved into listening than argued into it. The risk I am aware of is sentiment; I mitigated it by anchoring each emotional moment with a concrete fact. Composing nonfiction forces me to make, consciously, the choices I have been learning to see in others’ texts.

  • Clear purpose matched to audience and form.
  • Effective blend of appeals and voice.
  • Sound structure and register.
  • Reflective evaluation shows control.

Final self-assessment

Mark your piece against the criteria in the guide and set one goal for next time.

Assessment guidance: align to the NESA Stage 6 English marking guidelines — reward a clear purpose, controlled voice, balanced use of evidence and appeals, and coherent structure. Confirm current requirements at NESA Stage 6 English.
