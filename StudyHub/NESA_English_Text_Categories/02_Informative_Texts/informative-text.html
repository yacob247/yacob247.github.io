What Informative Texts “Are” and Why They Matter

An informative text gives clear, accurate information about the world. Its primary purpose is to inform — not to entertain (imaginative) or to convince (persuasive), although a text may do all three with one dominant purpose. Across the NSW English years, informative texts include explanations, descriptions, factual recounts, news reports, procedures and journals.

Information is the currency of learning and of democracy. The student who can locate accurate facts, judge their reliability, organise them clearly and communicate them precisely can participate — in school, in work, in civic life. Conversely, the ability to write information well is inseparable from knowing it well: we understand a topic most deeply when we have had to explain it clearly to someone else.

Information in the NSW K–10 Syllabus

The NSW English K–10 Syllabus names informative texts — explanations, factual recounts, procedures, reports — as a core text type alongside imaginative and persuasive. Students are expected to respond to and compose information across the stages, moving from simple descriptions and recounts in the early years to structured reports, explanations and researched texts in Stages 4–5. The syllabus also foregrounds the skills of reading for information — locating, selecting, evaluating and synthesising — which are the reading half of the informative mode.

Teachers may also consult the Australian Curriculum: English, whose “texts in context” strand maps how informative text structure and language mature with age.

The Major Informative Forms

Informative texts come in distinct forms, each with its own structure and job. Naming the form is the first step to both reading and writing it. The table below is the essential reference:

FormPurposeStructureKey language features
ExplanationExplain how or why something happens.Introduction of phenomenon → sequenced causes/stages → conclusion.Causal language (because, therefore), technical terms, present tense.
DescriptionPicture a person, place, object or idea accurately.Introduction → organised details (parts, features, uses).Precise nouns and adjectives, comparison, classification.
Factual recountTell what happened in time order.Orientation (who/what/when/where) → events in sequence → personal or final comment.Time words (first, next), past tense, concrete details.
News reportReport a recent event concisely.Lead (who/what/when/where/why) → key facts → further detail.Inverted pyramid, objective voice, factual headline.
ProcedureGive steps to achieve a result.Goal → materials → numbered/sequenced steps.Imperatives (mix, pour), sequence markers, precise measurements.
Journal / enquiry recordRecord observations and questions over time.Dated entries, each with observation, question or finding.Dated structure, reflective voice, record of process.

Structures for Clear Information

Clarity in informative writing is mostly structure. Three organising patterns do most of the work:

  • Sequence (time order) — for recounts and procedures. Events or steps follow one another and connect with time markers. Great for “how” and “what happened.”
  • Cause and effect (logical order) — for explanations. The writer moves from cause to consequence, using causal connectives so the reader can follow the reasoning. Great for “why.”
  • Classification and comparison — for descriptions and reports. The writer groups features or compares cases to show relationships. Great for “what is it and how does it differ?”

A strong informative text also uses signposting: headings, topic sentences and paragraphing that let the reader navigate. And it uses support material — diagrams, labels, tables, maps and captions — because information often communicates best when words and images share the load. Choose the structure that matches the purpose, and the reader will feel the clarity before they can name it.

Language for Accuracy (with Band 6 analysis)

Where persuasive writing marshals emotion and imaginative writing courts atmosphere, informative writing prizes accuracy, clarity and objectivity. The table below pairs each feature with the top-band understanding of why it matters:

FeatureWhat it meansExampleBand 6 understanding
Precise vocabularyExact nouns/verbs instead of vague ones.“The temperature reached 38 degrees” not “it got really hot”.Precision removes ambiguity; the reader trusts what can be measured.
Objective voiceFocus on the topic, not the writer’s feelings.“The report found…”Keeping the subject, not the author, at the centre makes information seem disinterested and reliable.
Causal and sequence connectivesWords that signal logic and time.therefore, because, as a result · first, then, finallyConnectives carry the reader through the reasoning, so clarity is a grammar as much as a tone.
Technical terms (defined)Specialist vocabulary used correctly.“photosynthesis” then briefly defined.Technical language signals expertise, but the writer who defines it shows they respect the reader.
Support materialDiagrams, tables, labels, captions.A labelled diagram of a plant’s parts.Words and images share meaning; a strong text chooses the right mode for each fact.

Top students understand that “objective” is a craft: you choose the facts, the order and the words, so objectivity is a deliberate framing, not the absence of choices. Band 6 informative writing is transparent about what it omits and why — honesty is part of accuracy.

Research and Reliability

Informative composing depends on trustworthy sources. Teach the check: Who wrote it? Why? When? Is it backed up? Concretely, students should ask: Is the author or organisation credible? Does the source state its purpose? How recent is the information — and does it matter? Is the claim supported by evidence or by opinion dressed as fact? And is this the original source, or a summary of a summary? With the rise of digital information, evaluating reliability is one of the most important reading skills the informative mode teaches.

Worked Example, Band 6 Sample & Annotation

Below is a short, accurate explanatory text on a school-friendly phenomenon — why a beach ball floats but a rock sinks — written to a high standard, followed by annotation.

Model — “Why Some Things Float”

Objects float or sink because of density — how tightly an object’s matter is packed. Density is a measure of mass divided by volume: the amount of stuff in a space, compared with that space’s size. Water has a density of roughly one gram per cubic centimetre. A beach ball is mostly air, so its overall density is far lower than water’s, about a twentieth as dense; pushed under, the displaced water pushes back harder than the ball weighs, and up it comes. A rock, by contrast, squeezes the same material into a smaller space, so its density exceeds water’s — the water cannot push back enough, and down it sinks. This is why huge steel ships, filled with air, ride high while a single iron nail drops through the surface. As a result, we can predict floating or sinking by comparing densities alone.

Annotation — why it earns Band 6

FeatureTechniqueAnalysis
“how tightly its matter is packed”Precise, defined technical termDefines “density” plainly before using it, respecting the non-expert reader.
“mass divided by volume”Technical formula in wordsGives the exact basis so the explanation can be trusted, not just asserted.
“about a twentieth as dense”Precise comparisonA specific ratio makes an abstract idea graspable.
“steel ships… iron nail”Concrete exampleApplies the principle to a familiar pair, showing the writer can use, not just state, the idea.
“As a result… comparing densities alone”Causal conclusionEnds with the general rule, leaving the reader able to predict new cases.

Marking Criteria & How Examiners Judge Informative Writing

Across classroom and NESA-style assessments, informative writing is judged on a consistent skeleton, where accuracy and clarity outrank style:

CriterionBand 5–6 descriptorCommon gap
Factual accuracyCorrect, current and relevant factsVague, dated or invented detail
Clear structurePurpose-matched organisation with signpostingRandom order; missing headings
Precision of languageExact vocabulary; defined technical termsVague or imprecise wording
ObjectivityFocused on the topic; transparent choicesOpinion or emotive language creeping in
Use of support materialDiagrams, tables, labels that genuinely helpDecorative images that add nothing
Band 6 secret: clarity is a courtesy. The strongest informative writers put themselves in the reader’s shoes — they anticipate confusion and remove it before the reader meets it.

Responding To and Composing Information

Responding — read for information. A strong routine: (1) Locate the purpose and structure; (2) Select the key facts, not every fact; (3) Evaluate the source for reliability and bias; (4) Synthesise — put ideas together in your own words. This is the reading half of the mode.

Composing — the writing routine. Choose the form to match the purpose (explain, describe, recount, report, procedure); plan the structure (sequence, cause–effect, classification); gather accurate facts from reliable sources; draft with precision and signposting; then review for accuracy and clarity — and check your support material. Reward precision over padding: a short, exact text beats a long, vague one.

Exam-Style Questions & Model Responses

These questions and models show how informative writing and analysis are assessed — notice how a top response prizes accuracy, structure and reader-courtesy over ornament.

Question 1 (8 marks · ~15 minutes)

Read the given informational extract, then evaluate how effectively its structure and language achieve clarity for its intended audience.

Model response

The extract achieves clarity by matching structure to purpose and reader to language. Its classification structure — introducing the topic, then grouping features under headings — lets a reader who is skimming locate the relevant section in seconds, while a topic sentence at the head of each paragraph sets the expectation before the detail. The language is objective and precise: technical terms such as “habitat” and “migration” are used exactly and defined on first use, so the writer signals expertise without excluding the beginner. Where the text risks abstraction, a labelled diagram and a single statistic carry the idea visually and measurably. In short, every structural and linguistic choice serves the reader’s ease, which is the true test of an informative text’s effectiveness.

Question 2 (10 marks · ~20 minutes)

Write a short explanation of how a natural or social process works. Use a clear cause-and-effect structure, precise language, and one piece of support material (a diagram, table or label).

Model response

Why coastal dunes hold back the sea. A coastal dune is a hill of sand built and anchored by plants. First, wind lifts dry sand from the beach and drops it where an obstacle slows the air. Next, the sand piles into a ridge, and hardy grasses take root, their roots and leaves catching still more sand. As the dune grows, it becomes a natural breakwater: during storms, waves lose energy climbing it, so the land behind is protected from flooding. Precise words matter — “accumulates,” “anchored,” “absorbs energy” — because each one points to an exact action. A labelled cross-section of the dune, showing beach, dune crest and backshore, does in one image what a paragraph takes a minute to say, and completes the explanation for a visual reader.

StructurePurpose-matched organisation with signpostingClear order lets the reader follow without re-reading.
PrecisionExact vocabulary; defined termsExact words remove ambiguity and build trust.

Choosing the Right Form & the Informative-Writer’s Checklist

Choosing the wrong form is the most common error in informative composing. A writer who writes a procedure when the task needs an explanation, or a recount when it needs a report, has failed before writing a sentence. Teach students to decide by asking five questions:

  • Am I telling what happened? → a factual recount.
  • Am I explaining how or why something works? → an explanation.
  • Am I telling someone how to do something? → a procedure.
  • Am I picturing what something is? → a description.
  • Am I reporting a recent event? → a news report.

These five questions resolve most uncertainty, and they force students to think about purpose first — the same habit that underpins all clarity. Once the form is chosen, run the informative-writer’s checklist before handing in any piece:

CheckAsk yourselfThe result if you do it
AccuracyAre my facts right, current and sourced?A reader can trust and use what I wrote.
Purpose-matchIs the form the right one for my purpose?My structure does not fight my aim.
PrecisionAre my words exact and my terms defined?No ambiguity slows or misleads the reader.
SupportDoes a diagram, table or label help here?The clearest mode carries each idea.
ObjectivityHave I kept opinion out of the facts?The information stands on its own.
Reader-courtesyWould a new reader find this easy to follow?The text is clear not just accurate.

There is a gentle irony at the heart of the informative mode: the more carefully a writer takes it, the more invisible the craft becomes. A perfectly clear explanation reads as if it wrote itself, when in fact it is the product of relentless choices. Teaching students to value this invisible labour — and to recognise it in others — is one of the deepest lessons the informative text teaches.
