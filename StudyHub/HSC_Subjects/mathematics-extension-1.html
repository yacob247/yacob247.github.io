How to Get a Band E4 in Mathematics Extension 1

Extension 1 is not Band 6 — it is Band E4. And the paper is brutal in a specific way: 60 marks compressed into as few as 23 items, in 2 hours. This guide is about surviving that compression and proving, not just calculating.

01

Band E4: What It Actually Is

Start by correcting the vocabulary, because the terminology matters. Extension courses are not reported at Band 6. NESA explains that “For an Extension course, the bands are E4 (highest level of performance) to E1”3. So the goal here is Band E4 — colloquially still called “a Band 6”, but formally a different scale.

The other foundational rule is unchanged and applies in full:

“Your mark in each course is a 50:50 combination of your HSC exam mark and your school-based assessment mark.”

NESA, Understanding your HSC results3

Extension 1 is a 1-unit Year 12 elective. That has a real consequence: it is a smaller slice of your ATAR than a 2-unit course, but the difficulty per mark is higher. There are fewer marks available to hide behind. If you lose five marks in a 70-mark paper, that is 7.1% — where in Advanced, five marks is 5%. Compression rewards precision and punishes carelessness disproportionately.

The third paper you must sit

NESA is specific: “Candidates will also be required to complete either the Mathematics Advanced exam paper or the Mathematics Extension 2 exam paper, in addition to the Mathematics Extension 1 paper”1. Two papers in the same session. Practise that fatigue, because it is real and it costs you marks.

What assumed knowledge really costs you

NESA states that “The Mathematics Advanced course will be assumed knowledge for this exam” and that “The Mathematics Extension 1 Year 11 course may be examined”1. Advanced is therefore not optional background — it is examinable substrate. Students who take Extension 1 while struggling in Advanced make a compounding error, because Extension questions hide Advanced content inside unfamiliar wrappers.

Strategic implication. The fastest route to E4 is not more Extension 1 practice. It is Advanced fluency strong enough that Advanced content becomes invisible when it appears inside an Extension question. Diagnose your Advanced paper first, then repair.
02

The Compression Problem

Here is the arithmetic that makes Extension 1 different from every other mathematics paper in NSW, and that most students never explicitly calculate.

NESA specifies that Section II contains 23 to 28 items worth 60 marks, with at least one item worth 4 or 5 marks1. Take the favourable end: 23 items for 60 marks is an average of 2.61 marks per item. Compare Mathematics Advanced, where 37–42 items carry 90 marks — about 2.25 marks per item4.

Two consequences follow, and both are about time:

  • Less slack per item. Extension items cluster into 1, 2, 3 and then the 4–5 mark group. Because there are fewer items, an extra two minutes on any one is proportionally far more damaging than the same delay in Advanced.
  • The large item is worth more of your total. One 5-mark item is 7.1% of the paper; on the Advanced paper it is 5%. You cannot afford to be cold on the large item.

“Items worth larger mark values (eg 4 or 5 marks) provide opportunities for students to demonstrate depth of knowledge and application of skills. Students are presented with a substantial problem to solve with less scaffolding than a similar question broken up into smaller mark value parts.”

NESA, HSC exam advice for Mathematics Advanced, Extension 1 and Extension 24

That quotation is the specification for how this paper is designed. “Depth of knowledge” means you must retrieve a technique without being prompted. “Less scaffolding” means you must choose the method yourself. “Depth of application” means you must use the result meaningfully, not just compute it.

The method-selection trap

In Advanced, the question usually tells you the method. In Extension 1 it frequently does not. A question reading “hence find the value of the sum” may require the arithmetic–geometric series formula, a telescoping argument, or an integral test — and choosing between them is exactly the “insight” NESA describes2. A student who tries three methods in four minutes will run out of time on the very items that carry the band.

The fix: a pre-allocated method hierarchy. Before the exam, write a one-page decision list: “Finite sum → check AP then GP. Integral with a log → integration by parts. Complex modulus → multiply by the conjugate.” NESA’s E4 descriptor requires students to “synthesise mathematical techniques, results and ideas effectively to solve problems”2. Effectively is the operative word.

Not permitted

NESA has ruled out two common crutches: mathematical curve-drawing templates will no longer be permitted equipment for the suite of new mathematics HSC examinations, and compasses and protractors will no longer be permitted4. So you cannot template your way to graph accuracy, and any geometric diagram in Section II must be drawn with a ruler and a pencil. Plan for that.

A 2-hour pacing plan

  • Reading time (10 min). NESA gives you ten minutes for a two-hour paper — a generous 8%. Use both passes: first for command words and marks, second for method decisions on the large item.
  • Minutes 0–30: Section I (10 marks) plus the 1–2 mark warm-ups. Objective response questions have “only one correct or best answer”5. Commit and move.
  • Minutes 30–100: the body of Section II. Roughly 70 minutes across perhaps 18–20 short items — about three to four minutes each. Deliberately slower than Advanced.
  • Minutes 100–115: the 4–5 mark item. Fifteen minutes is not unreasonable for one item worth 5 marks here.
  • Minutes 115–120: verify. Especially signs, moduli, and whether n ∈ ℤ appeared where it was needed.
Why the reading-time ratio matters here. Ten minutes out of 120 is 8.3% of your total; in Advanced, ten of 180 is 5.6%. NESA has effectively told you that method selection on a compressed paper needs proportionally more planning time. Candidates who skip reading time on Extension 1 lose the large item, and the large item is where E4 is decided.

The Band E4 Descriptor, Decoded

This is NESA’s published Band E4 performance band description for Mathematics Extension 1, reproduced verbatim2.

“The typical performance in this band:

“demonstrates extensive knowledge and skills appropriate to the course.”

“synthesises mathematical techniques, results and ideas effectively to solve problems.”

“demonstrates insightful and sophisticated multi-step mathematical reasoning and justification.”

“interprets, explains, justifies and evaluates solutions to problems across a wide range of familiar and unfamiliar contexts.”

“applies mathematical models to efficiently solve problems.”

“communicates complex ideas and arguments effectively using appropriate mathematical language, notation, diagrams and graphs.”

NESA, Performance band descriptions for Mathematics Extension 1, Stage 6 Syllabus (2017)2 [source]

Read the E3 descriptor alongside it. E3 says students “synthesises mathematical techniques, results and ideas to solve problems across a wide range of familiar and unfamiliar contexts”, demonstrating “well-developed multi-step mathematical reasoning”, and that they “interprets, explains, justifies and evaluates solutions to problems” — without the word “unfamiliar” in the second clause2.

E4 wording2What it demandsWhere E3 stops
“Synthesises… effectively” Method selection that is correct and efficient. The mark for choosing the shorter route is real. E3 only requires synthesis without the efficiency qualifier.
“Insightful” multi-step reasoning The large item. NESA guarantees at least one item at 4–5 marks1, presented with “less scaffolding”4. E3 says “well-developed” — strong but not insightful.
“Interprets, explains, justifies and evaluates” All four verbs. Evaluation means judging whether a result is reasonable, whether the method suits the context, and what a limit or domain does to it. E3 lists only the first three verbs2. Evaluation is the specific E3→E4 gap.
“Applies mathematical models to efficiently solve problems” Financial mathematics in particular — picking the right model quickly, and interpreting its parameters economically. E3 says only “applies mathematical models”.
“Communicates complex ideas and arguments” Written mathematical argument, not just notation. This is why proof appears in this course at all. E3 requires communicating “effectively” but not as an argument.
Your single diagnostic question: when you finish an item, do you merely have the answer, or do you have an answer plus a judgement about it? E4 is defined by the second. NESA’s descriptor for Extension 2 goes further still and requires that a candidate “proves results” — see the Extension 2 guide.

Course Map, Assumed Knowledge & Weightings

School-based assessment weightings

The two mandatory course components apply to Extension 1 exactly as they do to Advanced: Understanding, Fluency and Communication 50% and Problem Solving, Reasoning and Justification 50%1. NESA’s sample Year 12 formal program for Extension 1 contains four assessment tasks, with individual task weightings between 10% and 40%1.

The assessment trap

Because half the school-based assessment is “Problem Solving, Reasoning and Justification” 1, and because that half requires written justification, you should look for an assessment task that is not a timed exam. NESA explicitly supports “an investigative project or assignment involving presentation of work in class” and “a guided investigation or research task involving collection of data and analysis”1. An assignment gives you time to practise the justification writing that the exam’s large item demands 4.

The Year 12 modules

ModuleCore contentWhere E4 is won
ME12.1 Mathematical Proof Implication, equivalence, contrapositive; direct proof, contradiction/contradiction technique; mathematical induction; the structure of mathematical argument. This is the module that makes the descriptor’s “communicates complex ideas and arguments”2 assessable. Proofs appear in the exam and cannot be improvised.
ME12.2 Complex Numbers Complex plane, modulus and argument, De Moivre’s theorem, roots of polynomials, loci. De Moivre items routinely require method selection with no scaffold4. Loci items need a correctly drawn diagram — named in the descriptor2.
ME12.3 Further Calculus Integration by parts, trigonometric and hyperbolic integration, logarithmic differentiation, rational functions, improper integrals, further derivative applications. The most method-selection-heavy module. “Logarithmic differentiation” versus “differentiate then integrate” is exactly the “effectively” test2.
ME12.4 Financial Mathematics Annuities, compound interest, exponential and logarithmic growth and decay, present value and future value, effective vs nominal interest. Directly targets “applies mathematical models to efficiently solve problems”2, and provides the realistic contexts for “evaluate”.
ME12.5 Further Trigonometry Identities and equations, compound angles, related angles, proof of trigonometric identities, general solutions. Identity proof appears here, making it the second home of the ME12.1 proof skills. Do not treat it as a computational module.
The integration spine. Three of the five modules (ME12.1, ME12.3, ME12.5) reward the same underlying skill: hold a multi-step argument in your head and write it without prompting. Students who memorise proof templates without understanding the logic will freeze on the first unfamiliar instance — precisely the “insightful” test the E4 descriptor sets2.
06

Module-by-Module Mark-Winning Strategy

ME12.1 — Mathematical Proof

What E4 requires

E4 requires you to “communicate complex ideas and arguments effectively”2. In this module, that means writing a logically complete proof with no gaps.

Tactics

  • Choose the technique before you write. For p ⇒ q: contrapositive is often shortest; direct proof if p gives you q naturally; contradiction when you want to exploit an assumption without knowing it is false.
  • State the base case and the inductive step explicitly, and separately. NESA marks them separately. A merged sentence usually earns one mark.
  • Never assume what you are proving. The single most common deduction in proof marking. Say “assume for contradiction that…” and then derive the contradiction.
  • For equivalence, prove both directions. p ⇔ q and q ⇒ p are separate marks. Also note p ⇔ q ≡ ¬q ⇒ ¬p.
  • Anchor every step. “Since x is an integer, by the definition of an integer…” An unanchored step is where E4 proof marks are lost.

Trap

Proving the result by example, or by computing several cases. E4 demands reasoning that establishes the result generally — the “argument” in the descriptor2.

ME12.2 — Complex Numbers

What E4 requires

Method selection and interpretation of geometric information, since the descriptor names “diagrams and graphs” and “unfamiliar contexts”2.

Tactics

  • Modulus and argument, always together. If asked to “write z in polar form”, both r = |z| and θ = arg z are needed, and θ must be stated in the required range.
  • De Moivre: choose the smallest n. To find the fifth roots of z, you need the fifth root of r and (θ+2kπ)/5 for k=0,1,2,3,4. Using k=1…5 instead is an indexing error that costs marks.
  • Draw loci. Circle, line, ray, annulus — and know exactly which. Draw it before answering; the descriptor rewards diagrams2 and it prevents sign errors.
  • Rationalise the denominator when asked. If the question says “in the form a + bi” with integers a, b, multiply by the conjugate. If it says “in surd form”, leave it in surd form. Read the specification line.

Trap

Losing the modulus square root. |z1z2| = |z1||z2|, but |(z1)n| = |z1|n — you need the nth root, not the power.

ME12.3 — Further Calculus

What E4 requires

Efficient synthesis of techniques2. This module has the highest density of “which method?” decisions in the course.

Tactics

  • Integration by parts: choose u by LIATE. Logarithmic, Inverse, Algebraic, Trigonometric, Exponential. Choosing u as the other factor produces an integral at least as hard as the original.
  • Logarithmic differentiation for uv forms. Never use the power rule on a variable exponent. y = xx must be logged. NESA tests this deliberately.
  • Trigonometric integrals: substitution, then parts, then identities, in that order. ∫ sin²x dx is an identity; ∫ x²cos x dx is parts; ∫ sin³x dx is substitution. Know one example of each.
  • Rational functions: partial fractions. Proper fractions only. Decompose (2x+1)/((x−1)(x+2)) into A/(x−1) + B/(x+2) before integrating, and show the A, B work.
  • Improper integrals → limits. If the integrand diverges, substitute t = 1/x or work with a parameter. Substitution of infinity directly is never valid.

Trap

Using the indefinite integration-by-parts formula on a definite integral. If you get ex(x2−2x) + C and then try to substitute bounds into C, the result is meaningless. Use the definite version from the start.

ME12.4 — Financial Mathematics

What E4 requires

This module is where “applies mathematical models to efficiently solve problems”2 is tested almost literally, and where “evaluate” has a natural home.

Tactics

  • Know every annuity variant and its trigger. Present value of an ordinary annuity subtracts a period; annuity due adds one. Monthly loan repayments are annuity questions with n × 12 periods and r/12. Perpetuity is PV = rM where M is the periodic payment.
  • Effective versus nominal interest. If i is the nominal annual rate compounded n times, the effective rate is (1+i/n)n−1. NESA asks you to compare them, and the difference is the point of the item.
  • Write the model, then solve. For exponential growth or decay, state P = P0ekt or A = A0bt and identify P0 and k before substituting. Marks attach to the model, not just the arithmetic.
  • Evaluate model suitability. “This model assumes the interest rate and repayment amount remain constant, which is unrealistic over twenty years, so it overestimates affordability.” That is a full E4 evaluation mark2.

Trap

Converting between time units inconsistently. If the rate compounds quarterly and the question asks about weeks, convert both, and show the conversion.

ME12.5 — Further Trigonometry

What E4 requires

Identity proof — ME12.1 logic wearing trigonometric clothing. Treating this module as pure computation is the most common strategic error in Extension 1.

Tactics

  • Start from the most complicated term. Always. It appears once and its solution appears twice, forcing an identity you can then divide out.
  • Choose the right identity, not the first one. For sin²x use (1−cos2x)/2; for cos²x use (1+cos2x)/2; for 1±sin²x use cos²x or sin²x directly.
  • State the identity before you use it. “Using 2sinθcosθ = sin2θ…” earns the mark; silently rearranging does not.
  • General solutions in radians with n ∈ ℤ. Also check for spurious solutions introduced by dividing — substitute back.

Trap

Dividing by sin x or cos x without acknowledging that this excludes values where they are zero, then failing to test those excluded values. NESA awards a mark for the exclusions check.

Worked Examples With Real Marks

Example 1 — The 5-mark large item (proof)

5 marks · the item type NESA guarantees at least one of1,4

Prove that for all positive integers n, the sum 1 + 3 + 5 + … + (2n − 1) = n².

Model response

Proof by mathematical induction.

Base case (n = 1): The left-hand side is 1 and the right-hand side is 1² = 1. The statement holds for n = 1. [1 mark]

Inductive step: Assume the statement holds for some positive integer k, that is, assume 1 + 3 + 5 + … + (2k − 1) = k². This is the induction hypothesis — an assumption, not something we are trying to prove. [1 mark]

Then 1 + 3 + 5 + … + (2k − 1) + (2k + 1) = k² + (2k + 1) = k² + 2k + 1 = (k + 1)². This is exactly the required statement with k replaced by k + 1. [2 marks]

By the principle of mathematical induction, the statement is true for all positive integers n. [1 mark]

MarkWhat it testsWhy it separates E3 from E4
Base case stated separatelyStructure of induction An E3 response often merges base case and step into one paragraph, earning one mark instead of three.
Explicit induction hypothesis“Insightful” reasoning2 Naming it as an assumption demonstrates the insight the descriptor demands.
Written conclusion“Communicates complex ideas and arguments”2 The closing sentence is a mark. Never stop at the algebra.

Example 2 — The 3-mark method-selection item

3 marks · “synthesises… effectively”2

Find the indefinite integral ∫ x²ex dx.

Model response

Substitution will not work because differentiating x²ex does not reduce the power of x. Integration by parts is required. Taking u = x² (algebraic) and dv = exdx (exponential), so du = 2x dx and v = ex:

∫ x²exdx = x²ex − ∫ ex·2x dx.

Integrating by parts again with u = 2x and dv = exdx:

∫ 2xexdx = 2xex − 2ex, so

∫ x²exdx = ex(x² − 2x + 2) + C. [3 marks]

ElementWhy it is there
The opening sentence justifying integration by parts This is the E4 “effectively” mark — the method was chosen, not guessed.
Labelling u, dv, du, v Standard NESA marking practice; makes each substitution markable.
Both applications of parts shown Skipping the intermediate integral loses a mark.
Factored final form Matches the expected answer and is easier to check.

Example 3 — The 3-mark evaluation item

3 marks · “applies mathematical models to efficiently solve problems”2

A loan of $40,000 is repaid by equal monthly repayments over 5 years at 6% p.a. compounded monthly. Find the monthly repayment, and explain why the model is only an approximation of the real cost of borrowing.

Model response

The periodic interest rate is r = 0.06/12 = 0.005 and the number of periods is n = 5 × 12 = 60. The present value of an ordinary annuity gives

40000 = R × [1 − (1.005)−60] / 0.005

Now (1.005)−60 = 0.74064, so the annuity factor is (1 − 0.74064)/0.005 = 51.871. Hence

R = 40000/51.871 = $771.11 per month. [2 marks]

The model assumes the interest rate and the repayment amount remain constant for the full five years. In reality the rate may be variable, so the repayment would change; the model also ignores fees and inflation, meaning the real value of the repayments falls over time, so the model likely overstates the affordability of the loan. [1 mark]

MarkWhat it testsHow E4 is secured
Annuity setupModel selection and correct parameters Converts the rate and the period count explicitly before substituting.
Numerical answerAccurate computation Shows the annuity factor, so an arithmetic slip still preserves the method mark.
Evaluation“Interprets, explains, justifies and evaluates”2 Names two specific assumptions and states the directional effect of each. Direction is the difference between evaluation and description.

An Annotated E4 Response

Question (4 marks)

Complex numbers in an unfamiliar context

The complex number z = 3 + 4i satisfies z3 = az + b where a and b are real. (a) Find |z2 + 3z|. (b) Find a and b.

Model response

(a) z² = (3+4i)² = 9 + 24i + 16i² = −7 + 24i, so z² + 3z = (−7 + 24i) + (9 + 12i) = 2 + 36i. Therefore |z² + 3z| = √(2² + 36²) = √(4 + 1296) = √1300 = 10√13 ≈ 36.1. [2 marks]

(b) z3 = z·z² = (3 + 4i)(−7 + 24i) = −21 + 72i − 28i + 96i² = −117 + 44i. Equating with az + b = a(3 + 4i) + b = (3a + b) + 4ai and comparing real and imaginary parts: 4a = 44 so a = 11, and 3a + b = −117 so 33 + b = −117, giving b = −150. [2 marks]

AnnotationWhy it matters
Expanding z² fully with i² = −1 shown Method markable. Skipping straight to the answer forfeits it.
|√1300| simplified to 10√13 NESA requires surd form unless a decimal is requested. Both are correct; only one may be specified.
“Equating with… and comparing real and imaginary parts” Names the technique. This is the “explains” that the descriptor requires2.
Each of a and b boxed with a check Substituting back confirms the result and costs fifteen seconds.
Why E3 answers fall short. A Band E3 response typically writes |z2+3z| = 36.1 and a = 11, b = −150 with no intermediate algebra and no statement of the equating technique. Same answers, fewer marks — because E3 requires that a candidate “communicates effectively using appropriate mathematical language”2 while E4 requires communication of “complex ideas and arguments”.

The 12 Mark-Losers

On a 70-mark paper these cost proportionally more than anywhere else.

01

Stalling on method selection

One 5-mark item is 7.1% of the paper. Two minutes of trial and error on two items is 14% of your score.

Fix: memorise a one-page method hierarchy and apply it mechanically.
02

Assuming what you are proving

The classic proof error, and the most heavily penalised across the whole course.

Fix: write “Assume for contradiction that…” before using any false statement.
03

Merging base case and inductive step

NESA marks them separately. A merged paragraph typically earns one mark instead of three.

Fix: label them on separate lines.
04

Losing the square root on moduli

|(zn)| = |z|n requires the nth root, not the power.

Fix: for De Moivre roots, write r1/n explicitly.
05

Power rule on a variable exponent

d/dx (xx) is not xx−1.

Fix: log first, always, when the base and exponent both vary.
06

Presenting the answer in the wrong form

Decimal when surd is specified, or as an improper fraction when “simplest form” is requested.

Fix: read the specification line twice and restate the required form in your answer.
07

Skipping the “hence” follow-up

On a compressed paper this is disproportionately expensive.

Fix: physically arrow from “hence” to the next line.
08

Ignoring spurious solutions

Dividing by a possibly-zero expression without testing the excluded values.

Fix: after any division, list the excluded values and substitute them back.
09

No evaluation sentences

“Evaluates” appears only in E4, not E32. If you never write a judgement, you cannot be credited with one.

Fix: end one item per module with “this assumes…, which means…”.
10

Skipping the reading time

Ten of 120 minutes here is 8.3% of your paper, versus 5.6% in Advanced.

Fix: treat reading time as part of the paper, not a gift.
11

Forgetting you sit two papers

NESA requires this Extension paper plus Advanced or Extension 21. Practise the fatigue.

Fix: sit two full papers back to back at least twice before the real thing.
12

Drawing diagrams without a ruler

Compasses and protractors are no longer permitted4. Sketchy diagrams cost accuracy marks.

Fix: bring a pencil, ruler and eraser for every Extension paper.

Your 12-Week Plan

Assumes about 7 hours per week. Because the paper is compressed, mock frequency matters more than question volume.

Week 1

Diagnostic + Advanced repair

Sit a full Extension 1 paper cold. Then repair Advanced algebra and functions, since Advanced is assumed knowledge1.

7 hrs · 1 paper
Week 2

ME12.1 Proof

Implication and equivalence; direct proof; contradiction. Write ten proofs by hand — typing proofs teaches you nothing.

8 hrs · 10 proofs
Week 3

ME12.1 Induction

Base case and step drilled separately. Mock 1 at the weekend.

9 hrs · Mock 1
Week 4

ME12.2 Complex numbers

Polar form, De Moivre, roots of unity, loci. Practise drawing loci with a ruler.

8 hrs · loci drill
Week 5

ME12.3 Integration

Parts, trig and hyperbolic integration, partial fractions, logarithmic differentiation. Build the LIATE decision card.

9 hrs · decision card
Week 6

ME12.3 improper integrals

Limits and parameter methods. Mock 2 at the weekend.

8 hrs · Mock 2
Week 7

ME12.4 Financial maths

Annuities, effective versus nominal, exponential growth and decay. Practise with real Australian interest rates.

8 hrs · applied problems
Week 8

ME12.5 Identities

Proof of trigonometric identities from the most complicated term. Mock 3 at the weekend.

9 hrs · Mock 3
Week 9

Large-item drilling

Only 4–5 mark items, timed at 8 minutes each1. This is the highest-value single week in the plan.

9 hrs · large items
Week 10

Past paper blitz

Four papers, guideline-marked the next morning. Also re-sit your weakest Advanced topic.

11 hrs · 4 papers
Week 11

Dual-paper simulation

Sit Advanced then Extension 1 back to back, exactly as NESA requires1. Mock 4.

10 hrs · dual sit
Week 12

Taper

Proof structures, the method hierarchy, annuity variants. No new content. Sleep properly.

4 hrs · then rest
