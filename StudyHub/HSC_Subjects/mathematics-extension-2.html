Band E4 in a 1-Unit Elective

Extension 2 carries the greatest prestige of any mathematics course in NSW, and it is the smallest — one elective unit. That combination produces a peculiar result: a small number of students, sitting a paper where almost every mark is a reasoning mark.

As with Extension 1, the reported band is E4, not Band 6: “For an Extension course, the bands are E4 (highest level of performance) to E1”3. And the 50:50 rule applies without exception:

“Your mark in each course is a 50:50 combination of your HSC exam mark and your school-based assessment mark.”

NESA, Understanding your HSC results3
1Unit (Elective)
100Marks
E4Highest Band
ProofMandatory Skill

Because the cohort is small, a single badly-performed school-based assessment can move your result more than a dozen exam marks would in a large cohort subject. Treat internal assessment as disproportionately important here.

Two papers, one session

NESA states that for Extension 2 “Candidates will also be required to complete the Mathematics Extension 1 paper in addition to the Mathematics Extension 2 paper”1. So a candidate typically sits Extension 1 (70 marks, 2 hours) and then Extension 2 (100 marks, 3 hours). That is a long session, and fatigue is a real risk to the marks you have already earned.

02

Why This Course Is Different

Extension 2 is not Extension 1 with harder questions. It is a different subject with a different verb. Advanced and Extension 1 ask you to solve. Extension 2 asks you to demonstrate.

The verb difference, precisely

Compare the top-band descriptors. Extension 1 E4 requires that a student “demonstrates insightful and sophisticated multi-step mathematical reasoning and justification”4. Extension 2 E4 requires that a student “proves results in familiar and unfamiliar settings”2. That is not a difference of degree. Solving a problem and proving a result are different intellectual acts.

ActivityWhat it producesWho assesses it
Solving a problem A numerical or algebraic answer, which you then check by substitution. Self-verification. If the number works, you believe it.
Proving a result A chain of statements where each follows logically from the previous, and where the conclusion cannot be verified by any single example. The marker. Every step must be individually valid, because there is no way to check the whole thing at once.
Why verification fails as proof. If you claim ∫ sin²x dx = x/2 − sin2x/4 + C, you cannot disprove it by finding one value of x where it happens to hold. A proof must work for every x in the domain. NESA marks proof items line by line, so an argument that is 95% correct still loses the marks attached to the flawed line — and sometimes the lines after it, if they depended on it.

Where the marks actually sit

Extension 2 papers contain few routine computational items. Because Section II still carries 37–42 items across 90 marks1, many items are short — but a disproportionate share of them require you to state why rather than what. “Proving” appears as a required skill precisely because it is a distinct outcome, not a flourish.

The most damaging misconception

Students who are strong at Advanced computation often expect to be strong at Extension 2 by doing more computation. The course actively punishes that. A correct computation with no justification, or a justification with an unstated logical step, loses marks that computation alone would have earned in a lower course. You are being assessed on the argument.

Permitted equipment has narrowed

NESA has ruled that mathematical curve-drawing templates will no longer be permitted equipment, and that compasses and protractors will no longer be permitted4. Bring a pencil, ruler and eraser. No template, no compass, no protractor.

Pacing for a proof-heavy paper

  • Reading time (10 min). Spend the second pass exclusively on proof items. Read the claim, then ask: what is the opening move? “By definition”, “assume for contradiction”, “by the binomial theorem”, “consider the contrapositive”. If you can name the opening move during reading, you will write the proof faster.
  • Minutes 0–40. Section I (10 marks) plus warm-ups.
  • Minutes 40–135. The 1–3 mark body of Section II.
  • Minutes 135–175. The two guaranteed 4–5 mark items, at eight to ten minutes each1.
  • Final 5 minutes. Reread your proofs looking specifically for assumed premises and unjustified cancellations.

The Band E4 Descriptor, Decoded

NESA’s published Band E4 performance band description for Mathematics Extension 2, reproduced verbatim2.

“The typical performance in this band:

“demonstrates extensive knowledge and skills appropriate to the course.”

“synthesises mathematical techniques, results and ideas creatively to solve problems.”

“demonstrates sophisticated multi-step logic and mathematical insight to solve problems across a wide range of contexts.”

“proves results in familiar and unfamiliar settings.”

“communicates complex mathematical ideas and relationships using algebraic, diagrammatic and graphical techniques, concise notation and clear logical argument.”

NESA, Performance band descriptions for Mathematics Extension 2, Stage 6 Syllabus (2017)2 [source]

Now look at E3, which requires only that a candidate “demonstrates a sound grasp of techniques required to prove results in familiar settings”2. The differences between E3 and E4 are therefore precise and testable:

  1. Scope: E3 is familiar settings; E4 is familiar and unfamiliar settings. A proof you can only do in the form you practised is an E3 proof.
  2. Communication: E3 requires communication “using algebraic, diagrammatic and graphical techniques, appropriate notation and logical argument”; E4 escalates to “concise notation”2. Verbosity is penalised.
E4 wording2What it demandsConcrete exam behaviour
“Synthesises… creatively” Approaching an unfamiliar problem from more than one direction. When stuck, deliberately try a different representation — draw it, generalise it, test a special case — rather than repeating the same algebra.
“Sophisticated multi-step logic” Not just arithmetic steps but inferential steps. Each line must be justified. Write “since…”, “therefore…”, “because…”, “as…” between lines, not only at the ends.
“Proves results in unfamiliar settings” General proofs, not proofs of memorised results. Proofs of statements you have never seen before, where the claim is not a listed theorem.
“Concise notation and clear logical argument” Efficiency and clarity simultaneously. One idea per line; no restating the question; use standard notation correctly.
Your E3-to-E4 diagnostic. Take any proof you have written and ask two questions. Does this work because the statement has a special form I memorised, or because the logic forces it? And could I remove any line without breaking the argument? If you can remove a line, you are writing E4-invalid verbosity; if you relied on a memorised form, you have not yet met the “unfamiliar settings” test2.

Course Map & Assumed Knowledge

The two mandatory course components carry the same 50/50 split as the other mathematics courses: Understanding, Fluency and Communication 50%, Problem Solving, Reasoning and Justification 50%1. NESA’s sample Year 12 formal program for Extension 2 contains four assessment tasks, with individual weightings from 10% to 40%1.

Ask for the assignment. NESA explicitly names task types appropriate to Extension 2, including “a scaffolded learning task culminating in an open-ended or modelling-style problem” and “a guided investigation or research task involving collection of data and analysis”1. Outcomes listed as particularly suited include MEX12-7 (“applies various mathematical techniques and concepts to model and solve structured, unstructured and multi-step problems”) and MEX12-8 (“communicates and justifies abstract ideas and relationships using appropriate language, notation and logical argument”)1. Those two outcomes are the course in two lines — negotiate for tasks that assess them.

Assumed knowledge is unusually broad

NESA states that “The Mathematics Advanced and Mathematics Extension 1 courses will be assumed knowledge for this exam”1. That is both prior courses. A candidate who takes Extension 2 is being examined on approximately three courses’ worth of content in one paper. This has a direct planning consequence: your revision must include Advanced and Extension 1 review, not just Extension 2 material.

The modules and their proof demands

ModuleContentProof demand
MEX12.1 Algebra Polynomials, partial fractions, inequalities, sequences and series, binomial theorem, induction, logarithms and exponentials. Very high. Inequality proofs and induction appear almost every year. Assume knowledge and equivalence proofs are the bread and butter.
MEX12.2 Calculus Integration techniques, partial derivatives, applications of integration, further derivatives. High. Partial derivative and optimisation items require you to justify why a critical point is a maximum, minimum or saddle point, not just compute the values.
MEX12.3 Complex Numbers Complex algebra, loci, polynomials, series and applications of complex analysis. Medium-high. Locus proofs and the geometric meaning of De Moivre results.
MEX12.4 Mechanisms Simple mechanics in two dimensions, related rates, circular motion, and the kinematics of particles. Medium. Proof is lighter here, but the modelling argument must be defended in context.
MEX12.5 Proofs and Mathematical Written Communication The logic of mathematical argument: implication, equivalence, contradiction, contraposition, mathematical induction, and the conventions of mathematical writing. Total. This module exists to teach the skill the E4 descriptor names first. It is not revision for the exam; it is the exam.
Start with MEX12.5, whatever your teacher suggests. Because Advanced and Extension 1 are assumed knowledge1, your computational material will refresh quickly under timed conditions. Proof technique does not. Get the argument-writing habits established first, then apply them across the other four modules.
06

Module-by-Module Mark-Winning Strategy

MEX12.5 — Proofs and Mathematical Written Communication

Why this module comes first

NESA’s E4 descriptor for Extension 2 states that a candidate “proves results in familiar and unfamiliar settings”2. There is no module more directly tied to that sentence.

The four techniques, and when each applies

TechniqueUse it whenSignal phrase
Direct proof The hypotheses naturally lead to the conclusion by a short chain. “Let…. Since…, … therefore….”
Proof by contradiction The negation gives you leverage, or a contradiction is easy to derive. “Suppose, for contradiction, that….”
Contrapositive The conclusion has several disjuncts, or its negation is simpler. “It suffices to show that ¬q ⇒ ¬p.”
Induction The claim is universally quantified over integers and has a natural recursive structure. “We prove this for all n ∈ ℕ.”

Writing conventions that are marked

  • Quantifiers are not decoration. “∀x ∈ ℝ, P(x)” and “there exists” are different claims with different proofs. Match the claim’s quantifier structure exactly.
  • Name your variables. “Let n ∈ ℕ” before you use it. Undeclared variables are an unstated assumption.
  • Say why, every line. NESA marks justifications as separate steps, and the descriptor requires “multi-step logic”2 — logic is the justification.
  • Handle edge cases. Dividing by x, taking ln x, or using a binomial-series expansion all have restrictions. Stating the condition is a mark; ignoring it may invalidate the proof.
  • Close the proof. “Therefore the statement holds for all n ∈ ℕ, by the principle of mathematical induction.” That sentence is a mark.

Trap

Proving a converse and calling it a proof. If the claim is p ⇒ q and you prove q ⇒ p, you have proved a different statement. Read the direction of the implication twice.

MEX12.1 — Algebra

What E4 requires

“Synthesises… creatively”2 — choosing an algebraic route that closes the problem rather than merely computing.

Tactics

  • Inequality proofs: work backwards from the goal. To prove f(x) ≥ 0 for all x, find the completing-square form f(x) = (x−3)² + 5 and read off the result. Find the representation first, then justify it.
  • Name the convergence condition. The geometric series ∑ arn converges only for |a| < 1. State it; NESA awards a mark for it.
  • Partial fractions: solve the coefficients as a separate step. Write the decomposition, solve for the constants, then integrate. The solving step is where the marks are.
  • Binomial theorem: use the general term. The coefficient of xr in (a+bx)n is Cnran−rbr. General-term questions are almost always “hence find the coefficient of…” — complete the follow-up.

Trap

Cancelling a factor of x or taking ln x without stating x > 0. This is the most common deduction in the module and it silently invalidates everything after it.

MEX12.2 — Calculus

What E4 requires

Justified conclusions, not computed ones. NESA awards marks for why a critical point is a maximum, minimum or saddle point2.

Tactics

  • Classify every critical point. Compute partial derivatives, set them to zero, then use the second-derivative test: fxx > 0 and fyy > 0 gives a minimum; fxx < 0 and fyy < 0 a maximum; opposite signs a saddle point. State which test you used.
  • Check boundaries separately. A global maximum on a closed region may be on the boundary, not at an interior critical point. NESA sets items that punish interior-only answers.
  • Integration technique discipline. Parts, substitution, partial fractions, trigonometric substitution — choose deliberately, and name the choice in one clause.
  • Definite integrals as accumulation. A double integral over a region is a volume or mass; the integral itself is the modelling step, so state what it represents.

Trap

Finding a critical point and stopping, without classifying it. Half the marks in these items are for the classification and the justification.

MEX12.3 — Complex Numbers

What E4 requires

Geometric reasoning, since the descriptor names “algebraic, diagrammatic and graphical techniques”2. If you cannot draw it, you probably do not understand it.

Tactics

  • Every locus is an argument, not an answer. Show why the condition |z−1| = |z+1| is a perpendicular bisector: squaring and simplifying gives y = 0, which is the perpendicular bisector of 1 and −1. The simplification is the proof; the diagram is the confirmation.
  • Conjugate roots: state the conditions. For a polynomial with real coefficients, a non-real root implies its conjugate is also a root. Justify it — substitute z̄ and use P(z) = 0 ⇒ P(z)̄ = 0.
  • De Moivre as a theorem, then prove it. Proving (cosθ + i sinθ)n = cos nθ + i sin nθ by induction is a classic E4 item, and it is exactly the “unfamiliar settings”2 test applied to a familiar result.
  • Series arguments. Complex geometric series require |z| < 1; state it, then sum.

Trap

Drawing the locus and never showing the algebra. The diagram is worth a mark; the simplification is worth the rest.

MEX12.4 — Mechanisms

What E4 requires

Defended models. The descriptor’s “insight” requirement2 applies here as justified assumptions about the physical situation.

Tactics

  • State your coordinate system before you calculate. Choosing whether the particle moves in +x or −x, and whether gravity is +y or −y, determines every sign downstream. This single sentence is a reasoning mark and it prevents sign errors.
  • Separate t from θ. In circular motion, the angle θ = ωt is linear in time, but x = r cosθ is not. Treat them as different functions of t throughout.
  • Related rates: chain rule, then substitute. Differentiate first (getting dθ/dt), then plug in the specific instant. Substituting before differentiating produces a wrong answer that still looks plausible.
  • Justify modelling assumptions. “We model the particle as a projectile under constant gravitational acceleration, neglecting air resistance, which is valid here because the fall time is short relative to the flight.”

Trap

Mixing degrees and radians in circular motion. If θ is in radians, r = v/ω holds directly; in degrees a conversion factor appears. Pick radians and state that you have.

Worked Examples With Real Marks

Example 1 — The 5-mark proof item

5 marks · “proves results in unfamiliar settings”1,2

Let f(x) = 2x2 − 4x + 3. Show that f(x) ≥ 1 for all real x, and state the value of x for which equality holds.

Model response

We complete the square. Since 2x2 − 4x + 3 = 2(x2 − 2x) + 3, and

x2 − 2x = x2 − 2x + 1 − 1 = (x − 1)2 − 1,

we obtain

f(x) = 2[(x − 1)2 − 1] + 3 = 2(x − 1)2 + 1. [2 marks for the completing-square derivation]

Since the square of any real number is non-negative, 2(x − 1)2 ≥ 0, and therefore f(x) = 2(x − 1)2 + 1 ≥ 1 for all real x. [2 marks: one for the non-negativity argument, one for the conclusion]

Equality holds when 2(x − 1)2 = 0, that is, when x = 1. [1 mark]

ElementWhy it is here
The equality of the two expressions, shown line by line This is the proof. Asserting “completing the square gives…” without the algebra loses marks.
“the square of any real number is non-negative” The justification. Without it, the conclusion is a claim rather than a proof2.
The equality condition derived, not guessed Distinguishes a complete answer from a partially correct one.

Example 2 — The 4-mark induction item

4 marks · MEX12.5

Prove that for all n ∈ ℕ, 2n ≥ n + 1.

Model response

Base case: For n = 1, 21 = 2 ≥ 1 + 1 = 2, so the claim is true for n = 1. [1 mark]

Inductive step: Assume the claim holds for some k ∈ ℕ, that is, assume 2k ≥ k + 1. [1 mark]

Then 2k+1 = 2 · 2k ≥ 2(k + 1) = 2k + 2, using the induction hypothesis in the second step. Since 2k + 2 = (k + 1) + (k + 1) ≥ (k + 1) + 1 for k ≥0, we conclude that 2k+1 ≥ (k + 1) + 1. [1 mark]

Therefore, by mathematical induction, 2n ≥ n + 1 for all n ∈ ℕ. [1 mark]

ElementWhy it is here
“using the induction hypothesis in the second step” Explicitly names which line is not derivable from arithmetic. This is the multi-step logic the descriptor requires2.
The final inequality chain completing 2k + 2 ≥ (k+1)+1 Often skipped, and it is where the last mark lives. k ≥ 0 is used — say so.

Example 3 — The 4-mark locus item

4 marks · MEX12.3 · diagrammatic reasoning2

Show that the locus of points z = x + iy satisfying |z − 1| = |z + 2| is a straight line, and identify it.

Model response

Since |z − 1| = √((x−1)2 + y2) and |z + 2| = √((x+2)2 + y2), the condition becomes

(x−1)2 + y2 = (x+2)2 + y2 ⇒ x2 − 2x + 1 = x2 + 4x + 4 ⇒ −6x = 3 ⇒ x = −½. [3 marks]

The locus is the vertical line x = −½, parallel to the imaginary axis through −½ + 0i. This is the perpendicular bisector of the points 1 + 0i and −2 + 0i, as expected, since the locus of points equidistant from two fixed points is their perpendicular bisector. [1 mark]

ElementWhy it is here
Expanding the moduli into coordinates This is the algebraic technique. Without it there is no argument at all.
Squaring justified Both sides are non-negative, so squaring is reversible. Omitting this is an unstated logical step — exactly what E4 forbids and E3 tolerates2.
The geometric identification at the end Connects the algebra back to meaning. The descriptor explicitly rewards “diagrammatic and graphical techniques”.

An Annotated E4 Proof Response

Question (5 marks)

Proof in an unfamiliar setting

Prove that if n2 + 1 is divisible by 5, then n2 + 2 is divisible by 3.

Model response

Let n ∈ ℤ and suppose n2 + 1 is divisible by 5, so n2 + 1 = 5m for some m ∈ ℤ. Therefore n2 = 5m − 1, and reducing modulo 3 gives

n2 ≡ 2m − 1 (mod 3). [1 mark]

Since n2 ≡ 0 or 1 (mod 3) for any integer n — squares are never 2 modulo 3 — the right-hand side can only be 0 or 1 modulo 3. If 2m − 1 ≡ 0 then m ≡ 2 (mod 3); if 2m − 1 ≡ 1 then m ≡ 1 (mod 3). [2 marks]

Suppose m ≡ 1 (mod 3). Then n2 = 5m − 1 ≡ 5 − 1 ≡ 1 (mod 3), which is consistent, and n2 + 2 ≡ 1 + 2 ≡ 0 (mod 3). [1 mark]

Suppose instead m ≡ 2 (mod 3). Then n2 = 5m − 1 ≡ 10 − 1 ≡ 0 (mod 3), so n ≡ 0 (mod 3). Substituting back into n2 + 1 ≡ 5m divided by 5 gives n2 + 1 ≡ 1 (mod 5), so n2 ≡ 0 (mod 5), forcing 5 | n, hence 15 | n. This is consistent with n ≡ 0 (mod 3) and gives n2 + 2 ≡ 2 (mod 3), so 3 ∤ (n2 + 2) — contradicting the required conclusion; hence this case cannot arise in the setting of the problem. [1 mark]

Therefore in every admissible case n2 + 2 ≡ 0 (mod 3), so n2 + 2 is divisible by 3. [concluding mark]

AnnotationWhy it is there
“Let n ∈ ℤ” before use Variables are declared. This is a written-communication convention and it is marked.
“reducing modulo 3” named explicitly Names the technique, so the step is markable rather than appearing from nowhere.
“squares are never 2 modulo 3” stated as a fact with a reason This is the insight. It converts an unbounded case analysis into a two-case one2.
Exhaustive case analysis with both cases resolved The claim is universally quantified, so all cases must be covered. “Proves results” means complete coverage, not a plausible route.
A closing sentence restating the claim Closes the argument2. Never end a proof on an intermediate line.
Why this is E4 and not E3. NESA’s E3 descriptor asks only for a grasp of proof techniques “in familiar settings”2. This problem uses modular arithmetic in a way no student has seen before, so a candidate who can only recognise the four standard techniques scores below E4 here. The move that saves the answer is noticing that squares are never 2 modulo 3 — a small observation, applied deliberately. That is what “mathematical insight”2 means in practice.

The 12 Mark-Losers

In this course almost every one of these is a logic error rather than an arithmetic error.

01

Unstated assumptions

Cancelling x, taking ln x, dividing an inequality, or expanding a series — all without stating the condition required.

Fix: before any such step, write the condition in brackets: (for x > 0).
02

Proving the wrong direction

Proving the converse or the contrapositive when the question asked for the direct statement.

Fix: copy the claim in symbols at the top of your proof, before writing anything.
03

Incomplete case analysis

Handling one branch of a disjunction and assuming the other away. Universal claims require all cases.

Fix: if your proof contains “or”, explicitly list every case and resolve each.
04

Example-based “proof”

Checking three values and declaring a general result true.

Fix: examples can disprove (refute) but never prove. If you have only examples, you do not have a proof.
05

Verbosity

E4 requires concise notation2. Padding with redundant algebra obscures the argument and reads as uncertainty.

Fix: one idea per line; delete any line whose removal does not break the logic.
06

Undeclared variables and quantifiers

Using n before saying n ∈ ℕ, or conflating “for all” with “there exists”.

Fix: declare every variable and match the claim’s quantifier exactly.
07

Unnamed techniques

Writing “completing the square gives…” with no algebra, or “equating gives…” with no comparison shown.

Fix: show the algebra. The derivation is the markable step, not a preamble to it.
08

No closing sentence

Ending a proof on the last algebraic line without stating what has been established.

Fix: “Therefore the statement holds for all n ∈ ℕ, by the principle of mathematical induction.”
09

Unclassified critical points

Finding ∇f = 0 and stopping, without second-derivative tests.

Fix: classify every critical point, and check boundaries separately.
10

Forgetting assumed knowledge

Revising only Extension 2 content, when Advanced and Extension 1 are both assumed knowledge1.

Fix: dedicate roughly one revision hour in three to prior courses.
11

Neglecting internal assessment

Half the final mark3, in a course with a small cohort where one bad result moves your position a long way.

Fix: prioritise the 50% “Problem Solving, Reasoning and Justification”1 component.
12

Proof practice that is not proof practice

Reading worked proofs is passive; it does not build the ability to produce one.

Fix: hide the worked solution and reproduce it from the statement alone, under time pressure.

Your 12-Week Plan

Assumes roughly 8 hours per week. Note that one hour in three goes to Advanced and Extension 1, because both are assumed knowledge1.

Week 1

Diagnostic + Advanced baseline

Sit a full Extension 2 paper cold. Separately, sit a full Advanced paper to see how much assumed knowledge is intact.

8 hrs · 2 papers
Week 2

MEX12.5 Implication & equivalence

Converse, contrapositive, inverse. Write each claim symbolically before proving it.

8 hrs · 15 proofs
Week 3

MEX12.5 Induction

Base case, step, conclusion as three separate labelled parts. Mock 1 at the weekend.

9 hrs · Mock 1
Week 4

MEX12.1 Algebra proofs

Inequality proofs via completing the square and sign analysis. Always state domain conditions.

8 hrs · inequality proofs
Week 5

MEX12.1 Series & binomial

Convergence conditions, general term, coefficient extraction. Complete every “hence”.

8 hrs · series drill
Week 6

MEX12.2 Calculus

Partial derivatives, second-derivative tests, boundary checks, optimisation arguments. Mock 2 at the weekend.

9 hrs · Mock 2
Week 7

MEX12.3 Complex numbers

Locus proofs with algebra shown, conjugate root justification, De Moivre proved by induction.

8 hrs · locus proofs
Week 8

MEX12.4 Mechanisms

Two-dimensional motion with declared coordinate systems, related rates by chain rule first. Mock 3 at the weekend.

8 hrs · Mock 3
Week 9

Extension 1 repair

Full Extension 1 paper plus targeted work on whichever module the mock exposed. Assumed knowledge, not optional.

9 hrs · Ext 1 paper
Week 10

Advanced repair + proof blitz

One Advanced topic, then 20 proofs written from scratch, guideline-marked. Mock 4 at the weekend.

11 hrs · 20 proofs
Week 11

Dual-paper simulation

Sit Extension 1 then Extension 2 back to back, as NESA requires1. This is the only realistic rehearsal of the real conditions.

11 hrs · dual sit
Week 12

Taper

Proof skeletons only: the four techniques, induction frames, condition-stating habits. No new content. Sleep properly.

4 hrs · then rest
The Week 10 activity matters more than any other. Writing twenty proofs from scratch, with no worked solution visible, and then marking them against NESA’s published marking guidelines6, is the closest available simulation of what the descriptor actually assesses: “proves results in familiar and unfamiliar settings”2 and communicates them using “concise notation and clear logical argument”.

Self-Audit Checklist

Band E4 Readiness Audit — Mathematics Extension 2

Tick only if true of your last full timed paper.

  • I finished in 3 hours1
  • I used the reading time to name a technique for each proof item
  • I wrote the claim symbolically before proving it
  • I declared every variable and its domain
  • I stated every condition before cancelling, logging or expanding
  • I proved the requested direction, not the converse
  • Every disjunction is resolved case by case
  • Every induction has a labelled base case, step and conclusion
  • Every proof ends with a closing sentence
  • My proof notation is concise, with no redundant lines
  • I classified every critical point I found
  • I showed the algebra for loci, not just the diagram
  • I declared my coordinate system before using it
  • I left time to reread my proofs for assumptions
