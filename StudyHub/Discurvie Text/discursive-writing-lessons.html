Lesson 1 · 40 min

What Is Discursive Writing?

Intended learning: define discursive writing, identify its purpose, and recognise it as "exploration," not argument.

Teaching content

Discursive writing is a mode of composition in which an author explores an idea or question from multiple perspectives without committing to a single, forceful conclusion. The word comes from the Latin discursus — literally "a running to and fro" — which captures how the piece moves between competing ideas, weighing each fairly.

In senior English, the defining contrast is with persuasion. A persuasive writer tries to convert the reader; a discursive writer tries to illuminate. The discursive composer appears in the text as a sincere, curious voice who treats the reader as an equal and invites them to think alongside. It is valued in Module C: The Craft of Writing precisely because it demands sincerity, balance and a personal voice.

Stimulus: "Belonging is both a comfort and a constraint."
Exam-style question (5 marks · ~8 min): Using the stimulus, write a short discursive paragraph that explores what belonging means — without arguing a single position.

Starter sentences (adapt these)

  • "The first place I truly belonged was…"
  • "To belong is to be known — and to be known is to be vulnerable."
  • "We speak of belonging as warm, yet it can also bind us."

Practice worksheet

Task 1. Define discursive writing in two sentences.

Task 2. Name its purpose and contrast it with persuasion.

Task 3. From a studied text, quote one line that explores rather than argues.

Band 6 sample answer

Model response

To belong is to be held, and to be held is to be bound. I think of the kitchen table of my childhood — a worn oak circle where every voice competed for air, where being known meant being seen, and being seen meant having no secrets. It was warmth, unquestionably; it was also constraint, for the same door that closed to keep us together closed sometimes to keep the world out. Belonging, then, is neither wholly comfort nor wholly cage, but both at once — a paradox each of us must learn to live inside.

Marking criteria / self-check

  • I defined discursive writing accurately and originally.
  • I contrasted its purpose with persuasion clearly.
  • I used a personal, sincere voice throughout.
  • I held two ideas in tension (comfort / constraint) rather than choosing.
  • My language was measured — hedged, not shrill.

Lesson 2 · 45 min

Discursive vs. Persuasive: Finding the Register

Intended learning: distinguish the discursive register from persuasion and argument, and consciously write in the exploratory mode.

Teaching content

Discursive and persuasive writing sit at opposite ends of a spectrum. Persuasion is thesis-first: it declares a position, marshals evidence as weaponry, and drives toward a triumphant verdict. Discursive writing is journey-first: it opens a question, weighs perspectives fairly, and lands on a moderate, often unresolved note. Mixing the two is the single most common reason students lose marks in Module C.

FeaturePersuasiveDiscursive
OpeningDeclares a thesisPoses a question / tension
GoalConvert the readerIlluminate an issue
EvidenceWeapon / ammunitionFuel for reflection
VoiceConfident advocateSincere, curious explorer
EndingEmphatic verdictQuiet, reflective landing
Stimulus: "The internet has changed the way we connect."
Exam-style question (8 marks · ~15 min): Write a discursive paragraph responding to the stimulus. You must explore, not argue — present at least two perspectives fairly.

Starter sentences

  • "Whether the internet has brought us together or driven us apart is a question with no simple answer."
  • "On one hand… on the other… and yet…"
  • "It would be easy to declare a winner in this debate — but that ease is itself the trap."

Practice worksheet

Task 1. Convert each persuasive line into a discursive one.

a) "Climate change is an emergency that must be stopped now!"

b) "Everyone knows homework is a waste of time."

Task 2. Rewrite a persuasive conclusion you have written as a discursive "quiet landing."

Band 6 sample answer

Model paragraph (discursive)

The internet has changed the way we connect — of that there is no doubt. How it has changed us is a far more open question. For the lonely and the isolated, a screen can be a doorway to a community that geography denied; for the first time, a teenager in a small town can find people who share their passions. And yet the same devices that connect can also isolate, offering the illusion of company while the room grows emptier. Perhaps the internet is neither bridge nor wall, but a mirror — reflecting back whatever capacity for connection we bring to it.

Marking criteria / self-check

  • I presented at least two perspectives fairly.
  • I did not slip into slogans or one-sided advocacy.
  • I used hedged, measured language.
  • My ending reflected rather than thundered.
  • My register stayed discursive throughout.

Lesson 3 · 45 min

The Personal, Sincere Voice

Intended learning: use a genuine first-person voice and lived experience to ground abstract ideas, while keeping sincerity rather than self-indulgence.

Teaching content

What separates discursive writing from a dry report is the presence of a real human being behind the words. NESA rewards a personal, reflective and imaginative voice. This does not mean writing about yourself for its own sake; it means using your own observation and uncertainty as a springboard into wider questions. Sincerity is the currency here — readers trust a writer who admits doubt, shares a specific moment, and treats the audience as equals.

The risk is self-indulgence. A powerful discursive voice uses the personal strategically: a concrete anecdote, a precisely rendered image, an honest admission of confusion — each earns its place by illuminating the idea, not by showing off.

Stimulus: "Home is not a place; it is a feeling."
Exam-style question (10 marks · ~18 min): Explore the idea of home through a personal memory. Let your lived experience open onto a wider question, but do not argue a single position.

Starter sentences

  • "I have lived in many houses, but only one has ever felt like home."
  • "Home, for me, was never a street address."
  • "The word 'home' catches in my throat, because I have more than one, and none of them is a building."

Practice worksheet

Task 1. Write one specific personal memory (2–3 sentences) that connects to an abstract idea.

Task 2. Now write the wider question your memory raises.

Task 3. Add one honest admission of uncertainty to your paragraph.

Band 6 sample answer

Model response

Home, for me, was never a street address. It was the smell of garlic frying in my grandmother's kitchen on a Tuesday night, when the rest of the world had gone quiet and her hands moved without hurry. Years later I drove past that house and found it painted a different colour, the garden paved over, and I understood that I had not really been looking for the building at all — I had been looking for the feeling it once held, and which I have been chasing, sometimes without knowing it, ever since. What is home, if not the place we are most ourselves in — and what does it say about us when that place can be lost to a coat of paint?

Marking criteria / self-check

  • I used a specific, sincere personal memory.
  • My memory opened onto a wider question.
  • My voice was authentic, not performative.
  • I avoided self-indulgence — every detail served the idea.
  • I held complexity rather than forcing a single answer.

Lesson 4 · 50 min

Structure & The Journey

Intended learning: apply the 8-stage Band 6 structural blueprint to plan a discursive piece that feels like a journey, not a dossier.

Teaching content

Discursive writing is not unstructured rambling — it is a carefully ordered journey. The highest-scoring responses share a skeleton that guides the reader from open question to settled (but not closed) reflection. This is the Band 6 blueprint:

  1. Hook — a concrete, sensory image or anecdote.
  2. Question / tension — the inquiry the piece explores.
  3. Personal entry — the sincere voice enters.
  4. Perspective A — one side, offered fairly and strongly.
  5. Concession / turning point — an honest hinge to the other side.
  6. Perspective B — the opposing view, treated with equal seriousness.
  7. Synthesis — nuance, limits, overlaps; ideas held in tension.
  8. Quiet landing — a moderate, reflective close.

The journey matters more than the destination: each stage should flow naturally into the next, and the reader should feel they have travelled rather than been lectured.

Stimulus: "Every ending is also a beginning."
Exam-style question (15 marks · ~30 min): Using the blueprint, plan and write a discursive piece on change inspired by the stimulus.

Starter sentences

  • "When I think of change, I think of the day we…"
  • "We treat endings as closings, but they are more often doorways."
  • "The last day of school felt like an ending; it was, in truth, a beginning I would not recognise for years."

Practice worksheet

Task 1. Fill in the blueprint for a piece on "leaving home": one line per stage.

Task 2. Write the opening hook for this piece.

Task 3. Write the concessive hinge sentence that moves to the other perspective.

Band 6 sample answer

Model plan (openings of each stage)

Hook: "The moving boxes were stacked in the hallway by seven; by eight the house was already someone else's." Question: "Was I leaving a home, or walking toward the one I had been building all along?" Perspective A: "There is a grief to leaving — the rooms hold your history." Concession: "And yet." Perspective B: "There is also a lightness — everywhere is new, and so too are you." Synthesis: "Perhaps endings and beginnings are the same door, seen from either side." Landing: "I still think of that hallway, and I have come to see that the boxes were never full of the past — they were full of the next place."

Marking criteria / self-check

  • I followed the 8-stage structure in order.
  • My stages flow naturally with clear transitions.
  • I held two perspectives in tension at the synthesis.
  • My landing is reflective, not a verdict.
  • My structure serves the journey, not a rigid formula.

Lesson 5 · 45 min

Language & Tone Toolkit

Intended learning: use hedging, concession, rhetorical questions and concrete detail to create a measured discursive tone — and avoid emotive excess.

Teaching content

Discursive language is the language of measured reflection. It avoids the shrill and the absolute, preferring words and structures that keep the door open to nuance. Four moves carry most of the weight:

  • Hedging: "may," "tends to," "arguably," "to some extent" — signals the writer is open to exception. Honesty, not weakness.
  • Concession: "while it is true that," "admittedly," "granted," "and yet" — grants a point before qualifying it, showing the writer has listened.
  • Rhetorical questions: "But is that always the case?" — models the act of inquiry and draws the reader in.
  • Concrete detail: specific sensory images that ground abstract ideas in lived experience.

Avoid: loaded emotive words ("outrageous," "shameful"), sweeping absolutes ("always," "never," "everyone knows"), and sarcasm aimed at opposing views.

Stimulus: "It might seem reckless. It might seem short sighted."
Exam-style question (8 marks · ~15 min): Using hedging and concession, rewrite the stimulus passage so it explores rather than asserts, then explain your choices in one sentence.

Starter sentence frames

  • "Arguably, …"
  • "And yet …"
  • "It would be easy to assume that … but …"
  • "Granted, this is …; nevertheless …"

Practice worksheet

Task 1. Soften each claim using hedging:

a) "Video games rot children's brains."

b) "Homework is useless."

Task 2. Add a concession to each sentence.

c) "Social media connects us."

Task 3. Replace one emotive word in a sentence of your own with a measured alternative.

Band 6 sample answer

Model rewrite

To some, such a choice may appear reckless; to others, it might seem short-sighted. Admittedly, there are reasons for concern — the outlay, the risk, the time away. And yet to reduce travel to a fiscal decision is to miss its quieter value: the way distance can sharpen the view of what we left behind. Perhaps the wiser question is not whether the trip was prudent, but what we risk losing by never going at all.

Explanation: I used hedging ("may," "perhaps"), concession ("admittedly," "and yet") and a rhetorical question to hold both perspectives openly rather than declaring one correct.

Marking criteria / self-check

  • I used hedging to soften absolute claims.
  • I used concession to grant the opposing view.
  • I avoided emotive and absolute language.
  • I included at least one rhetorical question.
  • My tone was measured and reflective.

Lesson 6 · 45 min

Craft Techniques: Metaphor, Anecdote & Imagery

Intended learning: use rhetorical craft — extended metaphor, anecdote, sensory imagery, aphorism — deliberately and analyse how a technique shapes meaning.

Teaching content

Craft in discursive writing is the deliberate, purposeful choice of language to create effect. NESA's Module C expects "sophisticated and sustained use of the craft." The most powerful techniques for the discursive mode are those that illuminate complexity rather than flatten it:

  • Extended metaphor: carries the central idea through the whole piece (e.g., a library as a "second home"). Adds depth and coherence.
  • Anecdote: a compressed story that makes an abstract idea human and trustworthy.
  • Sensory imagery: specific sights, smells, sounds that ground reflection in experience.
  • Aphorism: a concise, balanced statement that distils insight ("No one library can be my home; I am at home in all of them").

Use evidence — statistical, anecdotal, expert — as fuel for reflection, not as a weapon against a side. The reader should feel the writer has listened, not lobbied.

Stimulus: "I am like a bird flying south for the winter, never really able to stay in one place."
Exam-style question (8 marks · ~15 min): Create one extended metaphor of your own about movement or belonging, then in two sentences explain how it works to explore meaning.

Starter sentence frames

  • "I am like…"
  • "My mind is a… whose…"
  • "This city is a… that…"

Practice worksheet

Task 1. Identify the extended metaphor in the stimulus and state its effect.

Task 2. Write your own extended metaphor (2–3 sentences).

Task 3. Add one sensory image that supports your metaphor.

Band 6 sample answer

Model metaphor + explanation

I am like a lighthouse keeper whose light is always on, yet who stands on a shore no ship ever visits. I give and give my beam to others — steady, untiring — and wait, half-hoping, half-dreading, that a vessel might finally dock. Explanation: The metaphor explores the tension between service and solitude; comparing myself to a lighthouse allows me to hold "giving" and "isolation" together as a single image, so the piece reflects on loneliness without declaring it a tragedy or a virtue.

Marking criteria / self-check

  • My metaphor is sustained and coherent.
  • It illuminates, not decorates, the idea.
  • My sensory imagery is specific and purposeful.
  • I explained the effect on meaning, not just the technique name.
  • I used craft in service of exploration, not persuasion.

Analysing Real Discursive Texts

Intended learning: deconstruct authentic discursive texts using the five-stage analysis method and connect technique to meaning.

Teaching content

We learn to write discursively by reading how established composers do it. Three widely-studied texts model the register: Omar Sakr's memoir essay A Second Home, Mabel Kwong's What Does It Mean to Be Asian Australian?, and the personal essay Travel as the Ultimate Revenge. To analyse them, move through five stages: (1) identify the mode and purpose; (2) track the structural journey; (3) isolate the craft (technique + quote); (4) analyse effect in three layers; (5) synthesise back to Module C.

Stimulus (Sakr): "No one library can be my home, I am at home in all of them."
Stimulus (Kwong): "What does it mean to be Asian Australian?"
Stimulus (Travel): "Travel is more than just 'revenge' against a pandemic - it is a chance to start anew."
Exam-style question (12 marks · ~20 min): Choose ONE stimulus. Analyse how the composer uses ON key technique to achieve exploratory, discursive meaning. Support with close quotation.

Starter sentences

  • "Sakr's choice of the extended metaphor of 'home' reveals…"
  • "Kwong's decision to pose identity as a question rather than assert it shows…"
  • "Through the balanced aphorism, the composer…"

Band 6 sample analysis

Model (Sakr)

In A Second Home, Sakr's closing aphorism — "No one library can be my home, I am at home in all of them" — is the piece's discursive engine. The balanced structure (no one / all) refuses to settle on a single location, holding the idea that belonging is both impossible to localise and everywhere available. By coupling the negative ("no one") with the affirmative ("all of them"), Sakr explores identity as something carried rather than housed, inviting the reader to reflect on their own diffuse sense of home without prescribing an answer. This sustained, sincere exploration of a complex idea is precisely what Module C rewards.

Marking criteria / self-check

  • I named the mode and purpose of the text.
  • I quoted the technique precisely.
  • I analysed effect, not just identified technique.
  • I linked the technique to the exploratory mode.
  • I connected my analysis back to Module C.

Lesson 8 · 50 min

Planning & Drafting a Discursive Piece

Intended learning: move from prompt to a full plan and opening draft, and produce a statement of intention.

Teaching content

Good discursive writing begins long before the first sentence. In exam conditions (roughly 40 minutes for a Module C response), spend the first five minutes planning. Reframe the prompt as a question rather than a thesis. Sketch the eight-stage journey on one page. Choose your personal anecdote early. Decide where the concession will land. Then draft, aiming for restraint and precision over volume.

Many courses also require a short statement of intention — a few sentences explaining your choices of form, voice, structure and language. This reflection is your chance to show examiners you are crafting deliberately.

Stimulus: "Progress is not always forward."
Exam-style question (20 marks · ~40 min): Write a discursive response to the stimulus. In your response, demonstrate: a distinctive personal voice, balanced exploration, and deliberate craft. (Optionally add a short statement of intention.)

Starter sentences

  • "We assume that progress moves forward, but…"
  • "I once believed change was a straight line."
  • "The road of my life has rarely pointed where the sign said forward."

Practice worksheet

Task 1. Write the question version of the prompt.

Task 2. Sketch the 8-stage plan in one line each.

Task 3. Draft your opening paragraph.

Task 4. Write a two-sentence statement of intention.

Band 6 sample opening + intention

Model opening

We speak of progress as a movement forward, a journey toward something better — and I have no quarrel with the hope in that image. Yet my own small history has rarely moved in a straight line. The year I spent learning to play the piano, hours I once called wasted, has proved more useful to my way of thinking than any class I aced; the rounds I lost taught me more than the ones I won. Perhaps progress is less a line than a spiral — returning, again and again, to the same points, but each time a little higher up.

Statement of intention

I chose a personal anecdote to establish sincerity, used the spiral metaphor to hold "forward" and "return" in tension, and adopted a measured, hedged tone ("perhaps") to keep the piece exploratory rather than declarative.

Marking criteria / self-check

  • I reframed the prompt as a question before drafting.
  • I planned all eight stages before writing.
  • My draft shows a distinctive personal voice.
  • I used deliberate craft (metaphor, anecdote, hedging).
  • My statement of intention explains my choices.

Lesson 9 · 45 min

The Quiet Landing & Editing

Intended learning: conclude discursively with moderation and a resonant image, and edit for coherence, voice and control.

Teaching content

The ending is where discursive writing is most often lost. A triumphant, thunderous conclusion undoes the sincerity the piece has built. Instead, the discursive landing is quiet: it gathers the threads, returns to an opening image with new depth, expresses the writer's tentative lean, or poses a final question. It resists false certainty and leaves room for thought. Powerful landings often zip back to the very image that opened the piece, showing how far the journey has travelled.

Editing is where control and sophistication emerge. Read your draft for: consistency of register (did you slip into persuasion?); clarity of transitions; the strength of your strongest image; and whether every sentence earns its place. Cut the fluff; sharpen the hedges; ensure the voice stays sincere to the last line.

Stimulus: "Travel is more than 'revenge' — it is a chance to start anew."
Exam-style question (8 marks · ~15 min): Write TWO different discursive landings for an essay on travel — one that returns to an opening image, and one that ends on a question. Then edit a provided rough paragraph for tone.

Starter sentence frames for landings

  • "Perhaps the truth is…"
  • "And so I return to…"
  • "In the end, I am left with a question rather than an answer…"

Practice worksheet

Task 1. Write a landing that returns to your opening image.

Task 2. Write a landing that ends on a question.

Task 3. Edit this rough, shrill sentence to a measured, discursive tone: "Everyone knows travel is the best thing ever and anyone who disagrees is wrong!"

Band 6 sample landings

Landing 1 (returns to image)

I began with the memory of that 36-hour flight, the sun breaking over the clouds as we crossed the ocean. And so I return to it now — for that sun, I have come to believe, was not marking an ending but a first light, the beginning not of a holiday but of a self I had not yet met. Travel, in the end, is not revenge against anything; it is the quiet permission to begin again.

Landing 2 (ends on a question)

Perhaps the greatest gift of travel is not the places we see but the selves we become there. And I wonder, as I fold my passport back into my bag, whether home is not a place we return to, but a person we carry — and whether each journey is simply the chance to meet them again.

Marking criteria / self-check

  • My landing is moderate, not triumphant.
  • I returned to an image or ended on a question.
  • My ending reflects sincerity, not false certainty.
  • I edited for tone — removing emotive excess.
  • My register stayed discursive to the final line.

Lesson 10 · 45 min

Band 6 Marking Criteria & Self-Assessment

Intended learning: understand the Band 6 marking grid by course, self-assess a draft against it, and plan targeted improvement.

Teaching content

A Band 6 discursive response (roughly 45–50 marks out of 50) demonstrates sustained control and sophistication across the whole piece. The exact descriptors vary by course, but the core qualities NESA rewards are consistent:

  • Sustained control of register: Discursive from first line to last — never persuasive or purely expository.
  • A distinctive, sincere personal voice: The writer is genuinely present.
  • Sophisticated, sustained craft: Deliberate, purposeful choices throughout.
  • Balanced, genuine exploration: Complexity held, not flattened.
  • Strong awareness of audience and purpose.

Common pitfalls that prevent a Band 6: sliding into persuasion; vagueness disguised as balance; emotive overstatement; loss of personal voice; a falsely certain ending. Always confirm the precise descriptors in the current NESA syllabus for your course.

Stimulus: "We are not defined by where we come from, but by where we are going."
Exam-style question (20 marks · ~40 min): Write a discursive response to the stimulus, then self-assess it against the Band 6 grid below and note two improvements.

Starter sentences

  • "On reflection, my strongest technique was…"
  • "If I rewrote this, I would…"
  • "Where my draft slips toward persuasion is…"

Band 6 sample self-assessment

Model reflection

On reflection, my draft's strongest element was its consistent hedged tone — I kept the piece exploratory rather than shifting into persuasion. My weakest was structure: the concession arrived too late, so the balance felt uneven. If I rewrote this I would move the turning point earlier and strengthen the closing image, which currently repeats rather than develops my opening metaphor. These two adjustments — earlier synthesis and a deepened landing — would lift the piece toward sustained sophistication.

Marking criteria / self-check

  • I can state what a Band 6 requires in my course.
  • I self-assessed honestly against the grid.
  • I identified a strength with evidence.
  • I identified a specific weakness and a fix.
  • I know the common pitfalls that prevent a Band 6.

Adapt these opening lines for your own discursive pieces. Mix and match across the four functions.

Anecdotal openings

  • "The first time I understood what [idea] meant was…"

Questioning openings

  • "What does it truly mean to…?"
  • "Is it possible that the thing we fear most is also the thing we most need?"

Concessive openings

  • "It would be easy to assume that… yet…"
  • "Admittedly, [the counterview] has force; and still…"
  • "On one hand… on the other… and between them…"

  • "Perhaps the truth is less simple than we would like."
  • "I have changed my mind about [idea] more than once."
  • "In the end, I am less sure than I began — and that, I think, is the point."
