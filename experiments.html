HSC Chemistry Year 12 – Every Experiment, Organised by the Syllabus

Scope: Experiments only (the "conduct…" dot points of the 2017 NESA Stage 6 Chemistry syllabus). Secondary-source and modelling tasks are left out.

Source: The methods are condensed and rewritten in my own words from the investigations in Chemistry in Focus Year 12, with equipment and quantities built in. Where the syllabus gives an "e.g." and the book left the method open, I wrote a standard version. Check anything that differs from what your class did.

How to use each entry

Each experiment has: the syllabus dot point it satisfies, aim, hypothesis, variables, method (with quantities), diagram checklist, risk assessment (hazard → precaution → why), results/analysis, and evaluation.

The exam rules for any "outline an investigation" question

  • Put equipment, substances and realistic quantities inside the method steps. Don't write a separate materials list.
  • Realistic scale: 2 mL of reagent in a test tube, 20.00 mL pipetted aliquots, 100 mL beakers. A guess like 50 mL in a test tube gets no mark.
  • Diagrams: pencil, ruler, fully labelled, with the substances and their quantities drawn in.
  • Risk assessment: hazard → precaution → why the precaution works.
  • Aims never start "to prove". Hypotheses are supported or not supported.

Terms examiners expect you to use correctly

  • Independent variable: what you change. Dependent variable: what you measure. Controlled variables: what you keep constant, and how.
  • Valid: a fair test that answers the question (one variable changed, others controlled).
  • Reliable: repeat trials give consistent results.
  • Accurate: close to the true value. Precise: repeat values close together.
  • Random error gives scatter (reduce it by repeating). Systematic error gives a constant offset (calibration, parallax, heat loss).
  • Uncertainty: analogue = ±½ smallest division; digital = ± one whole division.
  • Improving reliability: repeat trials and average. Improving validity: control more variables. Improving accuracy: better equipment or calibration.

MODULE 5 – EQUILIBRIUM AND ACID REACTIONS

Experiment 1 – Reversibility of chemical reactions

Syllabus: Conduct practical investigations to analyse the reversibility of chemical reactions, e.g. CoCl₂ hydrated and dehydrated; Fe(NO₃)₃ and KSCN; burning magnesium; burning steel wool.

Aim: To observe a range of reactions and decide whether each is reversible.

Hypothesis: The cobalt(II) chloride and iron(III) thiocyanate reactions are reversible, while burning magnesium and steel wool are not.

Variables: Independent: the reaction type or the change applied. Dependent: observed colour or mass. Controlled: quantities of reagents, same oven temperature.

Method

  1. CoCl₂ paper: lay a 55 mm filter paper on a watch glass. Add 0.1 mol L⁻¹ cobalt(II) chloride dropwise with a dropper until the paper is covered (pink). Dry it in an oven at 35°C overnight and record the colour (blue). Add distilled water dropwise until covered and record the colour (pink). Dry it again and record.
  2. Iron(III) thiocyanate: pipette 1 mL of 0.1 mol L⁻¹ Fe(NO₃)₃ and 1 mL of 0.1 mol L⁻¹ KSCN into a 25 mL measuring cylinder. Make up to 20 mL with distilled water (pale red). Pour 5 mL into each of two test tubes, keeping one as a reference. To the other add 1 mL of 0.1 mol L⁻¹ fluoride solution (the red fades as Fe³⁺ is removed), then add 2 mL of 0.1 mol L⁻¹ Fe(NO₃)₃ (the red returns).
  3. Burning magnesium: hold a 5 cm piece of sandpapered magnesium ribbon in brass tongs over a heatproof mat. Ignite it with a gas lighter, looking away from the flame. Record the product (white powder). Add a few drops of water to the cooled product and try to regenerate the metal by heating.
  4. Burning steel wool: weigh a 5 × 5 cm piece of steel wool (±0.01 g) on a heatproof mat. Ignite it with the lighter, let it cool, and reweigh.

Diagram: watch glass with filter paper and dropper in a drying oven; test-tube rack with labelled tubes and volumes; tongs holding Mg over a heatproof mat; balance with steel wool.

Risk assessment

  • Cobalt(II) chloride is toxic to humans and wildlife. Use a dropper and brass tongs and wear gloves, so there is no skin contact or ingestion and no spills into waterways.
  • Fluoride and thiocyanate solutions are harmful. Wear gloves and goggles and collect waste in the waste bottle, because they are toxic if absorbed.
  • Burning magnesium emits intense white light and UV. Look away or wear tinted goggles, because the light can damage the retina.
  • Hot metal burns. Use tongs and a heatproof mat, because direct contact gives burns.

Results and analysis

  • CoCl₂ paper is pink when hydrated, blue when dry, and pink again with water, so the reaction is reversible: [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ (pink) ⇌ CoCl₂ (blue) + 6H₂O.
  • Fe³⁺ + SCN⁻ ⇌ FeSCN²⁺ (red): adding F⁻ removes Fe³⁺ and the red fades; adding Fe³⁺ restores it. The reaction is reversible.
  • Mg burns to form MgO (2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO) and steel wool burns with a mass increase because iron combines with oxygen from the air. Neither reverses on adding water or cooling, so they are not reversible. They run in open systems and the products can't regenerate the reactants.

Evaluation: Use the same quantities each time. Photograph the colours for a record. Repeat for reliability. The mass gain of steel wool is small, so use a balance with ±0.01 g or better.

Experiment 2 – Modelling dynamic equilibrium

Syllabus: Model static and dynamic equilibrium and analyse the differences between open and closed systems.

Aim: To model a reversible reaction and determine when dynamic equilibrium is reached.

Hypothesis: The volumes in the two cylinders will stop changing, because the forward and reverse transfer will become equal.

Variables: Independent: number of cycles. Dependent: volume in each cylinder. Controlled: cylinder size, pipette sizes, technique.

Method

  1. Label two identical 100 mL measuring cylinders A and B. Fill A with 100 mL of water; B is empty.
  2. Place a 10 mL graduated pipette upside down in A so the wide end is under water and let it fill. Seal the narrow tip with a thumb and transfer the contents to B (forward reaction).
  3. Do the same with a 5 mL graduated pipette from B back to A (reverse reaction). That is one cycle.
  4. Record the volume in each cylinder after every cycle for 50 cycles.
  5. Plot volume against cycle number for A and B on the same axes.

Diagram: two cylinders A and B with arrows labelled "forward: 10 mL pipette" and "reverse: 5 mL pipette", and volumes marked.

Risk assessment

  • Glass can break and cut. Keep cylinders and pipettes back from the bench edge and lay pipettes where they can't roll, so they don't fall and shatter.

Results and analysis: The volumes change quickly at first, then stay constant. The graph lines level off. Compare the slopes of the first five cycles with the last five. At equilibrium the forward and reverse transfers are equal, so there is no net change, but the transfer continues (dynamic). This models a closed system. Static equilibrium would mean no transfer at all.

Evaluation: Compared with a real chemical reaction, the model uses fixed volumes rather than rates that depend on concentration. Water level affects how much each pipette collects. Repeat and use the same operator for consistency.

Experiment 3 – Effect of concentration on equilibrium (Fe³⁺ + SCN⁻ ⇌ FeSCN²⁺)

Syllabus: Investigate the effects of concentration… on a system at equilibrium and explain with Le Chatelier's principle, e.g. iron(III) thiocyanate and varying concentration of ions.

Aim: To observe the effect of changing concentration on a system at equilibrium.

Hypothesis: Increasing [Fe³⁺] or [SCN⁻] will shift the equilibrium right (darker red); removing Fe³⁺ will shift it left (paler).

Variables: Independent: the ion added. Dependent: colour of the solution. Controlled: volume of equilibrium mixture (about 5 mL), volume of each addition (1 mL), temperature.

Method

  1. Pour 1 mL of 0.1 mol L⁻¹ iron(III) chloride into a 10 mL measuring cylinder and 1 mL of 0.1 mol L⁻¹ ammonium thiocyanate into another. Pour both into a 25 mL measuring cylinder and make up to 20 mL with distilled water.
  2. Label four test tubes A–D and pour equal portions (about 5 mL) of this solution into each. Keep A as the reference.
  3. Add 1 mL of 0.1 mol L⁻¹ FeCl₃ to B, 1 mL of 0.1 mol L⁻¹ NH₄SCN to C, and 1 mL of fluoride solution (calcium fluoride in the book) to D, which removes Fe³⁺.
  4. Photograph each tube against A on a white background.

Diagram: rack with four test tubes A–D, each with 5 mL and the 1 mL addition labelled.

Risk assessment

  • Splashes can irritate the eyes and skin. Wear goggles and wash hands afterwards, because the solutions are mildly harmful.
  • FeSCN²⁺ is harmful to aquatic life. Collect waste in a waste bottle, not the sink, so it doesn't reach waterways.

Results and analysis: B and C are darker red than A; D is paler. Fe³⁺ + SCN⁻ ⇌ FeSCN²⁺. Adding a reactant increases the rate of the forward reaction (more frequent collisions), so the system shifts right until a new equilibrium is reached. Removing Fe³⁺ lowers the forward rate, so the reverse reaction dominates and the system shifts left.

Evaluation: Colour comparison is qualitative. Using a colourimeter would give quantitative data. Keep the volumes identical for a fair comparison.

Experiment 4 – Effect of temperature on equilibrium ([Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ ⇌ [CoCl₄]²⁻)

Syllabus: Investigate the effects of temperature… e.g. heating cobalt(II) chloride hydrate.

Aim: To observe the effect of changing temperature on a system at equilibrium.

Hypothesis: Heating will turn the solution blue (shift right) and cooling will turn it pink (shift left), showing the forward reaction is endothermic.

Variables: Independent: temperature. Dependent: colour. Controlled: concentration and volume of the cobalt solution, amount of chloride.

Method

  1. Dissolve a pea-sized amount of CoCl₂·6H₂O in 1–2 mL of distilled water in a test tube (pink). Keep it as a reference.
  2. In a fume cupboard, the teacher dissolves a pea-sized amount in 1–2 mL of concentrated HCl in a second tube (blue). Keep as a reference.
  3. In a third tube, dissolve a pea-sized amount in 1–2 mL of water, then add concentrated HCl dropwise (teacher) until the solution is purple (equilibrium mixture of both ions).
  4. Hold the purple tube with tongs in a 250 mL beaker of hot water (about 70°C) and record the colour after 2 minutes. Then place it in a beaker of ice water (about 0°C) and record again.

Diagram: three test tubes (references and equilibrium mixture), hot-water bath with thermometer, ice bath.

Risk assessment

  • Concentrated HCl is very corrosive. The teacher dispenses it in a fume cupboard, because it burns skin and gives off irritating fumes.
  • Cobalt(II) chloride is an irritant and a suspected carcinogen. Wear gloves and goggles and wash hands, because it can be absorbed through skin or inhaled as dust.
  • Hot water can scald. Use tongs and a stable beaker, because spills burn.

Results and analysis: [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺(aq) (pink) + 4Cl⁻(aq) ⇌ [CoCl₄]²⁻(aq) (blue) + 6H₂O(l). Heating gives blue, so the forward reaction is favoured and is endothermic (ΔH > 0). Cooling gives pink. Le Chatelier: increasing temperature favours the endothermic direction. By collision theory, more particles have energy ≥ activation energy at the higher temperature, and the endothermic reaction speeds up more.

Evaluation: A thermometer in each bath gives a controlled temperature. Compare against the references.

Experiment 5 – Effect of volume and pressure on a gaseous equilibrium (NO₂ ⇌ N₂O₄)

Syllabus: Investigate the effects of… volume and/or pressure on a system at equilibrium, e.g. interaction between NO₂ and N₂O₄.

Aim: To observe the effect of changing the volume of a gaseous equilibrium system.

Hypothesis: Reducing the volume will first darken the gas, then the colour will fade as the system shifts toward N₂O₄ (fewer gas moles).

Method (teacher demonstration)

  1. In a fume cupboard, hold a large sealed syringe containing NO₂/N₂O₄ and record the colour of the mixture.
  2. Quickly push in the plunger to reduce the volume (e.g. from 50 mL to 25 mL). Record the immediate colour, then again after about 30 seconds.
  3. Pull the plunger back to increase the volume and record the changes.

Risk assessment

  • NO₂/N₂O₄ is toxic and corrosive to skin, eyes and lungs. Use a sealed syringe in a fume cupboard, so no gas escapes into the room.

Results and analysis: 2NO₂(g) (brown) ⇌ N₂O₄(g) (colourless). Compression first makes the gas darker (the same moles in a smaller volume, higher concentration). It then lightens as the system shifts to the side with fewer gas moles (N₂O₄). By collision theory, higher pressure increases collision frequency of both reactants and products, but the forward reaction (2 mol → 1 mol) is affected more. Expanding gives the opposite.

Experiment 6 – Keq of the iron(III) thiocyanate system

Syllabus: Conduct an investigation to determine Keq of a chemical equilibrium system, e.g. Keq of the iron(III) thiocyanate equilibrium.

Aim: To use colourimetry to determine Keq for Fe³⁺ + SCN⁻ ⇌ FeSCN²⁺.

Hypothesis: Keq will be consistent across mixtures of different starting concentrations at the same temperature.

Variables: Independent: initial concentrations of Fe³⁺ and SCN⁻. Dependent: absorbance (hence [FeSCN²⁺]). Controlled: wavelength (470 nm), temperature, acid concentration, cuvette path length.

Method Part A – calibration standards (excess Fe³⁺ forces all SCN⁻ into FeSCN²⁺)

  1. Label six 100 mL volumetric flasks A–F. Pipette 25.00 mL of 0.200 mol L⁻¹ Fe(NO₃)₃ into each.
  2. Add 1.00, 2.00, 4.00, 6.00, 8.00 and 10.00 mL of 0.00200 mol L⁻¹ KSCN respectively, using a graduated pipette.
  3. Make each up to the 100.00 mL line with 0.5 mol L⁻¹ HNO₃ (dropper for the last few mL). Stopper and invert.
  4. Set the colourimeter to 470 nm and zero it with a cuvette ¾ full of distilled water.
  5. Rinse a cuvette and beaker with each solution in turn, fill the cuvette ¾, wipe the clear sides, and record the absorbance of A–F. Plot absorbance against [FeSCN²⁺] (= initial [SCN⁻] after dilution).

Part B – equilibrium mixtures (your teacher's table may differ) 6. In five labelled flasks mix 5.00 mL of 0.00200 mol L⁻¹ Fe(NO₃)₃ with 1.00, 2.00, 3.00, 4.00 and 5.00 mL of 0.00200 mol L⁻¹ KSCN, then make each up to 10.00 mL with 0.5 mol L⁻¹ HNO₃. 7. Measure the absorbance of each at 470 nm. Read [FeSCN²⁺]eq from the calibration graph.

Diagram: six 100 mL volumetric flasks with the reagent volumes marked; colourimeter with cuvette.

Risk assessment

  • Nitric acid is corrosive. Wear goggles and gloves, because splashes burn skin and eyes.
  • Iron(III) thiocyanate is harmful to aquatic life. Collect waste in the waste bottle so it doesn't enter the sink.
  • Glassware may break. Keep it away from the bench edge and hold the pipette with its filler attached.

Calculation

  • [Fe³⁺]initial and [SCN⁻]initial after mixing (C₁V₁ = C₂V₂).
  • ICE table: [FeSCN²⁺]eq = x (from the graph), [Fe³⁺]eq = [Fe³⁺]initial − x, [SCN⁻]eq = [SCN⁻]initial − x.
  • Keq = [FeSCN²⁺] ÷ ([Fe³⁺][SCN⁻]). Average across mixtures. Typical values are a few hundred (check your class value).

Evaluation: Use the same cuvette orientation. Keep the temperature constant, since Keq changes with temperature. Check linearity of the calibration curve (Beer–Lambert: A ∝ c). Air bubbles and fingerprints on the cuvette are common sources of error. Use fresh solutions.

Experiment 7 – Solubility rules and predicting precipitates

Syllabus: Conduct an investigation to determine solubility rules, and predict and analyse the composition of substances when two ionic solutions are mixed, e.g. KCl + AgNO₃, KI + Pb(NO₃)₂, Na₂SO₄ + Ba(NO₃)₂.

Aim: To compare the solubility of ionic compounds through precipitation reactions and construct solubility rules.

Hypothesis: All nitrates and group 1 salts will be soluble; the combinations Ag⁺/Cl⁻, Pb²⁺/I⁻ and Ba²⁺/SO₄²⁻ will precipitate.

Variables: Independent: the pair of ions mixed. Dependent: whether a precipitate forms and its colour. Controlled: concentration (0.1 mol L⁻¹), volume (one drop each), background.

Method

  1. Draw a grid on an overhead projector sheet with anions across the top (e.g. nitrate, chloride, sulfate, carbonate, acetate, hydroxide) and cations down the side (e.g. Na⁺, Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, NH₄⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺, Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺, Al³⁺, Ba²⁺, Ca²⁺, Fe³⁺).
  2. Place the sheet on a dark background (black paper or a dark bench).
  3. In each square put one drop of the cation solution and one drop of the anion solution (all 0.1 mol L⁻¹, lead nitrate 0.02 mol L⁻¹) from dropper bottles, one on top of the other.
  4. Record whether a precipitate forms and its colour. Wipe with paper towel and collect waste.
  5. Write net ionic equations for each precipitate. Compare your rules with a solubility data table.

Diagram: the grid with dropper bottles, showing two drops in one square.

Risk assessment

  • Splashes irritate skin and eyes. Wear goggles and wash with soap and water if contact occurs.
  • Lead, barium and silver salts are toxic and silver stains skin. Wear gloves, wipe up with paper towel and put it in the heavy-metal waste, not the sink, so toxic ions don't enter waterways.

Results and analysis

  • KCl + AgNO₃ → white AgCl: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s).
  • KI + Pb(NO₃)₂ → yellow PbI₂: Pb²⁺(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → PbI₂(s).
  • Na₂SO₄ + Ba(NO₃)₂ → white BaSO₄: Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s).
  • Spectator ions (K⁺, Na⁺, NO₃⁻) stay in solution. Typical rules: all nitrates, group 1 salts and ammonium salts are soluble; most carbonates and hydroxides are insoluble except group 1.

Evaluation: Differences from published rules may come from slightly soluble salts (e.g. Ca(OH)₂, Ag₂SO₄), impure reagents or the tiny drop volumes. Repeating in test tubes with 1 mL gives a clearer observation.

MODULE 6 – ACID/BASE REACTIONS

Experiment 8 – Preparing and using indicators

Syllabus: Conduct an investigation to demonstrate the preparation and use of indicators as illustrators of the characteristics and properties of acids and bases and their reversible reactions.

Aim: To prepare natural indicators and test them on a range of substances.

Hypothesis: The natural indicators will give a range of different colours depending on the acidity or alkalinity of the substance.

Variables: Independent: the substance tested (and the indicator). Dependent: colour. Controlled: volume of substance (2 mL), number of indicator drops.

Method

  1. Red cabbage: shred two leaves into a 500 mL beaker and just cover with distilled water (about 200 mL). Heat on a hotplate and boil gently until the water is dark red-purple. Cool, then pour the liquid into a clean 100 mL beaker.
  2. Other plants (blueberries, petals, turmeric): grind in a mortar with 5–10 mL of warm water and strain through a filter into a beaker.
  3. Put 2 mL each of 0.1 mol L⁻¹ NaOH, 0.1 mol L⁻¹ HCl, vinegar, household ammonia, lemon juice, lemonade, bicarbonate of soda solution, washing powder solution, antacid solution and salt water into labelled test tubes in a rack.
  4. Add a few drops of the indicator to each and record the colour. Repeat for each indicator and compare with universal indicator.
  5. Reversibility: place 2 mL of HCl in a test tube with a few drops of indicator. Add NaOH dropwise until the colour stops changing. Then add HCl dropwise and watch the colour return.

Diagram: test-tube rack with labelled substances (2 mL) and indicator drops; the cabbage beaker on a hotplate.

Risk assessment

  • NaOH is caustic and HCl corrosive. Wear goggles and protective clothing and wash spills with plenty of water, because they burn skin.
  • Boiling water and a hotplate burn. Pour away from the body and use tongs, so scalds are avoided.
  • Ammonia vapour irritates the lungs. Keep it stoppered and use a ventilated area.

Results and analysis: Red cabbage is red/pink in acids, purple when neutral, and blue/green/yellow in bases. Indicators change colour at different pH ranges, so some are better than others. The colour change reverses on adding acid or base, showing the reaction is reversible: HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻. Universal indicator gives a more complete pH range than a single indicator.

Evaluation: Colour judgement is subjective; photograph against a white background. Home-made extracts vary in concentration, so use the same amount each time.

Experiment 9 – Enthalpy of neutralisation

Syllabus: Conduct a practical investigation to measure the enthalpy of neutralisation.

Aim: To determine the enthalpy of neutralisation of HCl with NaOH.

Hypothesis: The enthalpy change will be about −57 kJ per mole of water formed, for a strong acid and strong base.

Variables: Independent: the reactants (acid and base). Dependent: temperature change. Controlled: volumes, concentrations, same polystyrene cup, stirring.

Method

  1. Measure 50 mL of 2.0 mol L⁻¹ NaOH in a 100 mL measuring cylinder and pour it into a polystyrene cup standing in a 250 mL beaker. Record the temperature to ±0.1°C.
  2. Measure 50 mL of 2.0 mol L⁻¹ HCl in the cylinder and record its temperature. Average the two as the initial temperature.
  3. Pour the acid into the base, cover with a lid, stir gently with the thermometer and record the highest temperature reached.
  4. Repeat for reliability.
  5. (Optional Part A) Pour 100 mL of 1.0 mol L⁻¹ HCl into a cup. Weigh about 4.0 g of NaOH(s) and add it. Stir and record the highest temperature. Use the difference between A and B to explain the effect of the solid dissolving.

Diagram: polystyrene cup with lid, thermometer, 100 mL of solution and a surrounding beaker.

Risk assessment

  • NaOH is caustic. Use a spatula and never your fingers, because it burns skin.
  • HCl is corrosive. Wear goggles and protective clothing and pour carefully, to prevent splashes.

Calculation (example)

  • q = mcΔT with m = 100 g, c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. If ΔT = 13.7°C, q = 100 × 4.18 × 13.7 = 5.73 kJ.
  • n(H₂O) = 0.100 mol (50 mL × 2.0 mol L⁻¹ = 0.100 mol).
  • ΔH = −q ÷ n = −57 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O; net ionic equation: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.

Evaluation: Heat is lost to the surroundings, so the measured value is lower in magnitude than the accepted value. Improve by using a lid and insulation, stirring consistently, and plotting temperature against time to extrapolate to the true maximum. The solution is assumed to have the same specific heat capacity and density as water.

Experiment 10 – Measuring pH of a range of acids and bases

Syllabus: Conduct a practical investigation to measure the pH of a range of acids and bases.

Aim: To measure and compare the pH of various substances using universal indicator and a pH meter.

Hypothesis: The pH meter will give more precise values than universal indicator.

Variables: Independent: the substance tested. Dependent: pH. Controlled: the volume of sample, calibration of the meter, rinsing between samples.

Method

  1. Calibrate the pH meter with buffer solutions (pH 4.01 and 7.00, and 10.01 if available).
  2. Place a few mL (enough to cover the probe) of each sample in a labelled test tube: vinegar, lemon juice, dishwasher detergent, shampoo, antacid solution, baking soda solution, vitamin C solution, bleach, soda water, and 1 and 0.0001 mol L⁻¹ HCl and NaOH. Dissolve solids in distilled water first.
  3. Measure the pH with the meter and record it. Rinse the probe with distilled water between each sample.
  4. Add one drop of universal indicator to each sample, record the colour and the pH from the chart.
  5. Test water samples (pool, creek) in the same way; filter or let sediment settle first.

Diagram: test-tube rack with labelled samples, a pH meter probe in a tube, universal indicator chart.

Risk assessment

  • Household chemicals, NaOH and HCl can burn. Wear goggles and protective clothing, and tell the teacher about spills.
  • Food products can be contaminated in the lab. Never taste or eat them.

Results and analysis: Acids are below 7 (vinegar about 2.5, lemon juice about 2), neutrals about 7, and bases above 7 (detergent, bleach about 12). pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]; 1 mol L⁻¹ HCl is pH 0 and 0.0001 mol L⁻¹ HCl is pH 4. The meter gives values to ±0.01 whereas universal indicator is only about ±0.5–1.

Evaluation: An uncalibrated meter gives systematic errors. Temperature affects pH. A dirty probe gives cross-contamination.

Experiment 11 – pH and the strength of acids and bases

Syllabus: Conduct an investigation to demonstrate the use of pH to indicate the differences between the strength of acids and bases.

Aim: To compare the pH of strong and weak acids and bases at the same and at different concentrations.

Hypothesis: At the same concentration, strong acids and bases will be more extreme in pH than weak ones.

Variables: Independent: the acid or base (and its concentration). Dependent: pH. Controlled: volume (25 mL), temperature, calibrated pH meter.

Method

  1. Same concentration: pour 25 mL of each 0.1 mol L⁻¹ solution (HCl, CH₃COOH, NaOH, NH₃) into separate clean 50 mL beakers. Measure each pH with a calibrated meter, rinsing the probe with distilled water between samples.
  2. Different concentrations: repeat with 25 mL of 0.01 mol L⁻¹ HCl and 0.01 mol L⁻¹ NaOH.

Risk assessment

  • NaOH and HCl are corrosive. Wear goggles and protective clothing and rinse spills with plenty of water.
  • Ammonia vapour irritates. Use in a ventilated area.

Results and analysis: 0.1 mol L⁻¹ HCl is about pH 1; 0.1 mol L⁻¹ CH₃COOH is about pH 2.9. HCl ionises fully (HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻), acetic acid only partially (CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + CH₃COO⁻). 0.1 mol L⁻¹ NaOH is about pH 13 and 0.1 mol L⁻¹ NH₃ is about pH 11. A tenfold dilution of a strong acid raises its pH by 1. Strength is how much it ionises; concentration is how much is dissolved.

Experiment 12 – Primary standard solution and titration

Syllabus: Conduct practical investigations to analyse the concentration of an unknown acid or base by titration.

Aim: To prepare a primary standard of sodium carbonate and use it to find the concentration of a hydrochloric acid solution.

Hypothesis: The concentration of HCl can be determined accurately from concordant titres.

Variables: Independent: volume of HCl added. Dependent: the end-point volume (titre). Controlled: volume of Na₂CO₃ (25.00 mL), indicator (2 drops), rinsing technique.

Method Part A – primary standard (0.0500 mol L⁻¹ Na₂CO₃)

  1. Tare a clean, dry 150 mL beaker on a balance. Weigh about 1.4 g of anhydrous Na₂CO₃ to ±0.001 g and record the exact mass.
  2. Add about 80 mL of distilled water and stir until dissolved.
  3. Pour through a funnel into a 250.0 mL volumetric flask. Rinse the beaker three times with distilled water and add the rinsings, then rinse the funnel into the flask.
  4. Fill with distilled water until the bottom of the meniscus touches the line (dropper for the last few mL, eye level). Stopper and invert several times to mix.
  5. Calculate c = m ÷ (M × 0.2500), to 4 significant figures. Example: 1.400 g ÷ (105.99 × 0.2500) = 0.05284 mol L⁻¹.

Part B – titration 6. Rinse the burette with a small volume of the HCl and fill it, removing air from the tip. Record the initial reading to ±0.05 mL. 7. Rinse the 25.00 mL pipette with the Na₂CO₃ solution. Pipette 25.00 mL into a 250 mL conical flask that has been rinsed with distilled water only. Add 2 drops of methyl orange. 8. Place the flask on a white tile under the burette. Titrate with swirling to the first permanent orange-red, adding dropwise near the end. Record the final reading. 9. Repeat until you have titres agreeing within 0.10 mL (concordant). Average the concordant titres.

Diagram: burette with HCl clamped over a conical flask on a white tile with 25.00 mL Na₂CO₃ and 2 drops of methyl orange; separate sketch of the volumetric flask with the meniscus on the line.

Risk assessment

  • Solid Na₂CO₃ irritates the skin and eyes. Wear goggles and wash hands afterwards.
  • Glassware can break and cut. Keep it from the edge and leave the pipette filler on the pipette so it can't roll off.
  • Dilute acid may splash. Fill the burette below eye level using a funnel, so acid does not reach the face.

Calculation: Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂, so n(HCl) = 2 × n(Na₂CO₃). c(HCl) = n(HCl) ÷ V(titre in L).

Why it matters (common exam questions)

  • Rinse the burette with the solution it will hold (water would dilute it), the pipette with its own solution, and the conical flask with water only (rinsing with sample would add extra moles).
  • Anhydrous Na₂CO₃ is a primary standard because it is pure, stable and has a known formula. The hydrate's water content is variable.
  • Methyl orange suits a strong acid with a weak base because the equivalence point is acidic. Equivalence point is the exact stoichiometric point; end point is the observed colour change. They should be close.
  • Parallax error: read the burette with the eye level with the meniscus.

Evaluation: Concordant titres show reliability. Use a white tile to judge the colour change. A burette reading uncertainty is ±0.05 mL. Overshooting the end point is the most common random error.

Experiment 13 – Chemical analysis of a household substance

Syllabus: Conduct a chemical analysis of a common household substance for its acidity or basicity, e.g. soft drink, wine, juice, medicine.

Aim: To determine the acid content of orange juice by titration with NaOH, and the aspirin content of a tablet by back titration.

Part A – orange juice

Hypothesis: The acidity of the juice (as citric acid) can be determined from the pH–volume curve.

Method

  1. Weigh a clean 250 mL beaker, add about 20 mL of juice and reweigh (mass of juice).
  2. Add about 50 mL of distilled water so the pH electrode is covered.
  3. Fill a burette with about 0.1 mol L⁻¹ standardised NaOH. Place the calibrated pH meter in the beaker and record the initial pH.
  4. Add NaOH in 1 mL steps, recording pH after each, until the pH is steady in the basic region.
  5. Repeat with smaller steps (0.2 mL) near the steep rise.
  6. Plot pH against volume of NaOH. Find the equivalence volume from the middle of the steep rise.

Why a pH meter: the orange colour hides indicator colour changes.

Calculation: Citric acid is triprotic: H₃C₆H₅O₇ + 3NaOH → Na₃C₆H₅O₇ + 3H₂O. n(NaOH) = c × V; n(acid) = n(NaOH) ÷ 3; mass = n × 192.12 g mol⁻¹; % w/w = mass ÷ mass of juice × 100.

Part B – aspirin by back titration

Method

  1. Blank: pipette 5.00 mL of ethanol and 25.00 mL of 0.5 mol L⁻¹ NaOH into a 250 mL conical flask. Add 1–2 drops of phenolphthalein. Titrate with 0.5 mol L⁻¹ HCl until the pink just disappears. Repeat until concordant.
  2. Sample: crush an aspirin tablet in a mortar and weigh about 0.5 g into a flask. Add 5.00 mL of ethanol and 25.00 mL of 0.5 mol L⁻¹ NaOH. Stopper and leave for 1 hour.
  3. Add indicator and titrate the excess NaOH with 0.5 mol L⁻¹ HCl to the colourless end point.
  4. n(NaOH reacted with aspirin) = n(NaOH in blank) − n(NaOH in sample). Aspirin reacts with 2 NaOH (acid plus ester hydrolysis), so n(aspirin) = ½ × that.

Why back titration: the weak acid with strong base gives a poor end point; a strong acid with strong base gives a sharp one.

Risk assessment

  • NaOH splashes irritate and burn. Wear goggles and wash hands.
  • Ethanol is flammable. Keep it away from flames and work in a ventilated area, because vapour ignites.
  • Glassware can break. Keep it away from the bench edge.

Evaluation: Compare the result with the label value and calculate percentage error. Likely errors: incomplete hydrolysis, loss of sample, other acids in the juice (assumed all citric acid).

Experiment 17 – Enthalpy of combustion of alcohols

Syllabus: Conduct a practical investigation to measure and reliably compare the enthalpy of combustion for a range of alcohols.

Aim: To measure the enthalpy of combustion of several alcohols and compare it with theoretical values.

Hypothesis: The enthalpy of combustion per mole will increase with chain length.

Variables: Independent: the alcohol (molar mass/chain length). Dependent: temperature rise of the water (and mass of fuel burnt). Controlled: 100 mL of water, same beaker, distance from flame to beaker (about 5 cm), same temperature rise, draught shields.

Method

  1. Measure 100 mL of water in a measuring cylinder into a 250 mL beaker and clamp it about 5 cm above a spirit burner. Record the initial temperature to ±0.5°C.
  2. Weigh the spirit burner containing methanol, with its cap, on a balance (±0.01 g).
  3. Light the burner and stir the water gently with the thermometer. When the temperature has risen by 20°C, extinguish with the cap.
  4. Reweigh the burner and calculate the mass of alcohol burnt.
  5. Repeat with ethanol, 1-propanol, 1-butanol and 1-pentanol using fresh water each time, and with repeats for reliability.

Diagram: beaker on a clamp and retort stand with 100 mL water, thermometer, spirit burner of alcohol, 5 cm gap marked, draught shield.

Risk assessment

  • Alcohols are flammable liquids. Use spirit burners with caps so the fuel is separate from the flame, and keep the bench clear.
  • Hot glassware burns. Use beaker gloves and allow equipment to cool, because direct contact scalds.
  • Methanol is toxic. Don't inhale the vapour and wash hands afterwards.

Evaluation: Values are often far lower than the accepted values because heat is lost to the air and beaker, combustion is incomplete (soot), and the wick or fuel evaporates. Improve with a draught shield and a copper calorimeter, with the flame closer to the container, and by stirring constantly.

Experiment 19 – Production of alcohol by fermentation

Syllabus: Investigate the production of alcohols, including fermentation.

Experiment 20 – Production of an ester

Syllabus: Investigate the production, in a school laboratory, of simple esters.

Aim: To make and purify an ester (pentyl ethanoate).

Hypothesis: Refluxing a carboxylic acid and an alcohol with an acid catalyst will produce a fruity-smelling ester that can be purified by washing.

Variables: Independent: the alcohol and carboxylic acid used. Dependent: ester formed (smell, volume). Controlled: reflux time (30 minutes), amounts, catalyst.

Method

  1. Into a round-bottom flask add 10 mL of 1-pentanol, 12 mL of glacial ethanoic acid, 1 mL of concentrated H₂SO₄ and a few boiling chips. Clamp the flask securely.
  2. Fit a condenser vertically. Connect water in at the bottom and out at the top. Do not stopper the top.
  3. Heat with a heating mantle at a constant rate for about 30 minutes, making sure vapours do not escape from the top of the condenser.
  4. Cool, then decant (without the chips) into a separating funnel containing 15 mL of water. Stopper, shake, vent, let the layers separate and drain off the lower aqueous layer.
  5. Add about 15 mL of 1.0 mol L⁻¹ sodium carbonate in portions, venting the CO₂. Shake, vent again, and drain the aqueous layer.
  6. Add 10 mL of deionised water, shake, separate and discard the aqueous layer.
  7. If time permits, distil the ester, collecting the liquid near its boiling point (149°C for pentyl ethanoate).
  8. Record the smell, colour and volume.

Diagram: reflux apparatus with round-bottom flask (reagent volumes labelled), boiling chips, heating mantle, vertical condenser with water in and out, clamps; separate sketch of a separating funnel with the two layers.

Risk assessment

  • Organic liquids are flammable. Use a heating mantle instead of a Bunsen burner and keep reagents away from flames, because vapours ignite.
  • Vapours are toxic and irritating. Work in a fume cupboard and keep containers stoppered, to avoid inhalation.
  • Concentrated H₂SO₄ is corrosive. The teacher dispenses it and PPE is worn, because it causes severe burns.
  • Gas can build up. Leave the condenser top open and vent the funnel after shaking, because trapped pressure can eject the stopper.

Results and analysis: CH₃COOH + C₅H₁₁OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₅H₁₁ + H₂O (esterification, condensation). The ester has a banana-like smell.

  • Why reflux: it lets you heat a volatile mixture for a long time without losing reactants or products (vapour condenses and returns).
  • Why concentrated H₂SO₄: catalyst, and it absorbs water, shifting the equilibrium toward the ester.
  • Why excess acid: shifts the equilibrium to the right (Le Chatelier).
  • Why Na₂CO₃: neutralises leftover acid (2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂), making it water-soluble so it can be removed.
  • Why the funnel: the ester is non-polar and less dense than water, so it separates as the upper layer.

Evaluation: Yield is low because the reaction is reversible and some product is lost in transfers and washing. Impurities include unreacted alcohol. Distillation improves purity.

  2. Carbonate: add 1 mL of dilute HCl to 2 mL of sample. Fizzing that turns limewater milky shows CO₃²⁻.
  3. Sulfate: add 1 mL of 0.1 mol L⁻¹ Ba(NO₃)₂, then a few drops of dilute HNO₃. A white precipitate that persists is BaSO₄.
  4. Halides: acidify 2 mL of sample with 2–3 drops of dilute HNO₃ (to remove CO₃²⁻/OH⁻), then add 1 mL of 0.1 mol L⁻¹ AgNO₃. White = Cl⁻, cream = Br⁻, yellow = I⁻. Confirm with dilute ammonia: AgCl dissolves readily.
  5. Phosphate: add AgNO₃ to a fresh sample: yellow Ag₃PO₄ that dissolves in dilute HNO₃ (or use barium nitrate and test solubility in acid).
  6. Acetate: no precipitate with the above reagents. Teacher confirms by warming with ethanol and a drop of concentrated H₂SO₄ in a fume cupboard for a fruity ester smell.
  7. Obtain an unknown and follow the sequence, using confirmation tests.

Diagram: flow chart of the test sequence plus a rack of tubes showing reagent volumes.

Risk assessment

  • Concentrated acids and ammonia are dangerous. Use them in a fume cupboard, to avoid inhaling fumes and burns.
  • Silver and barium salts are toxic. Wear PPE and dispose of waste as directed.
  • Glass can break. Handle with care.

Evaluation: Results can be ambiguous when similar precipitates form (e.g. Ag₂CO₃ white, AgCl white), which is why you acidify first and use confirmation tests. Carbonate and hydroxide solutions are both basic, so test with acid as well.

Experiment 26 – Gravimetric analysis (sulfate in lawn fertiliser)

Syllabus: Conduct investigations and/or process data involving gravimetric analysis.

Aim: To determine the percentage of sulfate in a lawn fertiliser.

Hypothesis: The percentage of sulfate will be close to the value stated on the packet.

Variables: Independent: mass of fertiliser (about 1.00 g). Dependent: mass of BaSO₄. Controlled: temperature, amount of BaCl₂ added until precipitation is complete, drying to constant mass.

Method

  1. Weigh about 1.00 g of fertiliser accurately into a 250 mL beaker. Add about 50 mL of deionised water and 1 mL of dilute HCl, and stir to dissolve.
  2. Heat to near boiling. Add 0.1 mol L⁻¹ BaCl₂ dropwise until no more white precipitate forms.
  3. Keep the mixture gently heated (not boiling) for about an hour so crystals grow and filter easily.
  4. Weigh a filter paper beforehand. Filter through it using a funnel, rinsing the beaker into the funnel. Wash the precipitate with hot deionised water.
  5. Dry the filter paper and precipitate in an oven at about 100°C. Cool in a desiccator and weigh. Repeat the drying until the mass is constant.

Diagram: beaker on a hotplate with fertiliser solution and BaCl₂ added dropwise from a burette; filter funnel and flask; oven.

Risk assessment

  • Barium salts are toxic. Wear gloves, goggles and wash hands, and dispose of the precipitate in the heavy-metal waste.
  • HCl is corrosive. Wear goggles.
  • Hot liquids and oven burn. Use tongs and a heatproof mat.

Calculation: Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄. Mass SO₄²⁻ = mass BaSO₄ × (96.06 ÷ 233.4). % sulfate = mass of SO₄²⁻ ÷ mass of fertiliser × 100.

Evaluation: Sources of error include solubility of BaSO₄ (low result), co-precipitation of impurities (high result), fine particles passing through the filter, loss of precipitate in transfer and incomplete drying. Heating the precipitate slowly (digestion) and washing improve purity.

Experiment 27 – Precipitation titration (Volhard's method for iodide)

Syllabus: Conduct investigations and/or process data involving precipitation titrations.

Aim: To determine the concentration of iodide ions in a solution.

Hypothesis: A back titration with thiocyanate will give an accurate iodide concentration.

Variables: Independent: volume of NaSCN added. Dependent: end-point volume (permanent red). Controlled: 10.00 mL sample, 10.00 mL AgNO₃, indicator and acid volumes.

Method

  1. Pipette 10.00 mL of the 0.1 mol L⁻¹ NaI sample into a 250 mL conical flask.
  2. Pipette 10.00 mL of 0.1 mol L⁻¹ AgNO₃ (excess) into the flask. Add 5 mL of 1.0 mol L⁻¹ HNO₃ and 1 mL of saturated ferric ammonium sulfate. Prepare three flasks.
  3. Fill a burette with 0.1 mol L⁻¹ NaSCN and record the initial reading.
  4. Titrate the excess Ag⁺ until a permanent blood-red colour (FeSCN²⁺) appears. Record the final reading.

Diagram: burette of NaSCN over a conical flask containing AgI precipitate, Ag⁺ excess and indicator.

Risk assessment

  • Nitric acid is corrosive. Wear goggles and gloves, because it burns.
  • Silver salts are poisonous and stain. Avoid contact and dispose of waste as directed.
  • Glassware can break. Keep it away from the bench edge.

Calculation: Ag⁺ + I⁻ → AgI(s); Ag⁺ + SCN⁻ → AgSCN(s). n(I⁻) = n(Ag⁺ added) − n(SCN⁻ titrated). The AgI precipitate doesn't need filtering because AgI is less soluble than AgSCN.

Evaluation: Titrate to a persistent colour. A blank or standardised thiocyanate improves accuracy. The silver nitrate is sensitive to light, so store it in the dark.

Experiment 28 – Colourimetry and calibration curve (copper(II) sulfate)

Syllabus: Conduct investigations to determine the concentration of coloured species and/or metal ions in aqueous solution using colourimetry.

Aim: To determine the concentration of an unknown copper(II) sulfate solution using a calibration curve.

Hypothesis: Absorbance will increase linearly with concentration, so the unknown can be determined from a calibration curve.

Variables: Independent: concentration of CuSO₄. Dependent: absorbance. Controlled: wavelength, same cuvette and orientation, volume (10 mL), temperature.

Method

  1. Collect about 35 mL of 1.0 mol L⁻¹ CuSO₄ in a 50 mL beaker.
  2. Fill a clear container or cuvette with 10 mL and measure the absorbance with the colourimeter or light meter. Zero it with distilled water first.
  3. Measure 8 mL of 1.0 mol L⁻¹ CuSO₄ into a 10 mL measuring cylinder, make up to 10 mL with distilled water (0.8 mol L⁻¹), and measure the absorbance.
  4. Repeat for 6, 4 and 2 mL diluted to 10 mL (0.6, 0.4 and 0.2 mol L⁻¹).
  5. Measure the absorbance of 10 mL of the unknown.
  6. Plot absorbance (y) against concentration (x), draw a line of best fit, and read the concentration of the unknown.

Diagram: colourimeter with cuvette and light path, with the dilution series volumes shown.

Risk assessment

  • Copper(II) sulfate is harmful if swallowed and irritates the eyes. Wear goggles and wash hands.
  • Electrical equipment near liquids is a shock risk. Keep the colourimeter dry and wipe cuvettes before inserting them.

Results and analysis: Beer–Lambert: A = εlc, so A ∝ c (a straight line through the origin). Read the unknown from the graph (interpolation). If the line doesn't pass through the origin, suspect a zero error or a dirty cuvette.

Evaluation: Use dilutions made with pipettes or volumetric flasks (a measuring cylinder is imprecise). Keep the unknown within the calibration range. Avoid extrapolating outside the measured points.

Experiment 29 – Tests for functional groups (C=C, hydroxyl, carboxylic acid)

Syllabus: Conduct qualitative investigations to test for the presence in organic molecules of carbon–carbon double bonds, hydroxyl groups and carboxylic acids.

Aim: To distinguish an alkene, an alcohol and a carboxylic acid, and identify an unknown.

Hypothesis: Bromine water will test for C=C, acidified dichromate for hydroxyl groups, and sodium carbonate for carboxylic acids.

Variables: Independent: the sample (known compounds and unknown). Dependent: colour change or gas. Controlled: 2 mL of sample, reagent volumes, water-bath temperature.

Method

  1. Test known samples first: an alkene (cyclohexene), a carboxylic acid (ethanoic acid), and primary, secondary and tertiary alcohols (1-butanol, 2-butanol, 2-methyl-2-propanol).
  2. Alkene: add 1 mL of bromine water to 1 mL of sample and shake. Decolourisation means C=C.
  3. Carboxylic acid: add 2 mL of 0.1 mol L⁻¹ Na₂CO₃ to 2 mL of sample. Fizzing (CO₂) means –COOH.
  4. Hydroxyl: warm 2 mL of sample with 1 mL of acidified dichromate in a hot-water bath (about 70°C). Orange → green means a primary or secondary alcohol; no change suggests a tertiary alcohol (or no –OH).
  5. Apply these tests to the unknown and use a flow chart to justify its class.

Diagram: a flow chart showing tests and results, and a test-tube rack with the 1–2 mL volumes.

Risk assessment

  • Bromine water and dichromate are toxic and corrosive. Use a fume cupboard, gloves and goggles, to avoid skin contact and inhalation.
  • Organic liquids are flammable. Keep them away from flames and use a hot-water bath, not a Bunsen.
  • Acids and carbonate may splash. Wear goggles.

Results and analysis: Alkene + Br₂ → dibromoalkane (orange to colourless). RCOOH + Na₂CO₃ → RCOONa + H₂O + CO₂. Primary and secondary alcohols are oxidised; tertiary are not. Alcohols that give an acid product can also fizz with carbonate.

Evaluation: Test known compounds first to confirm the tests work. Alkane and alkene reactions with bromine can look alike if the alkane reacts slowly. Dichromate also reacts with other reducing groups.
